The bound states of an electron in a Coulomb field are fully worked out at level B: the series solution, the Bohr formula and $\ell \le n - 1$ ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-2|Theorem §B5.4.2]]), the degeneracy $n^2$ ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-3|Theorem §B5.4.3]]), the radial functions ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-4|Theorem §B5.4.4]]), Kramers' relation and the moments $\langle r^s\rangle$ ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-5|Theorem §B5.4.5]], [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-6|Theorem §B5.4.6]]); that the bound states alone are not complete is [[§27 Bound States Need Not Be Complete꞉ Hydrogen#^thm-27-1|556 Thm. §27.1]]. Level C changes the units to Gaussian, as Sakurai and the user's notes do (this note is the home of that convention), reaches the spectrum from the general bound-state equation of [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-2|Theorem §C6.1.2]] through Kummer's equation, expresses every scale through the fine-structure constant, and derives the normalization of the radial functions that level B quoted. It also says why the $\ell$-degeneracy is no accident, and where that is proved.

*Notation.* Gaussian units: the Coulomb energy of charges $q_1$, $q_2$ is $q_1q_2/r$, the electron charge is $-e$ with $e = 4.803\times10^{-10}$ esu, and $\alpha = e^2/\hbar c$. $m$ is the electron mass (the reduced mass for a nucleus of finite mass); $n, \ell, m$ in kets and wave functions are the quantum numbers. The Bohr radius is $a_0$ (level B writes $a$). Associated Laguerre polynomials $L^p_q$ are normalized as at level B ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^def-b5-4-1|Def. §B5.4.1]]). $\hbar$ is explicit.

## Units

> [!caution] Caution: Gaussian and SI units for the Coulomb problem
> Sakurai, the user's 511 notes and the user's hydrogen derivation work in Gaussian units, where Coulomb's law has no constant and the hydrogen potential is $-e^2/r$ ($-Ze^2/r$ for nuclear charge $Ze$). Level B (Griffiths) uses SI, where it is $-e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0r$ ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^mod-b5-4-1|Model §B5.4.1]]; Coulomb's law: [[§B2.1 The Electric Field, Its Divergence and Curl#^law-b2-1-1|EM Law §B2.1.1]]). The two systems assign different dimensions to charge, so the conversion is a substitution, not a factor: every Gaussian formula becomes the SI one under
>
> $$
> e^2 \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \qquad (q \to q/\sqrt{4\pi\epsilon_0}\ \text{for each charge}) .
> $$
>
> So $a_0 = \hbar^2/me^2$ becomes $4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2/me^2$, $E_n = -e^2/2a_0n^2$ becomes $-e^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0a_0n^2)$, and $\alpha = e^2/\hbar c$ becomes $e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c$. The numbers do not change: $a_0 = 0.529$ Å, $13.6$ eV, $\alpha = 1/137.036$ in both systems. Level A writes $ke^2$ for the same combination, and the user's series writes $e^2 \equiv q_e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0$ as a shorthand, which makes its SI formulas look Gaussian. Magnetic quantities convert differently (the Gaussian Lorentz force is $q(\mathbf E + \mathbf v\times\mathbf B/c)$ and a Gaussian magnetic moment is $IA/c$), which matters for the Zeeman Hamiltonian ([[§C7.2 Tensor Operators and the Wigner–Eckart Theorem]]); the electrostatic formulas of this chapter need only the substitution above.
>
> *Source: Sakurai App. A, eqs. (A.9)–(A.21) and the rule following (A.18) · the user's series, Part IV, §14*

## The spectrum through Kummer's equation

> [!model] Model §C6.2.1: Hydrogen-Like Atom
> An electron of mass $m$ and charge $-e$ moves in the field of a fixed point nucleus of charge $Ze$:
>
> $$
> H = \frac{\mathbf p^2}{2m} + V(r), \qquad V(r) = -\frac{Ze^2}{r} . \qquad (3.305)
> $$
>
> $Z = 1$ is hydrogen, $Z = 2$ is He$^+$, and so on. *Assumptions:* nucleus infinitely heavy (for finite nuclear mass replace $m$ by the reduced mass, [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^rem-b5-4-7|★ Remark: Reduced mass]]); nucleus pointlike; electron non-relativistic; spin and magnetic interactions ignored (fine structure: [[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]]). $H$ is rotation invariant, so its eigenkets are $|n\,\ell\,m\rangle$ and the radial equation of [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials]] applies ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-1|Theorem §C6.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.7.4, eq. (3.305), and App. B.6, eq. (B.54) · the user's 511 notes, §"The Coulomb Potential"*

^mod-c6-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.2.2: Bound States of the Coulomb Problem
> For $E < 0$ define $\kappa = \sqrt{-2mE}/\hbar$, $\rho = \kappa r$ and
>
> $$
> \rho_0 = \Bigl[\frac{2m}{-E}\Bigr]^{1/2}\frac{Ze^2}{\hbar} = \Bigl[\frac{2mc^2}{-E}\Bigr]^{1/2}Z\alpha . \qquad (3.306)
> $$
>
> In $u = \rho^{\ell+1}e^{-\rho}w(\rho)$ the function $F(x) = w(\rho)$, $x = 2\rho$, satisfies **Kummer's equation**
>
> $$
> x\frac{d^2F}{dx^2} + (c - x)\frac{dF}{dx} - aF = 0, \qquad c = 2(\ell + 1), \qquad a = \ell + 1 - \frac{\rho_0}2 , \qquad (3.308),\ (3.309)
> $$
>
> whose solution regular at the origin is the **confluent hypergeometric function**
>
> $$
> F(a; c; x) = 1 + \frac ac\frac{x}{1!} + \frac{a(a+1)}{c(c+1)}\frac{x^2}{2!} + \cdots, \qquad w(\rho) = F\Bigl(\ell + 1 - \frac{\rho_0}2;\ 2(\ell+1);\ 2\rho\Bigr) . \qquad (3.310),\ (3.311)
> $$
>
> $u$ is normalizable exactly when the series terminates, $a = -n_r$ with $n_r = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, i.e. $\rho_0 = 2n$ with the principal quantum number $n = n_r + \ell + 1 = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ and $\ell = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$ (3.312)–(3.314). Then
>
> $$
> E_n = -\frac12mc^2\,\frac{Z^2\alpha^2}{n^2} = -\frac{Z^2e^2}{2a_0}\,\frac1{n^2}, \qquad \frac1\kappa = \frac{\hbar}{mc\alpha}\,\frac nZ = a_0\,\frac nZ, \qquad a_0 = \frac{\hbar}{mc\alpha} = \frac{\hbar^2}{me^2} , \qquad (3.315)\text{–}(3.317)
> $$
>
> each level is $n^2$-fold degenerate (3.318), and
>
> $$
> R_{n\ell}(r) = \frac{1}{(2\ell+1)!}\Bigl(\frac{2Zr}{na_0}\Bigr)^\ell e^{-Zr/na_0}\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{2Z}{na_0}\Bigr)^3\frac{(n+\ell)!}{2n\,(n-\ell-1)!}\Bigr]^{1/2}F\Bigl(-n + \ell + 1;\ 2\ell + 2;\ \frac{2Zr}{na_0}\Bigr) . \qquad (3.320)
> $$
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.7.4, eqs. (3.305)–(3.320), and App. B.6, eqs. (B.55)–(B.56) · the user's 511 notes, §"The Coulomb Potential" · the user's hydrogen derivation, §5*

^thm-c6-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Kummer's equation.* The Coulomb potential satisfies the hypotheses of [[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-2|Theorem §C6.1.2]]. There $V/E = -Ze^2/(rE) = \frac{Ze^2\kappa}{-E}\cdot\frac1\rho = \frac{\rho_0}\rho$, with $\rho_0 = Ze^2\kappa/(-E) = \frac{Ze^2}{\hbar}\frac{\sqrt{2m(-E)}}{-E}$, which is (3.306); with $e^2/\hbar = \alpha c$ the second form follows. Multiplying (3.279) by $\rho$,
>
> $$
> \rho w'' + 2(\ell + 1 - \rho)w' + \bigl[\rho_0 - 2(\ell+1)\bigr]w = 0 , \qquad (3.307)
> $$
>
> the equation (4.61) of level B. With $x = 2\rho$, $w' = 2F'$ and $w'' = 4F''$; inserting and dividing by 2 gives (3.308) with $c$, $a$ as in (3.309).
>
> *The series.* Put $F = \sum_kb_kx^k$. The coefficient of $x^k$ in (3.308) is $b_{k+1}(k+1)(k+c) - b_k(k+a)$, so $b_{k+1}/b_k = (a+k)/[(c+k)(k+1)]$, which with $b_0 = 1$ is (3.310). The second solution of Kummer's equation behaves as $x^{1-c} = x^{-2\ell-1}$ at the origin, so that $u \sim \rho^{-\ell}$, which [[§B5.3 Central Potentials and the Radial Equation#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]] excludes. In terms of $\rho$ the coefficients of $w$ are $c_k = 2^kb_k$ with
>
> $$
> \frac{c_{k+1}}{c_k} = \frac{2(k + \ell + 1) - \rho_0}{(k+1)(k + 2\ell + 2)} ,
> $$
>
> exactly level B's recursion (4.63). So the rest of the argument is the same as there ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-2|Theorem §B5.4.2]], Steps 4–5): a series that does not stop grows like $e^{(2 - \varepsilon)\rho}$, so $u$ grows like $e^{(1-\varepsilon)\rho}$ and is not normalizable; a terminating one ($a + n_r = 0$, a polynomial of degree $n_r$) gives a normalizable state. Hence $\rho_0 = 2(n_r + \ell + 1) = 2n$.
>
> *Energies and lengths.* $\bigl[2mc^2/(-E)\bigr]^{1/2}Z\alpha = 2n$ gives $-E = \frac12mc^2Z^2\alpha^2/n^2$. Then $\kappa = \sqrt{-2mE}/\hbar = mcZ\alpha/n\hbar$, so $1/\kappa = (\hbar/mc\alpha)(n/Z)$, and $\hbar/mc\alpha = \hbar^2/me^2 = a_0$; also $\frac12mc^2\alpha^2 = \frac{me^4}{2\hbar^2} = \frac{e^2}{2a_0}$. The degeneracy is $\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-1}(2\ell+1) = n^2$, by Theorem §C6.1.1 for each $\ell$ and the coincidence of all $\ell \le n-1$ at $E_n$ ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-3|Theorem §B5.4.3]]). The constant in (3.320) is derived in Theorem §C6.2.3.

*Quantization by termination, for $\ell = 0$ (computed from $u = \rho\,e^{-\rho}F(1 - \nu; 2; 2\rho)$, $\rho = r/\nu a_0$, at trial energies $E = -13.6\ \mathrm{eV}/\nu^2$, normalized to unit slope at the origin). At $\nu = 1$ the series is $F = 1$ and $u = r\,e^{-r/a_0}$ decays. With $\nu$ five percent off in either direction the series does not terminate, and the regular solution runs off to $+\infty$ (energy below $E_1$) or $-\infty$ (above it): no normalizable state.*

> [!remark] Remark: What level C adds to the Coulomb spectrum
> The argument is level B's series argument; what changes is the frame. Theorem §C6.1.2 removes the two ends for every potential, and for $V \propto 1/r$ the remainder is a named equation whose regular solution is known in closed form for all $E$, not only at the eigenvalues; that is what makes the trial solutions of the figure computable. The answer is organized by one dimensionless number: $\alpha$ fixes the size $a_0 = \hbar/mc\alpha$ (the electron's Compton wavelength $\hbar/mc$ divided by $\alpha$) and the binding $\frac12mc^2\alpha^2$, and by the virial theorem ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-6|Theorem §B5.4.6]]) $\langle T\rangle = -E_n$, so $v_{\text{rms}} = c\,Z\alpha/n$. Hydrogen is non-relativistic because $\alpha \approx 1/137$ is small, and the relativistic corrections are smaller by $(Z\alpha)^2$ ([[§B8.3 The Fine Structure of Hydrogen#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]]). Sakurai also stresses the contrast with the Bohr model ([[§A2.2 The Bohr Model of Hydrogen]]): there the ground state had one unit of angular momentum, here it has $\ell = 0$, and several $\ell$ share each level above it.

> [!caution] Caution: The divergence of the series is $e^{2\rho}$, not $e^\rho$
> Sakurai's estimate after (3.311) replaces the ratio of the products $a(a+1)\cdots$ and $c(c+1)\cdots$ by $(N/2)^N/N^N$ and concludes $w \approx e^\rho$. For large $N$ that ratio tends to a power of $N$, not to $2^{-N}$: the term ratio of the series in $x = 2\rho$ is $(a + N)/[(c + N)(N + 1)] \to 1/N$, so $F(a; c; 2\rho)$ grows like $e^{2\rho}$ times a power and $u = \rho^{\ell+1}e^{-\rho}w$ like $e^{+\rho}$ — the rejected solution of $u'' = \kappa^2u$, as level B and the user's hydrogen derivation state ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-2|Theorem §B5.4.2]], Step 4). The conclusion, that the series must terminate, is unaffected.

## Normalization of the radial functions

> [!theorem] Theorem §C6.2.3: Generating Function and Normalization Integral of the Laguerre Polynomials
> With the associated Laguerre polynomials $L^p_q$ of [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^def-b5-4-1|Def. §B5.4.1]],
>
> $$
> \sum_{q=0}^\infty L^p_q(x)\,t^q = \frac{e^{-xt/(1-t)}}{(1-t)^{p+1}} \quad (\lvert t\rvert < 1), \qquad \int_0^\infty x^{p+1}e^{-x}\bigl[L^p_q(x)\bigr]^2dx = \frac{(2q + p + 1)\,(p+q)!}{q!} ,
> $$
>
> and $L^p_q(x) = \dbinom{p+q}{q}F(-q; p+1; x)$. Consequently the normalized radial functions of hydrogen-like atoms are
>
> $$
> R_{n\ell}(r) = \Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{2Z}{na_0}\Bigr)^3\frac{(n-\ell-1)!}{2n\,(n+\ell)!}\Bigr]^{1/2}\Bigl(\frac{2Zr}{na_0}\Bigr)^\ell e^{-Zr/na_0}\,L^{2\ell+1}_{n-\ell-1}\Bigl(\frac{2Zr}{na_0}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> which is level B's (4.89) for $Z = 1$ and Sakurai's (3.320).
>
> *Source: Sakurai Problem 3.30 (method; solved here in level B's normalization) · PHY 511, HW 10 (Problem 3.30 recommended reading) · Sakurai App. B.6, eqs. (B.56)–(B.57)*

^thm-c6-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Generating function.* The Laguerre polynomials $L_n$ of Def. §B5.4.1 ($L_n(0) = 1$) are the coefficients of $G(x, t) = e^{-xt/(1-t)}/(1-t) = \sum_nL_n(x)t^n$, the generating function of Sakurai's Problem 3.30(a) with his normalization $p!\,L_p$ divided out. (Expanding: the coefficient of $t$ is $1 - x = L_1$, that of $t^2$ is $1 - 2x + \frac12x^2 = L_2$.) Differentiating $p$ times in $x$, $\partial_x^pG = \bigl(-\frac{t}{1-t}\bigr)^pG$, and the polynomials of degree below $p$ drop out:
>
> $$
> \sum_q\Bigl(\frac{d}{dx}\Bigr)^pL_{p+q}\;t^{p+q} = (-1)^p\frac{t^p}{(1-t)^p}\,G .
> $$
>
> With $L^p_q = (-1)^p(d/dx)^pL_{p+q}$ and division by $t^p$ this is the stated generating function. At $x = 0$ it is $(1-t)^{-p-1} = \sum_q\binom{p+q}{q}t^q$, so $L^p_q(0) = \binom{p+q}q$.
>
> *Normalization integral.* Multiply two generating functions and integrate:
>
> $$
> \int_0^\infty x^{p+1}e^{-x}\,\frac{e^{-xt/(1-t)}e^{-xs/(1-s)}}{[(1-t)(1-s)]^{p+1}}\,dx = \frac{(p+1)!}{\lambda^{p+2}\,[(1-t)(1-s)]^{p+1}}, \qquad \lambda = 1 + \frac{t}{1-t} + \frac{s}{1-s} = \frac{1 - ts}{(1-t)(1-s)} ,
> $$
>
> using $\int_0^\infty x^ke^{-\lambda x}dx = k!/\lambda^{k+1}$. The right side is $(p+1)!\,(1-t)(1-s)\,(1 - ts)^{-(p+2)}$, and $(1 - ts)^{-(p+2)} = \sum_k\binom{k+p+1}{k}(ts)^k$. The coefficient of $t^qs^q$ on the left is $\int x^{p+1}e^{-x}[L^p_q]^2dx$ (the terms $t^qs^{q'}$ with $q' \ne q$ give the cross integrals). On the right, $t^qs^q$ arises from the $1$ and from the $ts$ in $(1-t)(1-s) = 1 - t - s + ts$ (the terms $-t$, $-s$ make the powers unequal):
>
> $$
> (p+1)!\Bigl[\binom{q+p+1}{q} + \binom{q+p}{q-1}\Bigr] = \frac{(p+q+1)!}{q!} + \frac{(p+q)!}{(q-1)!} = \frac{(p+q)!}{q!}\,(2q + p + 1) .
> $$
>
> *Relation to $F$.* $L^p_q$ solves $xy'' + (p + 1 - x)y' + qy = 0$ (Def. §B5.4.1), Kummer's equation with $c = p+1$, $a = -q$. Its solutions regular at $0$ are multiples of $F(-q; p+1; x)$ (the recursion of the series fixes every coefficient from the constant term), and comparing values at $0$ gives the factor $\binom{p+q}q$.
>
> *The radial functions.* By Theorem §C6.2.2, $R_{n\ell} = N_{n\ell}\,x^\ell e^{-x/2}L^{2\ell+1}_{n-\ell-1}(x)$ with $x = 2Zr/na_0$. Since $r = \frac{na_0}{2Z}x$,
>
> $$
> 1 = \int_0^\infty R_{n\ell}^2r^2dr = N_{n\ell}^2\Bigl(\frac{na_0}{2Z}\Bigr)^3\int_0^\infty x^{2\ell+2}e^{-x}\bigl[L^{2\ell+1}_{n-\ell-1}\bigr]^2dx = N_{n\ell}^2\Bigl(\frac{na_0}{2Z}\Bigr)^3\frac{2n\,(n+\ell)!}{(n-\ell-1)!} ,
> $$
>
> by the integral with $p = 2\ell + 1$, $q = n - \ell - 1$, $2q + p + 1 = 2n$. This is the constant stated. With $L^{2\ell+1}_{n-\ell-1} = \frac{(n+\ell)!}{(2\ell+1)!\,(n-\ell-1)!}F(-n+\ell+1; 2\ell+2; x)$ it becomes Sakurai's (3.320).

> [!caution] Caution: Three Laguerre conventions
> Level B (Griffiths 3rd ed.) and this note use $L_q(0) = 1$. Sakurai's Problem 3.30 and (B.57) use polynomials larger by a factorial: his $L_p$ is $p!$ times level B's, and his $L^q_p = (-1)^q\frac{d^q}{dx^q}L_{p+q}$ is $(p+q)!$ times level B's $L^q_p$; that is why (B.57) has $[(n+\ell)!]^3$ under the root where (4.89) has $(n+\ell)!$. Both give the same $R_{n\ell}$, with $R_{n\ell}(0) > 0$ for $\ell = 0$. In Sakurai's Problem 3.30(b) the generating function $h(x,t)$ and the value $L^q_p(0) = (-1)^q[(p+q)!]^2/p!q!$ carry a factor $(-1)^q$ that does not follow from the definition printed with them (differentiating $g$ as above gives no sign); with that factor (B.57) would make $R_{n0}(0)$ negative, contrary to (B.58). Older books also differ in the order of the indices. Before combining a formula with a table, check $L^p_q(0)$.

## Why the degeneracy is $n^2$

> [!remark] Remark: A symmetry, not an accident
> Rotational invariance explains the $2\ell + 1$ states of each $\ell$ (Theorem §C6.1.1) but not why $\ell = 0, \dots, n-1$ share one energy; the spherical well shows that a generic central potential separates them ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^thm-c6-1-3|Theorem §C6.1.3]] and the zeros listed after it). Sakurai: "This degeneracy is in fact not accidental, but reflects a subtle symmetry of the Coulomb potential." Classically the same specialness shows in the closed Kepler ellipses, whose axis is fixed by the conserved Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-5|CM Theorem §B2.2.5]], [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^rem-b2-2-4|CM Remark: Why Kepler orbits close]]); level B pointed to it in [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^rem-b5-4-2|★ Remark: Where the accidental degeneracy comes from]]. The quantum version is the Hermitian operator
>
> $$
> \mathbf M = \frac{1}{2m}\bigl(\mathbf p\times\mathbf L - \mathbf L\times\mathbf p\bigr) - \frac{Ze^2}{r}\,\mathbf x ,
> $$
>
> which commutes with $H$ (Sakurai Problem 3.31) but not with $\mathbf L^2$, and so, like $Q_{zz}$ for the oscillator ([[§C6.1 The Radial Equation and Elementary Central Potentials#^rem-c6-1-5|★ Remark: Conserved operators beyond the angular momentum]]), links states of different $\ell$ at one energy. Within a level $E_n$, $\mathbf L$ and the rescaled $\mathbf M$ generate rotations in four dimensions, $SO(4)$; they combine into two commuting angular momenta of equal size $k$, with $n = 2k + 1$ and degeneracy $(2k+1)^2 = n^2$. This is Pauli's algebraic solution of hydrogen (Sakurai §4.1.4, eqs. (4.20)–(4.37)), which is stated here and proved in Chapter C8 (planned). The user's series (§14) and the user's hydrogen derivation (§7, "a hidden SO(4) symmetry") make the same point.

^rem-c6-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Where each quantum number comes from
> The user's hydrogen derivation ends with a summary that the ket-space view sharpens. (1) $m$ is an integer: in the derivation from single-valuedness of $e^{im\phi}$; at level C, the algebra allows half-integers and orbital angular momentum $\mathbf x\times\mathbf p$ realizes only the integers ([[§C5.4 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics as Rotation Matrices#^thm-c5-4-3|Theorem §C5.4.3]]). (2) $\ell$ is a non-negative integer with $\lvert m\rvert \le \ell$: from regularity of the Legendre equation at $\theta = 0, \pi$, or from the ladder ([[§C5.3 The Angular-Momentum Spectrum and Rotation Matrices#^rem-c5-3-1|Remark: Eigenvalues and ladder matrix elements]]). (3) $n$: from normalizability, which forces the series to terminate (Theorem §C6.2.2), with $n = n_r + \ell + 1$ and $n_r$ the number of radial nodes. Only (3) depends on the potential; (1)–(2) hold for every central potential (Theorem §C6.1.1).
>
> *Source: the user's hydrogen derivation, §7*

^rem-c6-2-4

> [!caution] Caution: Two slips in the user's hydrogen derivation
> - §5.2 defines $\rho_0 = me^2/\hbar^2\kappa$ next to the equation $u'' = \bigl[1 - \rho_0/\rho + \ell(\ell+1)/\rho^2\bigr]u$. Dividing the radial equation by $\kappa^2$ gives the coefficient $2me^2/\hbar^2\kappa$, i.e. $\rho_0 = 2me^2/\hbar^2\kappa$ (Sakurai's (3.306) with $Z = 1$). Correspondingly §5.7's $\kappa = me^2/2n\hbar^2$ should read $\kappa = me^2/n\hbar^2 = 1/na_0$, which §5.8 then uses correctly. The recursion, $\rho_0 = 2n$, $E_n = -me^4/2\hbar^2n^2$ and $a_0 = \hbar^2/me^2$ are right.
> - §4.3 writes $Y^m_\ell = N_{\ell m}P^{\lvert m\rvert}_\ell(\cos\theta)e^{im\phi}$ with a positive $N_{\ell m}$, while its table lists $Y^{\pm1}_1 = \mp\sqrt{3/8\pi}\sin\theta\,e^{\pm i\phi}$. One formula without a sign factor cannot give both signs; the table is the Condon–Shortley one ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^cau-b5-2-1|Caution: Phase conventions]]), which needs $Y^m_\ell = (-1)^mN_{\ell m}P^m_\ell e^{im\phi}$ for $m \ge 0$ (with $P^m_\ell$ defined without $(-1)^m$) and $Y^{-m}_\ell = (-1)^m(Y^m_\ell)^*$ (Sakurai (B.36)).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §C6.2.1: Atomic Scales from Three Constants
> Evaluate $a_0$, $E_1$ and $\alpha$ in Gaussian units, and find the ground-state energy, size and electron speed for Li$^{2+}$ ($Z = 3$).
>
> With $\hbar = 1.0546\times10^{-27}$ erg s, $m = 9.1094\times10^{-28}$ g, $e = 4.8032\times10^{-10}$ esu and $c = 2.9979\times10^{10}$ cm/s:
>
> $$
> a_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{me^2} = 5.292\times10^{-9}\ \text{cm}, \qquad \frac{e^2}{2a_0} = 2.180\times10^{-11}\ \text{erg} = 13.606\ \text{eV}, \qquad \frac1\alpha = \frac{\hbar c}{e^2} = 137.036 ,
> $$
>
> and $\frac12mc^2\alpha^2 = \frac12(511.0\ \text{keV})/137.036^2 = 13.606$ eV, the same number. In SI the substitution of [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^cau-c6-2-1|Caution: Gaussian and SI units]] gives identical values. For Li$^{2+}$, $E_1 = -Z^2(13.606\ \text{eV}) = -122.5$ eV, the size is $a_0/Z = 0.176$ Å, and $v_{\text{rms}}/c = Z\alpha = 0.022$: the inner electrons of light atoms are still non-relativistic, but for $Z \approx 80$, $Z\alpha \approx 0.6$, and the non-relativistic model fails for the innermost shell.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.7.4, eqs. (3.315)–(3.317), and App. A, (A.14)–(A.15); numbers computed here*

^ex-c6-2-1

> [!example] Example §C6.2.2: How Often Is the Electron inside the Proton?
> Treat the proton as a sphere of radius $R = 0.84$ fm. Find the probability that the electron is inside it, in the $1s$, $2s$ and $2p$ states of hydrogen.
>
> $R/a_0 = 0.84/52918 = 1.59\times10^{-5}$, so the wave function can be replaced by its leading behaviour $R_{n\ell} \propto r^\ell$ (3.276). Using the radial functions of [[§C6.2 The Coulomb Problem#^rem-c6-2-2|Remark: What this closes at level B]] with $x = R/a_0$:
>
> $$
> P_{1s} = \int_0^R\frac{4}{a_0^3}e^{-2r/a_0}r^2dr = 1 - e^{-2x}(1 + 2x + 2x^2) \approx \frac43x^3 = 5.3\times10^{-15},
> $$
>
> $$
> P_{2s} \approx \frac{1}{2a_0^3}\cdot\frac{R^3}{3} = \frac{x^3}6 = 6.7\times10^{-16}, \qquad P_{2p} \approx \int_0^R\frac{r^2}{24a_0^5}r^2dr = \frac{x^5}{120} = 8.4\times10^{-27} .
> $$
>
> The density near the origin goes as $r^{2\ell}$, so relative to an $s$ state a $p$ state is suppressed by a further $(R/a_0)^2 \sim 10^{-10}$ (Sakurai's "goes like $(R/a_0)^{2\ell}$"); the probability inside the radius $R$ scales as $(R/a_0)^{2\ell+3}$. This is why the finite size of the nucleus and the contact (hyperfine) interaction ([[§B8.4★ The Zeeman Effect and Hyperfine Splitting#^thm-b8-4-5|Theorem §B8.4.5]]) affect $s$ states almost exclusively.
>
> *Source: Sakurai §3.7.1, discussion after (3.276) (the scaling); numbers computed here*
