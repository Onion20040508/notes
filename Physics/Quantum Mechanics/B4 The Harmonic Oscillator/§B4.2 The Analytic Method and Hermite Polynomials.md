*Sources: Griffiths & Schroeter, Introduction to Quantum Mechanics (3rd ed.), §2.3.2, Problems 2.14–2.16 · Zwiebach, MIT 8.04 lecture notes, Lecture 13, §3 · Fleisch, A Student's Guide to the Schrödinger Equation, §5.3.*

Level A reduced the oscillator's Schrödinger equation to Hermite's equation and observed that polynomial solutions require $E = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$, but it asserted without proof that every other solution blows up (the step (∗) in the [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^der-a4-5-3|derivation of Theorem §A4.5.3]]). This section solves the equation by power series and proves that step: a series that does not terminate grows like $e^{\xi^2}$, so quantization is the condition that the series stop. The method gives the stationary states explicitly as Hermite functions, an independent check of the ladder-operator spectrum of [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]], and the Hermite polynomials' standard identities, which are then used to normalize the states and to prove that they form a complete set. The series method is the one that carries over to hydrogen ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom|§B5.4]]). The section ends with the classical limit of the stationary states.

## Hermite's equation and its series solution

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.1: Dimensionless Form; Hermite's Equation
> With the dimensionless position and energy
>
> $$
> \xi = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}}\;x, \qquad K = \frac{2E}{\hbar\omega},
> $$
>
> the time-independent Schrödinger equation of [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^mod-b4-1-1|Model §B4.1.1]] reads
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2\psi}{d\xi^2} = \bigl(\xi^2 - K\bigr)\,\psi ,
> $$
>
> and the substitution $\psi(\xi) = h(\xi)\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$, which loses no generality, turns it into **Hermite's equation**
>
> $$
> h'' - 2\xi h' + (K - 1)\,h = 0 .
> $$
>
> For large $|\xi|$ the solutions of the original equation behave like $e^{\pm\xi^2/2}$ (up to powers of $\xi$), and only the decaying branch is normalizable; the substitution peels it off.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.2, eqs. (2.71)–(2.79) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 13, eqs. (3.31)–(3.44) · Fleisch §5.3, eqs. (5.68)–(5.74)*

^thm-b4-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Level A did this with $u = x/a$, $\varepsilon = 2E/\hbar\omega$ ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^der-a4-5-3|derivation of Theorem §A4.5.3]]); $\xi$ and $K$ are the same variables in Griffiths' notation ($a = \sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$). In brief: $d/dx = \sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,d/d\xi$ turns $-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2\psi = E\psi$ into $-\tfrac12\hbar\omega\,\psi_{\xi\xi} + \tfrac12\hbar\omega\,\xi^2\psi = E\psi$; divide by $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$. With $\psi = he^{-\xi^2/2}$, $\psi_\xi = (h' - \xi h)e^{-\xi^2/2}$ and $\psi_{\xi\xi} = \bigl(h'' - 2\xi h' + (\xi^2 - 1)h\bigr)e^{-\xi^2/2}$; the $\xi^2h$ terms cancel against the right side. Any $\psi$ can be written as $(\psi e^{\xi^2/2})\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$, so nothing is assumed (Griffiths' footnote 32). For the asymptotics, $\psi'' \approx \xi^2\psi$ at large $|\xi|$ is solved approximately by $e^{\pm\xi^2/2}$, since $(e^{\pm\xi^2/2})'' = (\xi^2 \pm 1)\,e^{\pm\xi^2/2}$.

^der-b4-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^mod-b4-1-1|Model §B4.1.1]], [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^der-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.2: Series Solution and Recursion Formula
> Every solution of Hermite's equation is a power series $h(\xi) = \sum_{j \ge 0} a_j\xi^j$ converging for all $\xi$, whose coefficients obey the **recursion formula**
>
> $$
> a_{j+2} = \frac{2j + 1 - K}{(j + 1)(j + 2)}\;a_j .
> $$
>
> $a_0 = h(0)$ and $a_1 = h'(0)$ are free; $a_0$ generates an even series $h_{\text{even}} = a_0 + a_2\xi^2 + a_4\xi^4 + \cdots$ and $a_1$ an odd one $h_{\text{odd}} = a_1\xi + a_3\xi^3 + \cdots$, and $h = h_{\text{even}} + h_{\text{odd}}$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.2, eqs. (2.80)–(2.83) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 13, eqs. (3.49)–(3.54) · Fleisch §5.3, eq. (5.76)*

^thm-b4-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $h = \sum a_j\xi^j$ into Hermite's equation, differentiating term by term ([[§26 Differentiation and Integration of Power Series#^thm-26-4|451 Thm. §26.4]]); shifting the index in $h'' = \sum_j (j + 1)(j + 2)a_{j+2}\xi^j$ gives
>
> $$
> \sum_{j \ge 0}\Bigl[(j + 1)(j + 2)\,a_{j+2} - 2j\,a_j + (K - 1)\,a_j\Bigr]\xi^j = 0 .
> $$
>
> A power series vanishing on an interval has all coefficients zero (they are its derivatives at $0$ divided by $j!$, by the same theorem), which is the recursion. The series converges everywhere: $|a_{j+2}/a_j| \to 0$, so by the ratio test ([[§14 Series#^thm-14-9|451 Thm. §14.9]]) the radius of convergence ([[§23 Power Series#^thm-23-2|451 Thm. §23.2]]) is infinite, and term-by-term differentiation is justified. The pair $(a_0, a_1)$ can be chosen freely, and Hermite's equation, linear of second order, has exactly a two-parameter family of solutions fixed by $h(0)$ and $h'(0)$; so these series are all the solutions.

^der-b4-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]], [[§26 Differentiation and Integration of Power Series#^thm-26-4|451 Thm. §26.4]], [[§14 Series#^thm-14-9|451 Thm. §14.9]], [[§23 Power Series#^thm-23-2|451 Thm. §23.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.3: Quantization by Termination
> The function $\psi = h\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$ is normalizable if and only if $h$ is a polynomial. This happens if and only if
>
> $$
> K = 2n + 1 \quad\text{for some } n = 0, 1, 2, \dots, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad E_n = \Bigl(n + \frac12\Bigr)\hbar\omega ,
> $$
>
> and the series of the other parity is absent ($a_1 = 0$ for even $n$, $a_0 = 0$ for odd $n$). Then $h$ is a polynomial of degree exactly $n$ containing only powers of the parity of $n$, so $\psi_n$ is even or odd as $n$ is. A series that does not terminate makes $|\psi|$ grow at least like $e^{\xi^2/4}$ at large $|\xi|$ (in fact faster than $e^{c\xi^2}$ for every $c < \tfrac12$). This is the step (∗) left open at level A, and the spectrum agrees with the algebraic one ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.2, eqs. (2.84)–(2.85), footnotes 35–37 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 13, eqs. (3.55)–(3.64) · Fleisch §5.3, eqs. (5.77)–(5.78)*

^thm-b4-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Even and odd parts separate.* On $[-L, L]$ the cross term $\int\psi_{\text{even}}^*\psi_{\text{odd}}\,d\xi$ vanishes (odd integrand), so $\int_{-L}^L|\psi|^2 = \int_{-L}^L|h_{\text{even}}|^2e^{-\xi^2} + \int_{-L}^L|h_{\text{odd}}|^2e^{-\xi^2}$. Hence $\psi$ is normalizable iff each part is, and it suffices to treat one parity.
>
> *A polynomial gives a normalizable $\psi$*, since $\int|\xi|^ke^{-\xi^2}d\xi < \infty$ for every $k$ ([[§36 Improper Integrals#^thm-36-2|451 Thm. §36.2]]).
>
> *A non-terminating series does not.* Suppose the series of one parity never stops. For $j > (K - 1)/2$ the numerator $2j + 1 - K$ is positive, so beyond some index all its coefficients are nonzero and of one sign; take them positive (else replace $h$ by $-h$). Compare with $e^{\beta\xi^2} = \sum_k \beta^k\xi^{2k}/k!$ for even $j$, or $\xi e^{\beta\xi^2}$ for odd $j$, with $\beta = \tfrac34$. Their coefficients $c_j$ have $c_{j+2}/c_j = 2\beta/(j + 2)$ or $2\beta/(j + 1)$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{a_{j+2}/a_j}{c_{j+2}/c_j} \longrightarrow \frac1\beta = \frac43 > 1 \qquad (j \to \infty),
> $$
>
> because $a_{j+2}/a_j = (2j + 1 - K)/((j + 1)(j + 2)) \approx 2/j$ (Griffiths' heuristic $a_{j+2} \approx (2/j)\,a_j$, made exact). So beyond some $J$ the ratios of the $a_j$ exceed those of the $c_j$, and $a_j \ge C\,c_j$ for $j \ge J$ with $C = a_J/c_J > 0$. For $\xi > 0$ all terms past $J$ are positive, hence $h(\xi) \ge C\,e^{\frac34\xi^2} - P(\xi)$ (or $C\xi e^{\frac34\xi^2} - P(\xi)$) with $P$ a polynomial, and $|\psi| = |h|\,e^{-\xi^2/2} \ge C\,e^{\xi^2/4} - P(\xi)e^{-\xi^2/2} \to \infty$. Such a $\psi$ is not normalizable; this is the bad branch $e^{+\xi^2/2}$ of [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]] reappearing inside the series (Griffiths' footnote 35).
>
> *Termination.* The series of the parity of $j$ stops after $a_n \ne 0$ exactly when $a_{n+2} = 0$, i.e. $K = 2n + 1$. For that $K$ the numerator $2j + 1 - K$ vanishes only at $j = n$, so the series of the other parity never stops and must be absent from the start. Finally $K = 2n + 1$ is $E = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$ ([[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]]).

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]], [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^thm-36-2|451 Thm. §36.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Quantization as a property of the tail
> The differential equation has two independent solutions for every energy; what is quantized is the existence of one that decays at both ends. Integrate outward from $\xi = 0$ with an even start ($\psi(0) = 1$, $\psi'(0) = 0$) or an odd one ($\psi(0) = 0$, $\psi'(0) = 1$) at a trial $K$: on one side of an allowed value the tail swings up to $+\infty$, on the other side down to $-\infty$, and only at $K = 2n + 1$ does it settle onto the axis (the figure). Searching for the energy at which the tail flips sign is the **shooting method**, a practical way to find the levels of any one-dimensional potential (Griffiths calls it "wagging the dog"; Zwiebach, Lecture 12, §4, describes it in general).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.2, Fig. 2.6, footnote 36 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 12, §4*

## Hermite polynomials

> [!definition] Definition §B4.2.1: Hermite Polynomials
> The **Hermite polynomial** $H_n(\xi)$ is the polynomial solution of Hermite's equation with $K = 2n + 1$,
>
> $$
> H_n'' - 2\xi H_n' + 2n\,H_n = 0 ,
> $$
>
> normalized so that its leading coefficient is $2^n$. By [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]] it exists and is unique, has degree $n$ and parity $(-1)^n$. The first few:
>
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> H_0 = 1, \qquad H_1 = 2\xi, \qquad H_2 = 4\xi^2 - 2, \qquad H_3 = 8\xi^3 - 12\xi, \\
> H_4 = 16\xi^4 - 48\xi^2 + 12, \qquad H_5 = 32\xi^5 - 160\xi^3 + 120\xi .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.2, Table 2.1, eq. (2.85) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 13, eqs. (3.65)–(3.67)*

^def-b4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.4: Generating Function, Rodrigues Formula and Recursion Relations
> For all $\xi$ and $z$,
>
> $$
> e^{-z^2 + 2z\xi} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{z^n}{n!}\,H_n(\xi) \qquad\text{(generating function)},
> $$
>
> $$
> H_n(\xi) = (-1)^n\,e^{\xi^2}\,\frac{d^n}{d\xi^n}\,e^{-\xi^2} \qquad\text{(Rodrigues formula)},
> $$
>
> $$
> H_{n+1} = 2\xi H_n - 2nH_{n-1}, \qquad H_n' = 2nH_{n-1}, \qquad H_{n+1} = 2\xi H_n - H_n' .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.16, eqs. (2.87)–(2.90) (stated there without proof) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 13, eq. (3.68) (stated); proved here*

^thm-b4-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Generating function and Rodrigues.* $G(\xi, z) = e^{-z^2 + 2z\xi} = e^{\xi^2}e^{-(z - \xi)^2}$ is an entire function of $z$, equal to its Taylor series about $z = 0$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^def-31-1|451 Def. §31.1]]). Define $R_n(\xi) = \partial_z^nG|_{z=0}$, so that $G = \sum R_nz^n/n!$. With $f(u) = e^{-u^2}$, $\partial_z^n f(z - \xi) = f^{(n)}(z - \xi)$, and at $z = 0$ this is $f^{(n)}(-\xi) = (-1)^nf^{(n)}(\xi)$ because $f$ is even. So $R_n = (-1)^ne^{\xi^2}\,d^ne^{-\xi^2}/d\xi^n$: the Rodrigues expression.
>
> *Recursions.* $\partial_\xi G = 2z\,G$; comparing coefficients of $z^n/n!$ gives $R_n' = 2nR_{n-1}$. $\partial_zG = (2\xi - 2z)\,G$; comparing coefficients of $z^n/n!$ gives $R_{n+1} = 2\xi R_n - 2nR_{n-1}$. Combining, $R_{n+1} = 2\xi R_n - R_n'$.
>
> *$R_n = H_n$.* From $R_0 = 1$ and $R_{n+1} = 2\xi R_n - R_n'$, each $R_n$ is a polynomial of degree $n$ with leading coefficient $2^n$. Differentiate $R_{n+1} = 2\xi R_n - R_n'$ and use $R_{n+1}' = 2(n + 1)R_n$: $2(n + 1)R_n = 2R_n + 2\xi R_n' - R_n''$, that is $R_n'' - 2\xi R_n' + 2nR_n = 0$. So $R_n$ is a polynomial solution of Hermite's equation with $K = 2n + 1$ and leading coefficient $2^n$, which by [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^def-b4-2-1|Def. §B4.2.1]] is $H_n$.

^der-b4-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^def-b4-2-1|Def. §B4.2.1]], [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^def-31-1|451 Def. §31.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.5: Normalized Stationary States
> The normalized stationary states of the oscillator are the **Hermite functions**
>
> $$
> \psi_n(x) = \Bigl(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{1/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n\,n!}}\;H_n(\xi)\,e^{-\xi^2/2}, \qquad \xi = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}}\,x ,
> $$
>
> the same functions as $(\hat a_+)^n\psi_0/\sqrt{n!}$ of [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]], sign included. Equivalently, the Hermite polynomials are orthogonal with weight $e^{-\xi^2}$:
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^\infty H_m(\xi)\,H_n(\xi)\,e^{-\xi^2}\,d\xi = \sqrt\pi\;2^n\,n!\;\delta_{mn} .
> $$
>
> $\psi_n$ has exactly $n$ real nodes, as the node theorem requires ([[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.2, eq. (2.86), footnote 39 (normalization not worked out there) and eqs. (2.68)–(2.69); the normalization is derived here from the ladder*

^thm-b4-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the variable $\xi$, $\hat a_+ = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\xi - d/d\xi)$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]). For any polynomial $H$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\xi - \frac{d}{d\xi}\Bigr)\bigl[H(\xi)\,e^{-\xi^2/2}\bigr] = \bigl(\xi H - H' + \xi H\bigr)e^{-\xi^2/2} = \bigl(2\xi H - H'\bigr)e^{-\xi^2/2},
> $$
>
> and for $H = H_n$ this is $H_{n+1}e^{-\xi^2/2}$ by [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]]. Starting from $\psi_0 = (m\omega/\pi\hbar)^{1/4}H_0\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]), induction gives $(\hat a_+)^n\psi_0 = 2^{-n/2}(m\omega/\pi\hbar)^{1/4}H_n(\xi)\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$, and dividing by $\sqrt{n!}$ gives the formula; by [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]] these are normalized and orthonormal. With $dx = \sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}\,d\xi$, $\langle\psi_m|\psi_n\rangle = \delta_{mn}$ becomes $\int H_mH_ne^{-\xi^2}d\xi\,/\bigl(\sqrt\pi\,\sqrt{2^mm!\,2^nn!}\bigr) = \delta_{mn}$. The $n$ nodes: [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]] for the $(n + 1)$st state, or directly from the Rodrigues formula (each derivative of $e^{-\xi^2}$ adds one sign change, by Rolle's theorem).

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]], [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]], [[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]], [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]], [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-7|Theorem §B3.1.7]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.6: Completeness of the Oscillator States
> The stationary states $\psi_0, \psi_1, \psi_2, \dots$ form an orthonormal basis of $L^2(\mathbb R)$: every square-integrable $f$ satisfies
>
> $$
> f = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n\,\psi_n, \qquad c_n = \langle\psi_n|f\rangle, \qquad \sum_n|c_n|^2 = \|f\|^2 ,
> $$
>
> the sum converging in the mean. For the oscillator the completeness postulate ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]]) is therefore a theorem, and so is the expansion of every oscillator state in stationary states ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.1, footnote 28, and §2.3.1, p. 64 (expansion "as always" by Fourier's trick); the proof is worked out here*

^thm-b4-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-8|556 Thm. §20.8]] it suffices to show completeness in the sense of [[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^def-20-2|556 Def. §20.2]]: if $\langle\psi_n|f\rangle = 0$ for all $n$, then $f = 0$. Work in $\xi$. The span of $\psi_0, \dots, \psi_N$ equals the span of $\xi^ke^{-\xi^2/2}$, $k \le N$, since $H_n$ has degree $n$. So the hypothesis says
>
> $$
> \int \xi^k\,g(\xi)\,d\xi = 0 \quad (k = 0, 1, 2, \dots), \qquad g(\xi) = e^{-\xi^2/2}f(\xi) .
> $$
>
> $g$ is absolutely and square integrable, by the Schwarz inequality ([[§17 Cauchy–Schwarz and the Induced Norm#^thm-17-1|556 Thm. §17.1]]) with $e^{-\xi^2/2} \in L^2$. Its Fourier transform ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|WO Def. §B4.4.1]]) is
>
> $$
> \tilde g(k) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int g(\xi)\,e^{-ik\xi}\,d\xi = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=0}^\infty\frac{(-ik)^j}{j!}\int\xi^j g(\xi)\,d\xi = 0 \qquad\text{for every } k .
> $$
>
> Exchanging sum and integral is allowed by dominated convergence ([[§15 The General Lebesgue Integral#^thm-15-8|551 Thm. §15.8]]): the partial sums are bounded by $e^{|k\xi|}|g(\xi)| = e^{|k\xi| - \xi^2/2}|f(\xi)|$, which is integrable by the Schwarz inequality since $e^{|k\xi| - \xi^2/2}$ is square integrable. By the Plancherel identity ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|WO Theorem §B4.4.4]]), $\int|g|^2 = 2\pi\int|\tilde g|^2 = 0$, so $g = 0$ almost everywhere, and so is $f = e^{\xi^2/2}g$. The expansion and Parseval's equality are then [[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-8|556 Thm. §20.8]].

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]], [[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^def-20-2|556 Def. §20.2]], [[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-8|556 Thm. §20.8]], [[§17 Cauchy–Schwarz and the Induced Norm#^thm-17-1|556 Thm. §17.1]], [[§15 The General Lebesgue Integral#^thm-15-8|551 Thm. §15.8]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|WO Theorem §B4.4.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two methods, one answer
> The algebraic method ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum|§B4.1]]) is quicker and gives the energies, the ladder and all matrix elements without any wave function; it rests on the special structure of the oscillator Hamiltonian. The power-series method is brute force and gives the wave functions explicitly; Griffiths stresses that "the same strategy can be applied to many other potentials", and it returns, with an extra factor $\xi^\ell$ peeled off near the origin, in the radial equation of hydrogen ([[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-b5-4-2|Theorem §B5.4.2]]). The general theory of series solutions of linear differential equations (Frobenius' method) and the special functions it produces (Hermite, Legendre, Laguerre) belong to Mathematical Methods (planned). Problems that reduce to the oscillator on half the line are handled by parity, without a new calculation: [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^ex-b3-1-2|Example §B3.1.2]] (the half oscillator).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3, p. 58; §2.3.2, footnotes 32–34*

## The classical limit

> [!remark] Remark: How the stationary states approach the classical oscillator
> A stationary state never moves ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]]), so the comparison with a classical oscillator of the same energy, amplitude $A_n = \sqrt{(2n + 1)\hbar/m\omega}$, is with its time-averaged behaviour.
> - *Position distribution.* Level A observed that $|\psi_{12}|^2$, smoothed over its wiggles, follows the classical density $1/\pi\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$ ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^rem-a4-5-5|Remark: Correspondence at large n]]; Griffiths shows $n = 60$). The WKB approximation proves this for any potential well: the locally averaged $|\psi|^2$ of a highly excited state is the classical density $2/(T\,v(x))$ ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-2|Theorem §B9.3.2]]).
> - *Moments and leakage.* Example §B4.2.2 below: averages of $x^2$ agree exactly, averages of $x^4$ up to a correction of relative size $1/n^2$, and the probability of being found beyond the turning points falls toward zero, but slowly.
> - *Motion.* Superpositions do move, and their means follow the classical equations exactly ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^ex-b1-2-1|Example §B1.2.1]]); the states that move most nearly like a classical particle are the coherent states of [[§B4.3 Coherent States|§B4.3]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.2, p. 71, Fig. 2.7 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 12, §2*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.2.1: Hermite Polynomials Three Ways
> Find $H_3$ from the recursion formula, from the Rodrigues formula and from the generating function, and check the three-term recursion.
>
> *Recursion formula.* For $K = 7$ ($n = 3$) the recursion reads $a_{j+2} = -2(3 - j)\,a_j/((j + 1)(j + 2))$ (Griffiths eq. (2.85)). Starting from $a_1$ (odd $n$, so $a_0 = 0$): $j = 1$ gives $a_3 = -\tfrac{4}{6}a_1 = -\tfrac23a_1$, and $j = 3$ gives $a_5 = 0$. So $h = a_1(\xi - \tfrac23\xi^3)$; the leading coefficient $-\tfrac23a_1 = 8$ fixes $a_1 = -12$, and $H_3 = 8\xi^3 - 12\xi$.
>
> *Rodrigues.* $\frac{d}{d\xi}e^{-\xi^2} = -2\xi e^{-\xi^2}$, $\frac{d^2}{d\xi^2}e^{-\xi^2} = (4\xi^2 - 2)e^{-\xi^2}$, $\frac{d^3}{d\xi^3}e^{-\xi^2} = \bigl(8\xi - 2\xi(4\xi^2 - 2)\bigr)e^{-\xi^2} = (12\xi - 8\xi^3)e^{-\xi^2}$; times $(-1)^3e^{\xi^2}$: $8\xi^3 - 12\xi$.
>
> *Generating function.* $e^{2z\xi}e^{-z^2} = \bigl(1 + 2z\xi + 2z^2\xi^2 + \tfrac43z^3\xi^3 + \cdots\bigr)\bigl(1 - z^2 + \cdots\bigr)$; the coefficient of $z^3$ is $\tfrac43\xi^3 - 2\xi$, and $3!$ times it is $8\xi^3 - 12\xi$.
>
> *Check.* $H_3 = 2\xi H_2 - 4H_1 = 2\xi(4\xi^2 - 2) - 8\xi = 8\xi^3 - 12\xi$, and $H_3' = 24\xi^2 - 12 = 6H_2$, as $H_n' = 2nH_{n-1}$ requires. The ladder gives the same: $\psi_3 = (\hat a_+)^3\psi_0/\sqrt{3!}$ with $\hat a_+ = (\xi - d/d\xi)/\sqrt2$ produces $(8\xi^3 - 12\xi)\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$ up to the factor $(m\omega/\pi\hbar)^{1/4}/\sqrt{2^3\,3!}$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 2.15–2.16 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 13, eq. (3.67)*

> [!example] Example §B4.2.2: How Classical Is a Stationary State?
> Compare $\psi_n$ with a classical oscillator of the same energy $E_n$, amplitude $A_n$ with $A_n^2 = (2n + 1)\hbar/m\omega$, observed at a random time.
>
> *Second moment.* Classically $x = A\cos\omega t$ averages to $\overline{x^2} = A^2/2$. Quantum mechanically $\langle x^2\rangle_n = (2n + 1)\hbar/2m\omega = A_n^2/2$ ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^ex-b4-1-1|Example §B4.1.1]]): equal, for every $n$.
>
> *Fourth moment.* Classically $\overline{\cos^4} = \tfrac38$, so $\overline{x^4} = \tfrac38A_n^4 = 3\bigl(\tfrac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\bigr)^2\bigl(2n^2 + 2n + \tfrac12\bigr)$. The ladder gives $\langle x^4\rangle_n = 3\bigl(\tfrac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\bigr)^2(2n^2 + 2n + 1)$ (the six orderings counted in [[§B8.1 Nondegenerate Perturbation Theory#^ex-b8-1-1|Example §B8.1.1]]). The difference is $\tfrac38(\hbar/m\omega)^2$, independent of $n$: at $n = 0$ the quantum value is twice the classical one, at large $n$ the relative difference is $\approx 1/4n^2$. At fixed energy it is of order $\hbar^2$, so it vanishes in the classical limit.
>
> *Leakage.* The probability of finding the particle beyond the classical turning points, $P_{\text{out}} = 1 - \int_{-A_n}^{A_n}|\psi_n|^2dx$, computed numerically from Theorem §B4.2.5:
>
> | $n$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 5 | 10 | 20 | 50 |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $P_{\text{out}}$ | 0.157 | 0.112 | 0.095 | 0.074 | 0.060 | 0.048 | 0.036 |
>
> The ground-state value $\operatorname{erfc}(1) = 0.157$ is Griffiths' Problem 2.14 (found at level A, [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^rem-a4-5-2|Remark: Three departures from the classical oscillator]]). The leakage does go to zero, but slowly, roughly like $n^{-1/3}$ over this range: the tail beyond each turning point narrows more slowly than the region between them grows.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.14 (the case $n = 0$) and §2.3.2, Fig. 2.7(b) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 12, §2; moments from the ladder, leakage computed here*
