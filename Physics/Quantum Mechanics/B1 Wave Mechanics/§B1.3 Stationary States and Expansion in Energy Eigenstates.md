For a time-independent potential, level A separated the variables ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]]), found that the stationary states $\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ have constant densities and sharp energies ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-5|Theorem §A4.1.5]]), and built motion from superpositions $\sum c_n\psi_ne^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]). Two claims were quoted, not proved: that *every* solution is such a sum, and that the stationary states are complete ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^rem-a4-3-1|Remark: Every state is such a sum]]). This section closes both gaps. With the conservation laws of §B1.1 it shows that the energy of a normalizable stationary state is real and that nothing measurable changes in it; it proves the completeness of the box states from Fourier series; and it proves that every solution of the Schrödinger equation in the box is the superposition of stationary states with coefficients that simply rotate in phase, $c_n(t) = c_ne^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$. Solving the time-dependent problem is thereby reduced to the eigenvalue problem $\hat H\psi = E\psi$, which occupies Chapters B3–B5. Following level A and PHY 308, the box has width $L$ (Griffiths writes $a$).

## Stationary states

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.1: Separable Solutions Have Real Energy
> Let $V(x)$ be real and independent of time. A nonzero product solution $\Psi(x, t) = \psi(x)\varphi(t)$ of the Schrödinger equation has, up to a constant factor, $\varphi(t) = e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, where $\psi$ solves the time-independent equation, the eigenvalue equation of the Hamiltonian ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]]),
>
> $$
> \hat H\psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V\psi = E\psi .
> $$
>
> If $\Psi$ is normalizable (and decays as in [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]), the separation constant $E$ is **real**, and $\Psi$ is normalized exactly when $\int|\psi|^2dx = 1$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.1, eqs. (2.1)–(2.7), (2.12); Problem 2.1(a) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 10, §1, eqs. (1.2)–(1.12) · Greensite ch. 5, §5.3 · the user's Classical notes, §6.2, §9*

^thm-b1-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The separation, with $E$ an a priori complex constant, is [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^der-a4-1-4|the derivation of Theorem §A4.1.4]]. Write $E = E_0 + i\Gamma$ with $E_0, \Gamma$ real. Then $|e^{-iEt/\hbar}|^2 = e^{2\Gamma t/\hbar}$ and
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi(x, t)|^2\,dx = e^{2\Gamma t/\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi(x)|^2\,dx .
> $$
>
> By [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] the left side does not depend on $t$, and $\int|\psi|^2dx \neq 0$; hence $\Gamma = 0$. With $E$ real, $|\Psi|^2 = |\psi|^2$. Zwiebach reaches the same conclusion from the hermiticity of $\hat H$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]); a complex $E$ is possible only with a non-Hermitian $\hat H$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^ex-b1-1-2|Example §B1.1.2]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.2: Nothing Happens in a Stationary State
> Let $\Psi = \psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ be a normalized stationary state of a real, time-independent $V$, with $\psi$ and $\psi'$ decaying as in [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] ($\psi$ may be complex). Then:
> 1. the expectation value of every dynamical variable $Q(x, p)$ ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^pr-b1-2-2|Principle §B1.2.2]]) is constant in time, $\langle Q\rangle = \int\psi^*\,Q(x, -i\hbar\,d/dx)\,\psi\,dx$;
> 2. the probability current vanishes everywhere, $J(x, t) = 0$, and $\langle p\rangle = 0$;
> 3. the energy is sharp: $\langle H\rangle = E$, $\langle H^2\rangle = E^2$, $\sigma_H = 0$, so every measurement of the energy gives $E$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.1, eqs. (2.8)–(2.9), (2.13)–(2.14) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 10, eqs. (1.13)–(1.14)*

^thm-b1-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) The operator $Q(x, -i\hbar\,\partial_x)$ does not act on $t$, so $\Psi^*\hat Q\Psi = e^{iEt/\hbar}\psi^*\,\hat Q\psi\,e^{-iEt/\hbar} = \psi^*\hat Q\psi$, using $E$ real ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]). (2) $\rho = |\psi|^2$ does not depend on $t$, so the continuity equation ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]) gives $\partial J/\partial x = 0$: $J$ is the same at every $x$. It tends to zero at $\pm\infty$, so it is zero everywhere. Then $\langle p\rangle = m\int J\,dx = 0$ ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]); equivalently, $\langle x\rangle$ is constant by (1). (3) $\hat H\psi = E\psi$ gives $\langle H\rangle = E\int|\psi|^2dx = E$, and $\hat H^2\psi = \hat H(E\psi) = E\hat H\psi = E^2\psi$ gives $\langle H^2\rangle = E^2$, so $\sigma_H^2 = \langle H^2\rangle - \langle H\rangle^2 = 0$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-2|Theorem §A3.4.2]], for $H$). A distribution with zero spread has every sample at its mean.

^der-b1-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^pr-b1-2-2|Principle §B1.2.2]], [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-2|Theorem §A3.4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the theorem adds to level A
> Level A proved items 1 and 3 for functions of $x$ and for $\hat p$ only, used $i\hbar\,\partial_t$ as the energy operator ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^cau-b1-2-2|Caution: Ĥ is the energy operator, not iħ∂/∂t]]), and got $\langle p\rangle = 0$ only for real $\psi$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-5|Theorem §A4.1.5]]). Here every operator of the operator rule is covered, the energy is the eigenvalue of $\hat H$, and $\langle p\rangle = 0$ holds for complex $\psi$ as well: a normalizable stationary state carries no probability current. (That bound states can be chosen real anyway is [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]].) A stationary state that is *not* normalizable can carry a current: for the plane wave $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$, $J = \hbar k\rho/m$ is constant in $x$ and $t$, a steady flow ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]), which is what the scattering states of [[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension|§B3.4]] describe. Motion needs a superposition of different energies (Griffiths Example 2.1; level A's [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-5|Theorem §A4.3.5]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.1 ("Nothing ever happens in a stationary state"); Problem 2.18*

## Completeness of the box states

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.3: Completeness of the Box States
> The stationary states $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(n\pi x/L)$, $n = 1, 2, \dots$, of the infinite square well ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^mod-a4-2-1|Model §A4.2.1]], [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]) form an orthonormal basis of the square-integrable functions on $[0, L]$. That is, for every $f$ with $\int_0^L|f|^2dx < \infty$, the coefficients
>
> $$
> c_n = \int_0^L\psi_n^*(x)\,f(x)\,dx
> $$
>
> satisfy:
> 1. **mean square**: $\displaystyle\int_0^L\Bigl|f - \sum_{n=1}^{N}c_n\psi_n\Bigr|^2dx \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$, and **Parseval**: $\sum_n|c_n|^2 = \int_0^L|f|^2dx$;
> 2. **pointwise**: if $f$ is piecewise smooth, $\sum_nc_n\psi_n(x) = \tfrac12[f(x^+) + f(x^-)]$ for $0 < x < L$;
> 3. **uniform**: if $f$ is continuous and piecewise smooth with $f(0) = f(L) = 0$, the series converges to $f$ uniformly on $[0, L]$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.2, eqs. (2.33)–(2.37) (completeness quoted there as Dirichlet's theorem); proof from Fourier series*

^thm-b1-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Coefficients.* Orthonormality is [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-3|Theorem §A4.2.3]]. If $f = \sum c_m\psi_m$, multiplying by $\psi_n^{\ast}$ and integrating kills every term but $m = n$ (Griffiths' "Fourier's trick", eq. (2.36)); that gives the formula for $c_n$, which is the sine-series coefficient in disguise: $\sum c_n\psi_n(x) = \sum B_n\sin(n\pi x/L)$ with $B_n = \sqrt{2/L}\,c_n = \tfrac2L\int_0^Lf\sin(n\pi x/L)\,dx$.
>
> *Parts 2 and 3* are then the convergence of the sine series on an interval, [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-7|WO Theorem §B4.1.7]], which extends $f$ to an odd function of period $2L$ and applies Fourier's theorem ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|WO Theorem §B4.1.6]]).
>
> *Part 1.* By [[§27 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-27-8|556 Thm. §27.8]] it suffices to show completeness: a square-integrable $g$ on $[0, L]$ with $\int_0^L\psi_n g\,dx = 0$ for all $n$ is zero. Let $\tilde g$ be the odd extension of $g$ to $[-L, L]$. For every $n$, $\tilde g(x)\cos(n\pi x/L)$ is odd, so its integral over $[-L, L]$ vanishes, and $\tilde g(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)$ is even, so its integral is $2\int_0^Lg\sin(n\pi x/L)\,dx = 0$. Hence $\tilde g$ is orthogonal to every $e^{in\pi x/L} = \cos(n\pi x/L) + i\sin(n\pi x/L)$, $n \in \mathbb Z$. These functions, divided by $\sqrt{2L}$, are the Fourier basis of $L^2[0, 2\pi]$ ([[§27 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-27-11|556 Thm. §27.11]]) carried over to $[-L, L]$ by $\theta = \pi(x + L)/L$, which multiplies each basis function by the constant $(-1)^n$ and preserves inner products up to the factor $L/\pi$. A function orthogonal to a complete set is zero, so $\tilde g = 0$ and $g = 0$. Parseval is then part (3) of 556 Thm. §20.8. The one analytic input, the completeness of the Fourier basis itself, is quoted in 556 as well (its proof is deferred there, with the standard route through Fejér's theorem indicated; Mathematical Methods, planned).

> [!remark] Remark: Completeness, from quoted to proved, and beyond the box
> Level A quoted completeness ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^rem-a4-3-1|Remark: Every state is such a sum]], [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^rem-a4-2-4|Remark: Stationary states as a basis]]); for the box it is now a theorem of Fourier analysis. For other potentials the proofs are, in Griffiths' words, "nasty and laborious", and "most physicists simply assume completeness": level B postulates it for the eigenfunctions of every observable ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]]; the cases where it is a theorem are collected in [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^rem-b2-2-1|Remark: When completeness is a theorem, and what it requires]]). The oscillator is the second potential for which it is proved here, with Hermite functions and Fourier analysis ([[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]). Two cautions belong with it.
> - The bound states alone need not be complete. For the finite well or hydrogen, the scattering states with $E$ in a continuous range must be included, and the sum becomes a sum plus an integral (Greensite eq. (5.40)); for hydrogen this is a theorem ([[§40 Bound States Need Not Be Complete꞉ Hydrogen#^cor-40-2|556 Cor. §40.2]]). For the free particle there are no bound states and the expansion is a pure integral, the Fourier integral ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-2|Theorem §B1.4.2]]).
> - "Every function" means every square-integrable function, with convergence in the mean. A function that does not vanish at the walls is still expanded, but its sine series converges to $0$ there, not uniformly, and the $|c_n|^2$ fall only like $1/n^2$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.2 · Greensite ch. 5, §5.3*

## Every solution is a superposition of stationary states

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.4: Expansion in Stationary States and Evolution of the Coefficients
> Let $V$ be real and time-independent, with normalized stationary states $\psi_n$ of energies $E_n$ that form an orthonormal basis (as in the box, [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]]). Let $\Psi(x, t)$ be a normalized solution of the Schrödinger equation satisfying the same boundary conditions (in the box, $\Psi(0, t) = \Psi(L, t) = 0$; on the line, decay at $\pm\infty$), and put $c_n(t) = \int\psi_n^*(x)\,\Psi(x, t)\,dx$. Then
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\frac{dc_n}{dt} = E_n\,c_n, \qquad c_n(t) = c_n\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}, \qquad c_n = \int\psi_n^*(x)\,\Psi(x, 0)\,dx ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, t) = \sum_n c_n\,\psi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}, \qquad \sum_n|c_n|^2 = 1, \qquad \langle H\rangle = \sum_n|c_n|^2E_n ,
> $$
>
> the sum converging in the mean, and $\sum|c_n|^2$ and $\langle H\rangle$ independent of time. The solution is uniquely determined by $\Psi(x, 0)$: every solution is the superposition of stationary states built from its initial values.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.1, eqs. (2.15)–(2.21); §2.2, eqs. (2.37)–(2.40) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 10, §1 · Greensite ch. 5, §5.3, eqs. (5.38)–(5.40)*

^thm-b1-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Coefficients rotate.* Differentiate under the integral and use the Schrödinger equation: $i\hbar\,\dot c_n = \int\psi_n^{\ast}\,i\hbar\,\partial_t\Psi\,dx = \int\psi_n^{\ast}\,\hat H\Psi\,dx$. Move $\hat H$ onto $\psi_n$: the potential term moves because $V$ is real, and for the kinetic term two integrations by parts ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]) give
>
> $$
> \int\psi_n^*\,\Psi''\,dx - \int\psi_n^{*\prime\prime}\,\Psi\,dx = \bigl[\psi_n^*\Psi' - \psi_n^{*\prime}\Psi\bigr]_{\text{ends}} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> since $\psi_n$ and $\Psi$ both vanish at the walls (or at $\pm\infty$). Hence $\int\psi_n^*\hat H\Psi\,dx = \int(\hat H\psi_n)^*\Psi\,dx = E_n^*c_n = E_nc_n$, with $E_n$ real by [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]. The equation $i\hbar\,\dot c_n = E_nc_n$ has the solution $c_n(t) = c_n(0)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$.
>
> *Expansion.* At each time $\Psi(\cdot, t)$ is square-integrable, so by completeness $\Psi(\cdot, t) = \sum c_n(t)\psi_n$ in the mean, with $\sum|c_n(t)|^2 = \int|\Psi(x, t)|^2dx = 1$ (Parseval, [[§27 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-27-8|556 Thm. §27.8]]); $|c_n(t)| = |c_n|$ does not change. For the energy, insert the complete set ([[§38 The Completeness Relation#^cor-38-4|556 Cor. §38.4]]): $\langle H\rangle = \int\Psi^{\ast}\hat H\Psi\,dx = \sum_n\bigl(\int\Psi^{\ast}\psi_n\,dx\bigr)\bigl(\int\psi_n^{\ast}\hat H\Psi\,dx\bigr) = \sum_nc_n^{\ast}(t)\,E_nc_n(t) = \sum_n|c_n|^2E_n$ (Griffiths checks the same with the double sum).
>
> *Uniqueness.* If $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ are solutions with the same initial values, $\Psi_1 - \Psi_2$ is a solution (linearity, [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-2|Theorem §A4.1.2]]) with $\int|\Psi_1 - \Psi_2|^2dx = 0$ at $t = 0$, and by [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] at all times. Conversely, for a smooth $\Psi(x, 0)$ obeying the boundary conditions, the series with the phase factors attached converges well enough to be differentiated term by term and is a solution ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]); for a general square-integrable $\Psi(x, 0)$ it defines the evolved state in the mean-square sense.

> [!remark] Remark: The recipe, and what the coefficients mean
> - *The recipe* (Griffiths): given $V(x)$ and $\Psi(x, 0)$, (1) solve $\hat H\psi = E\psi$ for the $\psi_n$ and $E_n$; (2) find $c_n = \int\psi_n^{\ast}\Psi(x, 0)\,dx$ by Fourier's trick; (3) attach to each term its "wiggle factor" $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$. Only the functional form of the $\psi_n$ and the allowed energies change from one potential to the next.
> - *Time evolution is a rotation.* In the coordinates $c_n$ the evolution multiplies each coordinate by its own phase: a unitary map that is diagonal in the energy basis, the explicit form of the conservation of inner products ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]). The relative phases change, and that is the only source of motion.
> - *Meaning of $|c_n|^2$.* It is the probability that a measurement of the energy gives $E_n$ (Griffiths eq. (2.19); level A's [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^pr-a4-3-3|Principle §A4.3.3]]), whose general form for any observable is the generalized statistical interpretation ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]). Since $|c_n|$ is constant, so is the probability of each energy and its mean: this is **energy conservation** in quantum mechanics.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.1–2.2 (eqs. (2.17), (2.19)–(2.21))*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.3.1: A Parabolic Start in the Box
> A particle in the box starts in $\Psi(x, 0) = Ax(L - x)$ for $0 \le x \le L$.
> - *Normalization.* $|A|^2\int_0^Lx^2(L - x)^2dx = |A|^2L^5/30 = 1$, so $A = \sqrt{30/L^5}$.
> - *Coefficients.* Two integrations by parts give
>
> $$
> c_n = \sqrt{\frac2L}\sqrt{\frac{30}{L^5}}\int_0^L x(L - x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = \frac{4\sqrt{15}}{(n\pi)^3}\bigl[1 - \cos n\pi\bigr] = \begin{cases} 8\sqrt{15}/(n\pi)^3, & n \text{ odd},\\ 0, & n \text{ even}.\end{cases}
> $$
>
> The even coefficients vanish because $\Psi(x, 0)$ is symmetric about $x = L/2$ and the even-$n$ states are antisymmetric about it. $\Psi(x, 0)$ is continuous, piecewise smooth and zero at the walls, so the series converges uniformly ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]], part 3), and by [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, t) = \sqrt{\frac{30}{L}}\Bigl(\frac2\pi\Bigr)^3\sum_{n = 1, 3, 5, \dots}\frac{1}{n^3}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,e^{-in^2\pi^2\hbar t/2mL^2} .
> $$
>
> - *Energy probabilities.* $|c_1|^2 = 960/\pi^6 = 0.998555$: the parabola is almost the ground state, and more than 99.8% of energy measurements give $E_1 = \pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2$. The probabilities add up to one, $\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}960/(\pi^6n^6) = 1$, because $\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}n^{-6} = \pi^6/960$.
> - *Mean energy, two ways.* From the coefficients, with $\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}n^{-4} = \pi^4/96$,
>
> $$
> \langle H\rangle = \sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac{960}{\pi^6n^6}\,\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2} = \frac{480\hbar^2}{\pi^4mL^2}\cdot\frac{\pi^4}{96} = \frac{5\hbar^2}{mL^2} ;
> $$
>
> directly from $\Psi(x, 0)$, with $V = 0$ inside and [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]], $\langle H\rangle = \tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_0^L|\Psi'|^2dx = \tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\cdot\tfrac{30}{L^5}\int_0^L(L - 2x)^2dx = \tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\cdot\tfrac{30}{L^5}\cdot\tfrac{L^3}{3} = 5\hbar^2/mL^2$. This is slightly above $E_1 = 4.935\,\hbar^2/mL^2$, because of the small admixture of $n = 3, 5, \dots$, and it never changes.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Examples 2.2–2.3; Problem 2.9*

^ex-b1-3-1

> [!example] Example §B1.3.2: Measuring the Energy, Then the Position
> A particle in the box is at $t = 0$ in $\Psi(x, 0) = C\bigl(\alpha\psi_1(x) + \beta\psi_2(x)\bigr)$ with $\alpha, \beta$ real (PHY 308 calls them $a$, $b$).
> - *(a)* By orthonormality $\int|\Psi|^2dx = C^2(\alpha^2 + \beta^2)$, so $C = 1/\sqrt{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}$ (up to a phase).
> - *(b)* By [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]] and the meaning of $|c_n|^2$, an energy measurement gives $E_1 = \pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2$ with probability $\alpha^2/(\alpha^2 + \beta^2)$ or $E_2 = 4E_1$ with probability $\beta^2/(\alpha^2 + \beta^2)$, nothing else; these probabilities do not depend on when the measurement is made.
> - *(c)* Now the position is measured, afterwards (as the problem asks) or instead, and the particle is found near $x_0$. Immediately afterwards $\Psi$ has collapsed to a narrow spike around $x_0$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^rem-b1-1-1|Remark: Collapse, and the Born rule at level B]]); everything about $\alpha$, $\beta$ and the earlier energy is erased. The new state is not a combination of $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ but of all the $\psi_n$. To see how many, model the spike as $1/\sqrt\varepsilon$ on an interval of width $\varepsilon \ll L$ about $x_0$:
>
> $$
> c_n = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L\varepsilon}}\int_{x_0 - \varepsilon/2}^{x_0 + \varepsilon/2}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L\varepsilon}}\,\frac{2L}{n\pi}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x_0}{L}\,\sin\frac{n\pi\varepsilon}{2L} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{L}}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x_0}{L} \quad (n \ll L/\varepsilon) .
> $$
>
> The probabilities $|c_n|^2$ do not decrease with $n$ until $n \sim L/\varepsilon$, so a subsequent energy measurement can give energies far above $E_2$; for this sharp-edged spike $\sum|c_n|^2E_n$ even diverges, since $|c_n|^2 \propto 1/n^2$ at large $n$ while $E_n \propto n^2$, as [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]] predicts for a wave function with jumps. A smooth spike of width $\varepsilon$ has $\langle H\rangle \sim \hbar^2/m\varepsilon^2$: a sharp position measurement deposits energy, the uncertainty principle at work.
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 5 (solved here; part (c) made quantitative)*

> [!example] Example §B1.3.3: The Time Dependence Is Forced
> PHY 308 asks for which real phase $\phi_n(t)$ the function $\psi_n(x, t) = N\sin(n\pi x/L)\,e^{-i\phi_n(t)}$ (zero outside the box) solves the time-dependent Schrödinger equation. Inside the box $V = 0$, and
>
> $$
> i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi_n}{\partial t} = \hbar\,\dot\phi_n\,\psi_n, \qquad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{\partial^2\psi_n}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Bigl(\frac{n\pi}{L}\Bigr)^2\psi_n ,
> $$
>
> so $\dot\phi_n = n^2\pi^2\hbar/2mL^2 = E_n/\hbar$ and $\phi_n(t) = E_nt/\hbar + \phi_n(0)$: the phase must turn uniformly at the rate $E_n/\hbar$, and the constant it turns at is real, as [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]] requires. Normalization gives $N = \sqrt{2/L}$. The remaining parts of the problem, $\langle x\rangle = L/2$, $\sigma_x = L\sqrt{1/12 - 1/2n^2\pi^2}$, $\langle p\rangle = 0$, $\sigma_p = n\pi\hbar/L$, are level A's [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-4|Theorem §A4.2.4]], now valid at every $t$ by [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]]; the product
>
> $$
> \sigma_x\sigma_p = \frac\hbar2\sqrt{\frac{n^2\pi^2}{3} - 2}
> $$
>
> is $0.568\,\hbar$ for $n = 1$ and grows linearly with $n$: the uncertainty relation holds for every box state and is never saturated ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^rem-a4-2-5|Remark: What these numbers mean]]); only a Gaussian saturates it ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-5|Theorem §B2.4.5]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 3, Problem 3 (solved here) · Griffiths Problem 2.4*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The box states are an orthonormal basis of $L^2[0, L]$ ([[§27 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^def-27-4|556 Def. §27.4]], [[§27 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-27-8|556 Thm. §27.8]]); the expansion of Theorem §B1.3.4 is the completeness relation $\sum_n|\psi_n\rangle\langle\psi_n| = \mathbf 1$ ([[§38 The Completeness Relation#^thm-38-2|556 Thm. §38.2]]), and $\langle H\rangle = \sum|c_n|^2E_n$ is "inserting a complete set of states" ([[§38 The Completeness Relation#^cor-38-4|556 Cor. §38.4]]). Hydrogen's bound states alone are not complete ([[§40 Bound States Need Not Be Complete꞉ Hydrogen#^cor-40-2|556 Cor. §40.2]]).
> - The classical twin: on a string fixed at both ends the separable solutions are the same sines, and every motion is a superposition of normal modes ([[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-1|WO Theorem §B4.2.1]]); the difference is the time factor, $\cos(\omega_nt)$ there, $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ with $E_n \propto n^2$ here.
> - In Dirac notation ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation|§B2.1]]), $c_n = \langle\psi_n|\Psi\rangle$ and $|\Psi(t)\rangle = \sum_ne^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|\psi_n\rangle\langle\psi_n|\Psi(0)\rangle$.
