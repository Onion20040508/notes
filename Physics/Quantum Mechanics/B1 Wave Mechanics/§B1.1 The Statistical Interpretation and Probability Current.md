Level A gave the wave function its meaning, $|\Psi|^2$ is a probability density ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-1|Principle §A3.4.1]]), demanded that it be normalized ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-2|Def. §A3.4.2]]), and gave it an equation of motion ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]]). It never checked that the two fit together: the Schrödinger equation fixes $\Psi$ at all times once $\Psi(x, 0)$ is given, so normalizing at $t = 0$ is consistent only if the equation itself keeps the total probability equal to $1$. This section proves that it does, and more: probability is conserved *locally*, flowing from place to place with a **probability current** $J$, just as electric charge flows with a current density. The current is the tool with which Chapter B3 defines reflection and transmission, and it already shows which states move and which do not.

*Notation for level B.* Level B follows Griffiths. The potential energy is $V(x, t)$ (see the caution below), the kinetic energy $T$; $\Psi(x, t)$ is the full wave function and $\psi(x)$ a stationary state, $\Psi = \psi\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}$; operators carry hats, $\hat Q$, where confusion is possible; $\langle Q\rangle$ is an expectation value and $\sigma_Q$ the standard deviation ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-3|Def. §A3.4.3]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-4|Def. §A3.4.4]]). With this, the Schrödinger equation reads (Griffiths eq. (1.1))

$$
i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} + V\Psi .
$$

> [!caution] Caution: V, not U
> Level A, following University Physics, writes $U(x)$ for the potential energy; level B, following Griffiths, Zwiebach and the PHY 308 problem sets, writes $V(x)$. It is the same quantity, an energy in joules, and **not** an electric potential in volts: for an electron in an electrostatic potential $\phi(x)$, $V(x) = -e\,\phi(x)$. Likewise level A's kinetic energy $K$ is Griffiths' $T$. Every level-A formula carries over with $U \to V$, $K \to T$; for instance $\hat H = \hat p^2/2m + V(x)$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-5|Def. §A3.4.5]]). Other notes of level B link this caution instead of repeating it.

^cau-b1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Collapse, and the Born rule at level B
> Born's rule makes quantum mechanics *indeterministic*: even complete knowledge of $\Psi$ gives only the probabilities of the possible results of a position measurement. Whatever one thinks happened before the measurement, everyone agrees that an immediately repeated measurement returns the same value, so the measurement must change the state: if the particle is found at $C$, $\Psi$ **collapses** to a spike at $C$, after which it again evolves by the Schrödinger equation and spreads ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^rem-a3-4-3|Remark: Measurement and the Copenhagen interpretation]]). Quantum mechanics thus has two kinds of process, the smooth evolution studied in this chapter and the abrupt change at a measurement. At level B the Born rule becomes the position case of the generalized statistical interpretation, which gives the probabilities for any observable and makes collapse a postulate ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation|§B2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.2*

^rem-b1-1-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Where was the particle before the measurement?
> Griffiths sorts the reactions to quantum indeterminacy by the answer to one question: a measurement finds the particle at $C$; where was it just before?
> - *Realist* (Einstein): at $C$. Then $\Psi$ is not the whole story, and some further **hidden variable** is needed to describe the particle completely.
> - *Orthodox* (Copenhagen, Bohr, Jordan): nowhere in particular; the measurement forced the particle to take a position.
> - *Agnostic* (Pauli): the question cannot be tested, so it is not physics.
>
> Bell (1964) showed that the realist and orthodox answers make different, measurable predictions, and the experiments favour the orthodox one; Griffiths notes that nonlocal hidden-variable theories such as Bohm's, and many-worlds formulations, do not fit the three categories.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.2*

## The probability current

> [!definition] Definition §B1.1.1: Probability Density; Probability Current
> For a particle in one dimension with wave function $\Psi(x, t)$, the **probability density** is $\rho(x, t) = |\Psi(x, t)|^2$ and the **probability current** is
>
> $$
> J(x, t) = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\Bigl(\Psi\,\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x} - \Psi^*\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\Bigr) = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\operatorname{Im}\Bigl(\Psi^*\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> $J$ is real. For normalized $\Psi$ ($[\Psi] = \mathrm{m^{-1/2}}$) it has units of $1/\text{time}$: it is the probability per unit time that flows past the point $x$ in the $+x$ direction. In three dimensions,
>
> $$
> \vec J(\vec r, t) = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\operatorname{Im}\bigl(\Psi^*\,\nabla\Psi\bigr),
> $$
>
> a probability per unit time per unit area.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 1.14 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 6, §3–4, eqs. (3.6), (3.10), (4.1) · the user's Classical notes, §12*

^def-b1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.1: Continuity Equation for Probability
> Let $\Psi$ solve the Schrödinger equation with a **real** potential energy $V(x, t)$. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial J}{\partial x} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> and the probability $P_{ab}(t) = \int_a^b |\Psi(x, t)|^2\,dx$ of finding the particle between $a$ and $b$ changes only by flow through the endpoints:
>
> $$
> \frac{dP_{ab}}{dt} = J(a, t) - J(b, t) .
> $$
>
> In three dimensions $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$, and the probability $Q_\Omega$ of finding the particle in a fixed region $\Omega$ obeys $dQ_\Omega/dt = -\oint_{\partial\Omega}\vec J\cdot d\vec a$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.4, eqs. (1.22)–(1.25); Problem 1.14 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 6, eqs. (3.4)–(3.5), (3.13), (4.2)–(4.7) · the user's Classical notes, §12*

^thm-b1-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the product rule (Griffiths eq. (1.22)), $\partial_t|\Psi|^2 = \Psi^*\,\partial_t\Psi + \Psi\,\partial_t\Psi^*$. The Schrödinger equation and its complex conjugate give (eqs. (1.23)–(1.24))
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\,\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} - \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi, \qquad \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t} = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\,\frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2} + \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi^* ;
> $$
>
> the second line uses $V^* = V$. Substituting, the potential terms cancel, $-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}V|\Psi|^2 + \tfrac{i}{\hbar}V|\Psi|^2 = 0$, and what remains is a total derivative (eq. (1.25)):
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\Bigl(\Psi^*\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2}\Psi\Bigr) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Bigl[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\Bigl(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\Bigr)\Bigr] = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x} ,
> $$
>
> because the derivative of the bracket produces $\partial_x\Psi^*\,\partial_x\Psi$ twice, with opposite signs. The two forms of $J$ agree since $\Psi^*\partial_x\Psi - \Psi\,\partial_x\Psi^* = 2i\operatorname{Im}(\Psi^*\partial_x\Psi)$. Integrating over the fixed interval $[a, b]$ and using the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]), $dP_{ab}/dt = -\int_a^b\partial_xJ\,dx = J(a, t) - J(b, t)$. In three dimensions the same steps with $\nabla^2$ in place of $\partial_x^2$ use $\nabla\cdot(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi - \Psi\nabla\Psi^*) = \Psi^*\nabla^2\Psi - \Psi\nabla^2\Psi^*$ (the terms $\nabla\Psi^*\cdot\nabla\Psi$ cancel), and the integral over $\Omega$ becomes a flux by the divergence theorem ([[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-1|452 Thm. §28.1]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the current
> - The continuity equation is the same bookkeeping as local conservation of electric charge ([[§A1.1 Electric Charge#^pr-a1-1-2|EM Principle §A1.1.2]]), with $\rho$ for the charge density and $J$ for the current density ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^def-a5-2-3|EM Def. §A5.2.3]]): if the probability in a region changes, it has flowed in or out across the boundary. Probability cannot disappear at one place and reappear at another.
> - If $J(a) > 0$ probability enters $[a, b]$ at the left end; if $J(b) > 0$ it leaves at the right end (Zwiebach).
> - For a plane wave $\Psi = Ae^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ the current is $J = |A|^2\hbar k/m = \rho\,v$ with $v = \hbar k/m$ the particle velocity: density times speed, the flux of a beam that level A used to define reflection and transmission ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-1|Remark: Working with plane waves anyway]]). At level B the coefficients $R$ and $T$ are defined as ratios of currents ([[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 6, §3–4 (table of the analogy with electromagnetism) · Griffiths Problem 2.18*

^rem-b1-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.2: Current from Modulus and Phase
> Where $\Psi \neq 0$, write $\Psi = R\,e^{iS/\hbar}$ with $R = |\Psi| > 0$ and $S$ real. Then
>
> $$
> J = \frac{\rho}{m}\,\frac{\partial S}{\partial x} .
> $$
>
> The density is carried along with the local velocity $v(x, t) = (1/m)\,\partial S/\partial x$, set by the gradient of the phase; the modulus does not contribute. In particular:
> 1. if $\Psi(x, t) = e^{i\alpha(t)}f(x)$ with $f$ real, then $J = 0$; this holds for every stationary state $\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ with real $\psi$;
> 2. for $\Psi = Ae^{i(kx - \omega t)}$, $S = \hbar(kx - \omega t)$ and $J = \rho\,\hbar k/m$.
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 6, eq. (3.6) (current as $\tfrac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}\Psi^{\ast}\partial_x\Psi$); the polar form is worked out here*

^thm-b1-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\partial_x\Psi = \bigl(R' + iRS'/\hbar\bigr)e^{iS/\hbar}$, so $\Psi^*\partial_x\Psi = RR' + iR^2S'/\hbar$, whose imaginary part is $R^2S'/\hbar = \rho S'/\hbar$. By [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], $J = (\hbar/m)\,\rho S'/\hbar = \rho S'/m$. (1): $\Psi^*\partial_x\Psi = f f'$ is real, so its imaginary part vanishes, also where $f = 0$. (2): $S' = \hbar k$.

## Conservation of probability

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.3: Conservation of Normalization
> Let $\Psi$ solve the Schrödinger equation with a real potential energy, and let $J(x, t) \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$ (as it does if $\Psi \to 0$ and $\partial\Psi/\partial x$ stays bounded there). Then
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi(x, t)|^2\,dx = 0 .
> $$
>
> A wave function normalized at $t = 0$ stays normalized for all time.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.4, eqs. (1.20)–(1.27) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 6, §1–3, eqs. (1.5)–(1.6), (3.9)–(3.11)*

^thm-b1-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $L \to \infty$ in [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] with $a = -L$, $b = L$: $dP_{-L,L}/dt = J(-L, t) - J(L, t) \to 0$. (Griffiths differentiates under the integral sign over the whole line, eq. (1.21); this is legitimate when the convergence is uniform, which Griffiths' footnote leaves to "a competent mathematician" and which holds for every wave function met in practice.) With $\Psi \to 0$ and $\partial_x\Psi$ bounded, $|J| \le (\hbar/m)\,|\Psi|\,|\partial_x\Psi| \to 0$, which is Zwiebach's form of the condition.

^der-b1-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the theorem matters
> - At level A, normalization was a condition imposed on $\Psi$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-2|Def. §A3.4.2]]). The Schrödinger equation is linear, so $A\Psi$ is a solution whenever $\Psi$ is, and the constant $A$ can be chosen once, at $t = 0$. One cannot renormalize later: a time-dependent $A(t)\Psi$ is no longer a solution. Without the theorem the statistical interpretation and the equation of motion would contradict each other (Griffiths: "the whole theory would crumble").
> - Only the ratios $|\Psi|^2/\int|\Psi|^2$ are physical: a square-integrable but unnormalized $\Psi$ gives the probabilities $dP = |\Psi|^2dx/N$ with $N = \int|\Psi|^2dx$ (Zwiebach). Solutions with $\int|\Psi|^2 = \infty$, like the plane wave, and the trivial solution $\Psi = 0$, cannot represent a particle. A normalizable $\Psi$ that has a limit at $\pm\infty$ must go to zero there, and faster than $1/\sqrt{|x|}$.
> - The reality of $V$ is essential; with an imaginary part, probability leaks away ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^ex-b1-1-2|Example §B1.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.4 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 6, §1*

^rem-b1-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.4: Conservation of Overlaps
> Let $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ be normalizable solutions of the Schrödinger equation with the same real $V(x, t)$, both satisfying the conditions of [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi_1^*(x, t)\,\Psi_2(x, t)\,dx = 0 .
> $$
>
> In particular, two states that are orthogonal at one time ($\int\Psi_1^*\Psi_2\,dx = 0$) remain orthogonal at all times. [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] is the case $\Psi_1 = \Psi_2$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 1.15*

^thm-b1-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> As in [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^der-b1-1-1|the derivation of Theorem §B1.1.1]], with $\partial_t\Psi_2 = \tfrac{i\hbar}{2m}\partial_x^2\Psi_2 - \tfrac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi_2$ and $\partial_t\Psi_1^* = -\tfrac{i\hbar}{2m}\partial_x^2\Psi_1^* + \tfrac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi_1^*$ (again $V^* = V$),
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigl(\Psi_1^*\Psi_2\bigr) = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\Bigl(\Psi_1^*\frac{\partial^2\Psi_2}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial^2\Psi_1^*}{\partial x^2}\Psi_2\Bigr) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Bigl[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\Bigl(\Psi_1^*\frac{\partial\Psi_2}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial\Psi_1^*}{\partial x}\Psi_2\Bigr)\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> the $V$ terms cancelling. The bracket tends to zero at $\pm\infty$ because each wave function does and their derivatives stay bounded, so integrating over the line gives zero, as in the derivation of Theorem §B1.1.3.

^der-b1-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What is really conserved
> $\int\Psi_1^{\ast}\Psi_2\,dx$ is the inner product $\langle\Psi_1|\Psi_2\rangle$ of the two states ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation|§B2.1]]). Theorem §B1.1.4 says that time evolution preserves all inner products, lengths and angles alike: it is a unitary map of the state space. Zwiebach traces conservation of probability to one property of the Hamiltonian, $\int(\hat H\Psi_1)^{\ast}\Psi_2\,dx = \int\Psi_1^{\ast}\hat H\Psi_2\,dx$, which says that $\hat H$ is **Hermitian** ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators|§B2.2]]); for $\hat H = -\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2 + V$ it is exactly the two integrations by parts hidden in the derivation above, together with $V^{\ast} = V$. Level A's statement that $\sum|c_n|^2$ does not change in time ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]) is a special case.
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 6, §2, eqs. (2.10)–(2.14)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.1.1: A Gaussian Packet and Its Current
> At $t = 0$ a particle has $\Psi(x, 0) = C\,e^{-(x - x_0)^2/4\sigma^2}$ with $\sigma > 0$.
> - *Normalization.* $|\Psi|^2 = C^2e^{-(x - x_0)^2/2\sigma^2}$ and $\int e^{-u^2/2\sigma^2}du = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma$ ([[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^ex-25-4|452 Ex. §25.4]]), so $C = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}$.
> - *Shape and moments.* $|\Psi|^2$ is the normal distribution with mean $x_0$ and standard deviation $\sigma$: it peaks at $x_0$, is symmetric about it, and has $\langle x\rangle = x_0$, $\langle x^2\rangle = x_0^2 + \sigma^2$, $\sigma_x = \sigma$ (with $\int u^2e^{-u^2/2\sigma^2}du = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma^3$). The parameter $\sigma$ alone controls how well the particle is localized; writing the exponent with $4\sigma^2$, as PHY 308 does, makes $\sigma$ exactly the position uncertainty (level A's $e^{-x^2/2a^2}$ has $\sigma_x = a/\sqrt2$, [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^ex-a3-5-1|Example §A3.5.1]]). Griffiths uses two other width parameters, both called $a$: $e^{-ax^2}$ in his Problem 2.21, so $a = 1/4\sigma^2$, and $e^{-a(x - \langle x\rangle)^2/2\hbar}$ for the minimum-uncertainty packet, so $a = \hbar/2\sigma^2$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-5|Theorem §B2.4.5]]). Level B writes every Gaussian with $\sigma$.
> - *Current.* $\Psi(x, 0)$ is real, so $J(x, 0) = 0$ everywhere ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]): at this instant no probability flows, and $\partial_t\rho = -\partial_xJ = 0$. Multiply by a phase, $\Psi(x, 0) = C\,e^{-(x - x_0)^2/4\sigma^2}e^{ik_0x}$: the density, and with it every position probability, is unchanged, but now $S = \hbar k_0x$ and
>
> $$
> J(x, 0) = \frac{\hbar k_0}{m}\,\rho(x, 0), \qquad J(x_0, 0) = \frac{\hbar k_0}{m}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma} ,
> $$
>
> the rate at which probability crosses the centre. By [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] the probability to the right of $x_0$ grows at this rate: the packet starts to move toward $+x$. A snapshot of $|\Psi|^2$ cannot tell the two states apart; the phase of $\Psi$ carries the motion.
> - *Later times.* The free evolution of this packet is worked out in [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]]: the centre moves at $v_0 = \hbar k_0/m$, the width grows, and $J = \rho\,\bigl[v_0 + (x - \langle x\rangle)\,t/(t^2 + \tau^2)\bigr]$ with $\tau = 2m\sigma^2/\hbar$, so the front runs ahead of the centre and the rear lags behind (figure). For $k_0 = 0$, $J$ is positive to the right of the centre and negative to the left: the packet does not move, but spreads.
>
> *Source: PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 6 (parts a–d) · Griffiths Problem 1.14 (current); the current is worked out here*

> [!example]- ★ Example §B1.1.2: An Unstable Particle
> A crude model of a particle that decays with lifetime $\tau_d$ gives the potential energy an imaginary part, $V = V_0 - i\Gamma$, with $V_0$ real and $\Gamma > 0$ constant. Then the conjugated Schrödinger equation reads $\partial_t\Psi^* = -\tfrac{i\hbar}{2m}\partial_x^2\Psi^* + \tfrac{i}{\hbar}V^*\Psi^*$ with $V^* = V_0 + i\Gamma$, and in the derivation of [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] the potential terms no longer cancel:
>
> $$
> -\frac{i}{\hbar}(V - V^*)\,|\Psi|^2 = -\frac{i}{\hbar}(-2i\Gamma)\,|\Psi|^2 = -\frac{2\Gamma}{\hbar}\,\rho, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial J}{\partial x} = -\frac{2\Gamma}{\hbar}\,\rho .
> $$
>
> Integrating over all $x$, the total probability $P(t) = \int|\Psi|^2dx$ obeys $dP/dt = -(2\Gamma/\hbar)P$, so
>
> $$
> P(t) = e^{-2\Gamma t/\hbar} = e^{-t/\tau_d}, \qquad \tau_d = \frac{\hbar}{2\Gamma} .
> $$
>
> Probability is no longer conserved: it disappears everywhere at the same relative rate, without flowing anywhere. This is why [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] needs a real $V$ (a Hermitian $\hat H$). A stationary state of such a potential has the complex energy $E_0 - i\Gamma$; its probability decays as $|e^{-i(E_0 - i\Gamma)t/\hbar}|^2 = e^{-2\Gamma t/\hbar}$, and $2\Gamma = \hbar/\tau_d$ is the natural line width of level A ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]]). For a genuine, normalizable stationary state the energy must therefore be real ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 1.17*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The continuity equation is a local conservation law; its integral form uses the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]) in one dimension and the divergence theorem ([[§28 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-28-1|452 Thm. §28.1]]) in three; normalizability is the convergence of a doubly improper integral ([[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]]), membership in $L^2$ ([[§17 The Function Spaces Lᵖ(Ω)#^def-17-1|556 Def. §17.1]]).
> - Writing $\Psi = Re^{iS/\hbar}$ splits the Schrödinger equation into the continuity equation of Theorem §B1.1.2 (velocity $\nabla S/m$) and a Hamilton–Jacobi equation with a quantum correction: [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^rem-b8-3-4|CM Remark: The classical limit of the Schrödinger equation]]; the semiclassical version is the WKB approximation ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^rem-b9-3-1|Remark: The expansion in powers of ħ, and the classical action]]).
