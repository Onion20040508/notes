The free particle, $V = 0$, should be the simplest case, and in quantum mechanics it is surprisingly subtle: its stationary states are plane waves, which cannot be normalized, so a free particle never has a definite energy. Level A stated the way out, a wave packet built from plane waves with the Fourier transform of $\Psi(x, 0)$ as weight, and quoted the inversion step ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-1|Theorem §A4.6.1]]); it showed that the envelope moves at the particle velocity ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]]) and that free packets spread ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^rem-a3-3-3|Remark: Matter waves are dispersive, so free packets spread]]). The mathematics is already in Oscillations, Waves and Optics: Fourier transforms, inversion, Plancherel and the Gaussian ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function|WO §B4.4]]), and the spreading of a Gaussian packet in a medium with quadratic dispersion ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^thm-b6-2-4|WO Theorem §B6.2.4]]). This section does not re-derive them. It adds what quantum mechanics brings: the dispersion relation $\omega = \hbar k^2/2m$, exactly quadratic, so the second-order results of WO are exact; $|\Psi|^2$ as a probability density whose normalization the evolution keeps; and $|\phi(k)|^2$ as the distribution of momentum $p = \hbar k$, which for a free particle never changes.

## Conventions and the momentum amplitude

> [!caution] Caution: Fourier conventions in QM and in WO
> This vault's quantum mechanics follows Griffiths, with $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ in both directions (Griffiths eq. (2.103)):
>
> $$
> \Psi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk, \qquad \phi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx .
> $$
>
> WO puts $1/2\pi$ in the forward transform, $\tilde f(k) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int f(x)e^{-ikx}dx$, $f(x) = \int\tilde f(k)e^{ikx}dk$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|WO Def. §B4.4.1]]). **Conversion:** $\phi(k) = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\tilde\Psi(k)$. WO's Parseval–Plancherel identity $\int|f|^2dx = 2\pi\int|\tilde f|^2dk$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|WO Theorem §B4.4.4]]) becomes $\int|\Psi|^2dx = \int|\phi|^2dk$, and WO's Gaussian rule $e^{-x^2/2s^2} \mapsto \frac{s}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-s^2k^2/2}$ becomes $e^{-x^2/2s^2} \mapsto s\,e^{-s^2k^2/2}$. WO's travelling waves and packets ([[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^thm-b6-1-1|WO Theorem §B6.1.1]]) use $e^{i(\omega t - kx)}$, the complex conjugate of the quantum $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$: a WO packet formula becomes a quantum one by complex conjugation ([[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^cau-b6-1-1|WO Caution: Sign conventions for packets]]), with WO's spectrum $\hat a(k) = \phi(k)^{\ast}/\sqrt{2\pi}$. Names differ too: Griffiths calls the inversion pair itself "Plancherel's theorem", while WO keeps that name for the norm identity. Zwiebach (Lectures 7–8) and Fleisch (§4.4) use Griffiths' convention (Zwiebach writes $\Phi(k)$); Zwiebach's Lecture 5, eqs. (1.17)–(1.18), omits the $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$. The amplitude in the momentum variable, $\Phi(p) = \phi(p/\hbar)/\sqrt\hbar$ with $1/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$ in both directions (Zwiebach Lecture 8, eq. (1.11); Fleisch eqs. (4.38)–(4.39)), is the momentum-space wave function of [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]].

^cau-b1-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §B1.4.1: Momentum Amplitude
> The **momentum amplitude** (wave-number amplitude) of a square-integrable wave function $\Psi(x, t)$ is its Fourier transform in Griffiths' convention,
>
> $$
> \phi(k, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi(x, t)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx, \qquad \Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(k, t)\,e^{ikx}\,dk ,
> $$
>
> and $\phi(k) = \phi(k, 0)$. It is the weight with which the plane wave $e^{ikx}$, of momentum $p = \hbar k$, enters $\Psi$. $\Psi$ and $\phi$ carry the same information: either determines the other ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|WO Theorem §B4.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.4, eqs. (2.101)–(2.104) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 7, eqs. (1.1)–(1.2); Lecture 8, eq. (1.1) · Fleisch §4.4, eq. (4.15)*

## Free stationary states and wave packets

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.4.1: Free-Particle Stationary States
> For $V = 0$ the time-independent equation $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' = E\psi$ has bounded solutions only for $E \ge 0$; for $E > 0$ they are $\psi = Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}$, $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$, two independent solutions for each energy. The separable solutions are
>
> $$
> \Psi_k(x, t) = A\,e^{i(kx - \hbar k^2t/2m)}, \qquad k \in \mathbb R, \qquad E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}, \quad p = \hbar k ,
> $$
>
> moving toward $+x$ for $k > 0$ and toward $-x$ for $k < 0$. Each carries the steady probability current $J = |A|^2\hbar k/m$, and none is normalizable: $\int|\Psi_k|^2dx = |A|^2\cdot\infty$. There is no free particle with a definite energy. The crests move at $v_{\text{quantum}} = \hbar|k|/2m$, half the classical speed $\sqrt{2E/m}$ of a particle of energy $E$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.4, eqs. (2.91)–(2.100); Problem 2.18 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 5, eqs. (1.11)–(1.14) · Greensite ch. 5, §5.4*

^thm-b1-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The solutions where the potential is constant are [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-6|Theorem §A4.1.6]] with $U_0 = 0$. For $E < 0$ they are combinations of $e^{\pm\kappa x}$, $\kappa = \sqrt{-2mE}/\hbar$, and every nonzero one blows up at $+\infty$ or $-\infty$; for $E = 0$, $\psi = A + Bx$ is bounded only if $B = 0$, which is the case $k = 0$ of the formula. Attaching $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]) and letting $k$ take both signs gives $\Psi_k$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]]); its phase $kx - \hbar k^2t/2m$ is constant along $x = (\hbar k/2m)t + \text{const}$, which gives the crest speed ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-1|Theorem §A3.3.1]]). The current follows from [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]] with $S = \hbar(kx - \hbar k^2t/2m)$, and $|\Psi_k|^2 = |A|^2$ has an infinite integral.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.4.2: The Free Wave Packet
> Let $\Psi(x, 0)$ be normalized, smooth and rapidly decreasing (so that its derivatives have Fourier transforms), with momentum amplitude $\phi(k)$ ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^def-b1-4-1|Def. §B1.4.1]]). Then the unique solution of the free Schrödinger equation with these initial values is
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(k)\,e^{i(kx - \hbar k^2t/2m)}\,dk, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \phi(k, t) = \phi(k)\,e^{-i\hbar k^2t/2m},
> $$
>
> and $\int|\Psi(x, t)|^2dx = \int|\phi(k)|^2dk = 1$ for all $t$. It is the expansion of [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]] with an integral over the continuous label $k$ in place of the sum, and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\phi(k)\,dk$ in place of $c_n$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.4, eqs. (2.101)–(2.104) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 7, §3, eqs. (3.1)–(3.3); Lecture 8, eq. (1.9) · Greensite ch. 5, §5.4, eq. (5.44)*

^thm-b1-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Initial values.* $\phi = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\tilde\Psi$ ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^cau-b1-4-1|Caution: Fourier conventions in QM and in WO]]), so at $t = 0$ the integral is the inverse transform, which returns $\Psi(x, 0)$ by Fourier inversion ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|WO Theorem §B4.4.1]]).
>
> *Solution.* Each component $e^{i(kx - \hbar k^2t/2m)}$ solves the free equation ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]]). Differentiating under the integral sign brings down $-k^2$ from $\partial_x^2$ and $-i\hbar k^2/2m$ from $\partial_t$; this is legitimate because $\phi$, like $\Psi(x, 0)$, is smooth and rapidly decreasing (by the derivative rule of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|WO Theorem §B4.4.2]], $k^n\phi$ is the transform of $(-i)^n\Psi^{(n)}(x, 0)$ and so bounded for every $n$), so $k^2\phi(k)$ is integrable. So $\Psi$ solves the equation.
>
> *Uniqueness.* The difference of two solutions with the same initial values is a solution of zero norm at $t = 0$, hence at all $t$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]).
>
> *Norm.* By uniqueness of the transform, the momentum amplitude of $\Psi(\cdot, t)$ is $\phi(k)e^{-i\hbar k^2t/2m}$, whose modulus is $|\phi(k)|$. Plancherel ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|WO Theorem §B4.4.4]], converted) gives $\int|\Psi(x, t)|^2dx = \int|\phi(k)|^2dk = \int|\Psi(x, 0)|^2dx = 1$, in agreement with Theorem §B1.1.3.

## The momentum distribution

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.4.3: Momentum Moments from the Momentum Amplitude
> Let $\Psi(x, t)$ be normalized, smooth and rapidly decreasing in $x$, with momentum amplitude $\phi(k, t)$. Then $\int|\phi(k, t)|^2dk = 1$, and the expectation values of powers of the momentum given by the operator rule ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^pr-b1-2-2|Principle §B1.2.2]]) are the moments of the distribution $|\phi(k, t)|^2dk$ of $p = \hbar k$:
>
> $$
> \langle p^n\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi^*\Bigl(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Bigr)^n\Psi\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\hbar k)^n\,|\phi(k, t)|^2\,dk, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots ,
> $$
>
> and so $\langle f(p)\rangle = \int f(\hbar k)\,|\phi|^2dk$ for every polynomial $f$. For a **free** particle $|\phi(k, t)|^2 = |\phi(k)|^2$ does not change: the whole momentum distribution is conserved, not only its mean. That $|\phi|^2dk$ *is* the probability distribution of the measured momentum, in the variable $p$, is [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-6|Theorem §B2.3.6]].
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 8, §1–2, eqs. (1.9), (1.13), (2.22)–(2.27), (2.32) (in the variable $p$) · Griffiths §2.4 (the $\phi(k)$ of eq. (2.104)) · Fleisch §4.5*

^thm-b1-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the derivative rule ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|WO Theorem §B4.4.2]], with $x, k$ for $t, \omega$), the transform of $\partial_x\Psi$ is $ik\,\phi$, so the transform of $\hat p^n\Psi = (-i\hbar\,\partial_x)^n\Psi$ is $(-i\hbar\cdot ik)^n\phi = (\hbar k)^n\phi$. The polarized Plancherel identity ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|WO Theorem §B4.4.4]]), in Griffiths' convention $\int f\,g^*\,dx = \int\phi_f\,\phi_g^*\,dk$, with $f = \hat p^n\Psi$ and $g = \Psi$, gives
>
> $$
> \int\Psi^*\,\hat p^n\Psi\,dx = \int(\hbar k)^n\,\phi\,\phi^*\,dk = \int(\hbar k)^n|\phi|^2\,dk .
> $$
>
> $n = 0$ is the normalization. Linearity gives polynomials. For the free particle, $|\phi(k)e^{-i\hbar k^2t/2m}| = |\phi(k)|$ by [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-2|Theorem §B1.4.2]].

^der-b1-4-3

*Uses:* [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^def-b1-4-1|Def. §B1.4.1]], [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^pr-b1-2-2|Principle §B1.2.2]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|WO Theorem §B4.4.2]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|WO Theorem §B4.4.4]], [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-2|Theorem §B1.4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The momentum distribution, from rule to theorem
> Theorem §B1.4.3 is an identity of Fourier analysis: the operator rule for functions of $p$ and the moments of $|\phi(k)|^2$ agree. It makes two readings consistent, the operator rule and the statement "$|\phi(k)|^2dk$ is the probability that a momentum measurement gives a value in $[\hbar k, \hbar(k + dk)]$". Either implies the other (a distribution is fixed by its moments in all cases met here), so one of them must be assumed. Zwiebach postulates the density and derives the rule; Griffiths assumes the rule ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^rem-b1-2-1|Remark: One assumption, three ways to make it]]). Level B postulates the density as part of the generalized statistical interpretation: the momentum-space wave function $\Phi(p, t) = \langle p|\Psi\rangle = \phi(p/\hbar, t)/\sqrt\hbar$ ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]]) has $|\Phi|^2dp = |\phi|^2dk$, and its reading as the momentum probability is [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-6|Theorem §B2.3.6]], the home of the momentum density, which uses the present theorem for its averages. This delivers what level A promised for "Chapter B1" ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^rem-a3-4-6|Remark: Why this rule, and why its layer]]): the expectation-value rule for $p$ and $p^2$ is a theorem once the density is postulated. How the layers change, and what becomes of the two facts that level A's proof of Heisenberg's relation took on trust, is collected in [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^rem-b2-3-3|Remark: Level A's rules as special cases]] and [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^rem-b2-3-4|Remark: What this settles from level A]]. The constancy of $|\phi(k)|^2$ for a free particle extends Ehrenfest's $d\langle p\rangle/dt = 0$ ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]) to every moment; in general it is a conservation law ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-8|Theorem §B2.4.8]]).
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 8, eq. (1.13) ("we postulate") · Griffiths §1.5, §2.4*

## The Gaussian packet

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.4.4: The Free Gaussian Packet
> Let a free particle start in the Gaussian packet
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, 0) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}\,e^{-(x - x_0)^2/4\sigma^2}\,e^{ik_0x}
> $$
>
> (Example §B1.1.1: $\langle x\rangle = x_0$, $\sigma_x = \sigma$). Put $v_0 = \hbar k_0/m$, $\omega_0 = \hbar k_0^2/2m$ and $\tau = 2m\sigma^2/\hbar$. Then:
> 1. $\phi(k) = (2\sigma^2/\pi)^{1/4}\,e^{-\sigma^2(k - k_0)^2}\,e^{-i(k - k_0)x_0}$, so $|\phi|^2$ is a Gaussian in $k$ with mean $k_0$ and standard deviation $1/2\sigma$: $\langle p\rangle = \hbar k_0$, $\sigma_p = \hbar/2\sigma$, both constant in time;
> 2. at time $t$,
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, t) = \frac{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}}{\sqrt{1 + it/\tau}}\,\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(x - x_0 - v_0t)^2}{4\sigma^2(1 + it/\tau)}\Bigr]\,e^{i(k_0x - \omega_0t)}
> $$
>
> (principal square root), and $|\Psi(x, t)|^2$ is the normal distribution with mean $\langle x\rangle = x_0 + v_0t$ and standard deviation
>
> $$
> \sigma_x(t) = \sigma\sqrt{1 + \frac{t^2}{\tau^2}} ;
> $$
>
> 3. $\sigma_x(t)\,\sigma_p = \dfrac\hbar2\sqrt{1 + t^2/\tau^2}$, which equals $\hbar/2$ only at $t = 0$;
> 4. the probability current is $J = \rho\,\bigl[v_0 + (x - \langle x\rangle)\,t/(t^2 + \tau^2)\bigr]$.
>
> The packet moves at the group velocity $v_0$, hardly changes for $t \ll \tau$, and for $t \gg \tau$ widens linearly, $\sigma_x \approx (\sigma_p/m)\,t$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 2.21, eq. (2.111) (case $x_0 = k_0 = 0$, with $a = 1/4\sigma^2$) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 7, §3, eqs. (3.4)–(3.8) · Greensite ch. 5, §5.5, eqs. (5.57)–(5.71) · Fleisch §4.5, eqs. (4.41)–(4.46) · PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 6 (the initial packet)*

^thm-b1-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Momentum amplitude.* By the Gaussian, shift and modulation rules ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|WO Theorem §B4.4.2]]) with WO's width $s = \sqrt2\,\sigma$ and the conversion of [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^cau-b1-4-1|Caution: Fourier conventions in QM and in WO]], $e^{-u^2/4\sigma^2} \mapsto \sqrt2\,\sigma\,e^{-\sigma^2k^2}$; shifting by $x_0$ multiplies by $e^{-ikx_0}$, and the factor $e^{ik_0x}$ replaces $k$ by $k - k_0$. With the prefactor, $(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}\sqrt2\,\sigma = (2\sigma^2/\pi)^{1/4}$. $|\phi|^2 \propto e^{-2\sigma^2(k - k_0)^2} = e^{-(k - k_0)^2/2(1/2\sigma)^2}$, and [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-3|Theorem §B1.4.3]] turns its mean and spread into $\langle p\rangle$ and $\sigma_p$, constant in time.
>
> *Evolution.* For matter waves $\omega(k) = \hbar k^2/2m$ is exactly quadratic: with $q = k - k_0$, $\omega = \omega_0 + v_0q + \tfrac12(\hbar/m)q^2$ with no remainder. So the packet is WO's [[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^thm-b6-2-4|WO Theorem §B6.2.4]] read in the quantum convention: complex-conjugate it, put WO's amplitude width $\sigma_{\text{WO}} = \sqrt2\,\sigma$, its dispersion $\Gamma = \omega''(k_0) = \hbar/m$, its carrier $k_c = k_0$ and group velocity $v_g = v_0$, and multiply by the normalization $(2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}$ (the equation is linear). WO's $\sigma_{\text{WO}}^2 - i\Gamma t$, conjugated, becomes $2\sigma^2 + i\hbar t/m = 2\sigma^2(1 + it/\tau)$, which gives item 2. WO's theorem assumes a dispersion relation that is exactly quadratic, which for water or light in glass is an approximation (higher terms in $q$ are dropped); for matter waves it is exact, so the formula holds for all $t$, however wide the band of $k$. (Directly: inserting $\phi$ into [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-2|Theorem §B1.4.2]] leaves the Gaussian integral $\int e^{-\sigma^2(1 + it/\tau)q^2 + iq(x - x_0 - v_0t)}dq$, evaluated by the lemma in the derivation of WO Theorem §B6.2.4, or by completing the square as Griffiths suggests.)
>
> *Density.* $|1 + it/\tau| = \sqrt{1 + t^2/\tau^2}$ and $\operatorname{Re}\,[1/(1 + it/\tau)] = 1/(1 + t^2/\tau^2)$, so with $X = x - x_0 - v_0t$,
>
> $$
> |\Psi|^2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2(1 + t^2/\tau^2)}}\,\exp\Bigl[-\frac{X^2}{2\sigma^2(1 + t^2/\tau^2)}\Bigr] ,
> $$
>
> the normal distribution of item 2. Item 3 follows by multiplying.
>
> *Current.* The phase of $\Psi$ is $S/\hbar = k_0x - \omega_0t + \operatorname{Im}\bigl[-X^2/4\sigma^2(1 + it/\tau)\bigr] + \text{(terms without } x) = k_0x + \dfrac{X^2\,t/\tau}{4\sigma^2(1 + t^2/\tau^2)} + \cdots$, so $\partial_xS/m = v_0 + \dfrac{\hbar}{2m\sigma^2}\,\dfrac{X\,t/\tau}{1 + t^2/\tau^2} = v_0 + \dfrac{X\,t}{\tau^2 + t^2}$, using $\hbar/2m\sigma^2 = 1/\tau$; [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]] gives item 4.

> [!remark] Remark: What quantum mechanics adds to the classical spreading
> - **The rate is set by $\hbar/m$.** WO's dispersion $\Gamma = \omega''$ is here $\hbar/m$, so the spreading time $\tau = 2m\sigma^2/\hbar$ grows with the mass and with the square of the initial width: a narrow packet spreads fast.
> - **Nothing classical forces a spread of velocities; quantum mechanics does.** For $t \gg \tau$ the width is $(\sigma_p/m)\,t$, just what a classical swarm of free particles with velocity spread $\sigma_p/m$ would show. The quantum point is that $\sigma_p \ge \hbar/2\sigma_x$: localizing the particle at $t = 0$ guarantees the velocity spread that later delocalizes it. The Gaussian starts with the smallest possible $\sigma_x\sigma_p$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-4|Theorem §B2.4.4]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-5|Theorem §B2.4.5]]) and then moves away from the minimum, as item 3 shows.
> - **The current tells the story.** The local velocity $v_0 + (x - \langle x\rangle)t/(t^2 + \tau^2)$ is largest at the front: the faster components gather ahead and the slower behind, and for $t \gg \tau$ it tends to $(x - x_0)/t$, the velocity a classical particle released at $x_0$ needs to be at $x$ at time $t$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^ex-b1-1-1|Example §B1.1.1]] and its figure).
> - **The envelope moves at the group velocity, the crests at half of it** ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-4|Theorem §A3.3.4]]; Griffiths eqs. (2.106)–(2.110)). For every free packet, not only Gaussians and not only narrow ones, $\langle x\rangle = \langle x\rangle_0 + \langle p\rangle t/m$ exactly ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^ex-b1-2-1|Example §B1.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 7, §2, eqs. (2.19)–(2.27) · Greensite ch. 5, §5.5–5.6 · Griffiths §2.4*

^rem-b1-4-2

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: A moving packet is a boosted packet at rest
> Item 2 of Theorem §B1.4.4 has the form $\Psi_{k_0}(x, t) = e^{i(k_0x - \omega_0t)}\,\Psi_0(x - v_0t, t)$, where $\Psi_0$ is the packet with $k_0 = 0$: the moving packet is the one at rest seen from a frame moving at $-v_0$, multiplied by a phase. In general, if $\Psi$ solves the free equation, so does $e^{i(mvx - \frac12mv^2t)/\hbar}\,\Psi(x - vt, t)$, as substitution shows. The density simply translates, but the phase, and with it the local wavelength, changes: Zwiebach's observation that observers in different Galilean frames assign different de Broglie wavelengths $h/(p - mv)$ to the same particle, whereas the wavelength of a water or sound wave is the same for all of them. A wave whose wavelength depends on the observer cannot be a directly measurable field; only $|\Psi|^2$ and phase differences are observable ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^rem-a3-4-2|Remark: What is observable and what is not]]).
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 4, §1, eqs. (1.3)–(1.13); the transformation is checked here*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.4.1: A Particle Released from a Slot
> A free particle is confined to $-a < x < a$ and released at $t = 0$: $\Psi(x, 0) = A$ there and $0$ outside.
> - *Normalization.* $2a|A|^2 = 1$, $A = 1/\sqrt{2a}$; $\langle x\rangle = 0$, $\sigma_x = a/\sqrt3$.
> - *Momentum amplitude.*
>
> $$
> \phi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2a}}\int_{-a}^{a}e^{-ikx}\,dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi a}}\,\frac{\sin ka}{k} ,
> $$
>
> real and even because $\Psi(x, 0)$ is. Its main lobe lies between the zeros $k = \pm\pi/a$: a narrow slot (small $a$) gives a broad $\phi$, and conversely. Measured by full widths, slot $2a$ and main lobe $2\pi/a$, $\Delta x\,\Delta k = 4\pi$ whatever $a$ is: the reciprocal spreading of [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]] (the standard deviation $\sigma_k$ is infinite here, see below). Check: $\int|\phi|^2dk = \frac{1}{\pi a}\int\frac{\sin^2ka}{k^2}\,dk = \frac{1}{\pi a}\cdot\pi a = 1$, as Plancherel requires.
> - *Momentum.* $|\phi|^2$ is even, so $\langle p\rangle = 0$; but $\langle p^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{\pi a}\int\sin^2(ka)\,dk = \infty$ ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-3|Theorem §B1.4.3]]), since $|\phi|^2$ falls only like $1/k^2$. The kinetic energy is infinite, as [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]] predicts for a wave function that jumps; $\sigma_x\sigma_p = \infty$. A real slot has soft edges; the sharp one is an idealization that only the high-$k$ tail notices.
> - *Evolution.* By [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-2|Theorem §B1.4.2]], $\Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{2a}}\int\frac{\sin ka}{k}\,e^{i(kx - \hbar k^2t/2m)}dk$, an integral that has no elementary closed form (Griffiths eq. (2.105)) and is done numerically: within a time of order $ma^2/\hbar$ the flat top and sharp edges round off into a central bump, wider than the slot, with small side ripples. The roles of $x$ and $k$ can be exchanged: a flat $\phi(k)$ of width $\Delta k$ gives the sinc packet $\Psi(x, 0) \propto \sin(\Delta k\,x/2)/x$, with $\Delta x \approx 2\pi/\Delta k$ (Zwiebach, Fleisch).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 2.6, eqs. (2.104)–(2.105) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 7, §1, eqs. (1.16)–(1.17) · Fleisch §4.4, eqs. (4.22)–(4.26)*

^ex-b1-4-1

> [!example] Example §B1.4.2: How Fast Do Packets Spread?
> By [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]] a Gaussian packet keeps its shape for $t \ll \tau = 2m\sigma^2/\hbar$, doubles its width at $t = \sqrt3\,\tau$, and later grows at the rate $\sigma_p/m = \hbar/2m\sigma$.
> - *An electron localized to an atom*, $\sigma = 1.0\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm m$: $\tau = 2(9.11\times10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg})(1.0\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm{m^2})/(1.055\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}) = 1.7\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm s$, and the width then grows at $\hbar/2m\sigma = 5.8\times10^5\ \mathrm{m/s}$. A $10\ \mathrm{eV}$ electron ($v = \sqrt{2E/m} = 1.9\times10^6\ \mathrm{m/s}$) crosses $1\ \mathrm m$ in $5.3\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm s$, by which time $\sigma_x \approx 0.31\ \mathrm m$: the packet is almost as wide as the distance travelled. Zwiebach's estimate $t \approx m(\Delta x)^2/\hbar \approx 10^{-16}\ \mathrm s$ for $\Delta x = 10^{-10}\ \mathrm m$ is the same order.
> - *A baseball*, $m = 0.25\ \mathrm{kg}$, localized just as sharply: $\tau = 2(0.25)(1.0\times10^{-20})/(1.055\times10^{-34})\ \mathrm s = 4.7\times10^{13}\ \mathrm s$, about $1.5$ million years before the width has grown noticeably.
>
> The factor is $m$: $1.8\times10^{-30}\ \mathrm{kg}$ against $0.5\ \mathrm{kg}$ in $2m\sigma^2$. Macroscopic wave functions stay put; an electron's spreads at once, which is one reason the electron cannot be pictured as a classical wave of matter. That electron tracks in a detector are nevertheless sharp is the work of repeated position measurements, each collapsing the packet ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^rem-b1-1-1|Remark: Collapse, and the Born rule at level B]]).
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 7, §2, eq. (2.28) · Greensite ch. 5, §5.5, eqs. (5.78)–(5.86) (Greensite's width parameter is $\sqrt2\,\sigma$; numbers recomputed here)*
