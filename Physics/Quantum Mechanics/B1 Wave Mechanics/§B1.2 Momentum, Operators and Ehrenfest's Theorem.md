Level A took the momentum operator $\hat p = -i\hbar\,\partial/\partial x$ and the "sandwich" rule $\langle p\rangle = \int\Psi^*\hat p\,\Psi\,dx$ as given ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-5|Def. §A3.4.5]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]]), and quoted $\langle p\rangle = m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt$ without proof ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-5|Theorem §A4.3.5]]). This section derives from the Schrödinger equation how fast the mean position moves, states the operator rule for every dynamical variable as the principle it is, and proves Ehrenfest's theorem: the mean momentum changes at the rate of the mean force. Expectation values obey Newton's second law in the form $m\,d^2\langle x\rangle/dt^2 = \langle F\rangle$, and the difference between $\langle F(x)\rangle$ and $F(\langle x\rangle)$ measures how far quantum motion is from classical. Potential energy is $V$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^cau-b1-1-1|Caution: V, not U]]).

## How fast the mean position moves

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.1: Velocity of the Mean Position
> Let $\Psi$ solve the Schrödinger equation with real $V$, with $\Psi$ normalized and $x\,J(x, t) \to 0$, $\Psi \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} J(x, t)\,dx = -\frac{i\hbar}{m}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi^*\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\,dx .
> $$
>
> The mean position moves with the total probability current.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5, eqs. (1.28)–(1.31)*

^thm-b1-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\langle x\rangle = \int x\,\rho\,dx$, and $x$ is only the variable of integration, so all the time dependence sits in $\rho$. With the continuity equation ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]) and an integration by parts ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]),
>
> $$
> \frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \int x\,\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\,dx = -\int x\,\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}\,dx = -\bigl[xJ\bigr]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int J\,dx = \int J\,dx .
> $$
>
> Now $J = \tfrac{i\hbar}{2m}(\Psi\,\partial_x\Psi^* - \Psi^*\partial_x\Psi)$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]]), and one more integration by parts moves the derivative in the first term: $\int\Psi\,\partial_x\Psi^*\,dx = \bigl[\Psi\Psi^*\bigr]_{-\infty}^{\infty} - \int\Psi^*\,\partial_x\Psi\,dx = -\int\Psi^*\,\partial_x\Psi\,dx$. Hence $\int J\,dx = \tfrac{i\hbar}{2m}\bigl(-2\int\Psi^*\partial_x\Psi\,dx\bigr)$, which is Griffiths' eq. (1.31).

^der-b1-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: x under the integral is not the particle
> It is tempting to pull the time derivative onto $x$ in $\int x\,|\Psi|^2dx$, note $\partial x/\partial t = 0$, and conclude $d\langle x\rangle/dt = 0$. But $x$ is a dummy variable labelling points of the line, not the position of the particle; the motion is in $|\Psi(x, t)|^2$. Theorem §B1.2.1 is also not the velocity of the particle: a particle without a definite position has no definite velocity either, and what moves is the average over an ensemble of identically prepared particles ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^rem-a3-4-5|Remark: An average, not a prediction for one measurement]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5; Problem 1.6*

## Operators

> [!definition] Definition §B1.2.1: Operator; Position, Momentum and Hamiltonian Operators
> An **operator** $\hat Q$ is an instruction that turns a function into another function. It is **linear** if $\hat Q(a\varphi + b\chi) = a\hat Q\varphi + b\hat Q\chi$ for all functions $\varphi, \chi$ and complex numbers $a, b$; all operators of quantum mechanics are linear. Operators add pointwise, $(\hat A + \hat B)\varphi = \hat A\varphi + \hat B\varphi$, and multiply by composition, $\hat A\hat B\varphi = \hat A(\hat B\varphi)$, the operator nearest the function acting first; in general $\hat A\hat B \neq \hat B\hat A$. In one dimension:
> - **position** $\hat x$ multiplies by $x$, and any function of it, such as the potential energy $V(\hat x)$, multiplies by that function: $V(\hat x)\varphi = V(x)\varphi(x)$;
> - **momentum** $\hat p = -i\hbar\,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}$ (in three dimensions $\hat{\vec p} = -i\hbar\nabla$);
> - **kinetic energy** $\hat T = \hat p^2/2m = -\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}$, and the **Hamiltonian**, obtained from the classical $H(x, p) = p^2/2m + V(x)$ by the substitution $p \to \hat p$,
>
> $$
> \hat H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x) .
> $$
>
> With it the Schrödinger equation is $i\hbar\,\partial\Psi/\partial t = \hat H\Psi$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5, eqs. (1.34)–(1.35) and footnote 18; §2.1, eqs. (2.10)–(2.11) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 5, eqs. (1.4), (2.4)–(2.9), (2.21) · the user's Classical notes, §8*

^def-b1-2-1

> [!principle] Principle §B1.2.2: The Operator Rule for Expectation Values
> Every dynamical variable $Q(x, p)$ of the particle is represented by the operator obtained by replacing $p$ with $\hat p$, and its expectation value in the normalized state $\Psi$ is
>
> $$
> \langle Q(x, p)\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi^*\,\Bigl[Q\Bigl(x, -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Bigr)\Bigr]\Psi\,dx .
> $$
>
> The operator acts on $\Psi$; the result is multiplied by $\Psi^*$ and integrated. Examples: $\langle x\rangle = \int\Psi^*x\Psi\,dx$, $\langle p\rangle = \int\Psi^*(-i\hbar\,\partial_x\Psi)\,dx$, $\langle T\rangle = -\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int\Psi^*\partial_x^2\Psi\,dx$, $\langle H\rangle = \int\Psi^*\hat H\Psi\,dx$.
>
> *Domain: one non-relativistic particle without spin, in one dimension (in three, $\hat{\vec p} = -i\hbar\nabla$); variables built from $x$ and $p$ without products in which the order of $x$ and $p$ would matter (for such products see [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-2|Principle §B2.3.2]]).*
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5, eqs. (1.33)–(1.37) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 8, eq. (2.28) · the user's Classical notes, §8.2*

^pr-b1-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: One assumption, three ways to make it
> The rule generalizes level A's [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]], which covered $p$, $p^2$ and the energy. It is not a consequence of the Born rule, which speaks only about position; some further assumption about momentum is needed, and the sources choose different ones.
> - *Griffiths* postulates that $\langle v\rangle = d\langle x\rangle/dt$ (his eq. (1.32)). Theorem §B1.2.1 then shows that $m\langle v\rangle$ is computed by sandwiching $-i\hbar\,\partial_x$, and the rule for every $Q(x, p)$ is offered as a generalization, which "if you prefer to think of it as an axiom, that's fine."
> - *Greensite* postulates **Ehrenfest's principle**: Hamilton's equations ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-2|CM Theorem §B7.1.2]]) hold for expectation values, $d\langle x\rangle/dt = \langle p/m\rangle$ and $d\langle p\rangle/dt = \langle -\partial V/\partial x\rangle$. The first equation, combined with the Schrödinger equation, yields $\hat p$; the second is then a check, Theorem §B1.2.5 below.
> - *Zwiebach* postulates that $|\Phi(p)|^2$, built from the Fourier transform of $\Psi$, is the probability density of momentum, and derives the sandwich for $\langle p\rangle$ (Lecture 8, §2); this vault does so in [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-3|Theorem §B1.4.3]].
>
> In Chapter B2 the rule becomes the observable postulate ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-2|Principle §B2.3.2]]), and the sandwich for averages a theorem of the generalized statistical interpretation ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-5|Theorem §B2.3.5]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^rem-b2-3-3|Remark: Level A's rules as special cases]]); Greensite's route, which derives the position-space form of $\hat p$ from the canonical commutator $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$, is [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^rem-b2-4-1|Remark: From Poisson brackets to commutators]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5 · Greensite ch. 5, §5.1–5.2, eqs. (5.2)–(5.3), (5.16)–(5.17) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 8, §1–2*

^rem-b1-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Ĥ is the energy operator, not iħ∂/∂t
> Level A listed $\hat E = i\hbar\,\partial/\partial t$ among the operators ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-5|Def. §A3.4.5]]). At level B the energy is represented by $\hat H$, which acts on $\Psi(x, t)$ at a single time. $i\hbar\,\partial_t$ is not an operator on the state at one instant (it needs $\Psi$ at neighbouring times), and $i\hbar\,\partial_t\Psi = \hat H\Psi$ is not an identity between operators but the equation of motion, true only on solutions. On solutions the two give the same $\langle E\rangle$, so the level-A results stand; but $\langle H\rangle$ can be computed from $\Psi$ at one time alone, without solving anything (as in [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^ex-b1-3-1|Example §B1.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.1, eqs. (2.11)–(2.13) (energy as $\hat H$); Problem 2.9; the comparison with level A's $\hat E$ is made here*

^cau-b1-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.3: Mean Momentum Is Mass Times the Velocity of the Mean Position
> Under the conditions of [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]],
>
> $$
> \langle p\rangle = m\,\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = m\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}J\,dx = -i\hbar\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi^*\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\,dx ,
> $$
>
> and $\langle p\rangle$ is real.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5, eq. (1.33) · Greensite ch. 5, eqs. (5.5)–(5.16)*

^thm-b1-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^pr-b1-2-2|Principle §B1.2.2]] with $Q = p$ gives the last expression for $\langle p\rangle$; multiplying [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]] by $m$ gives the same expression for $m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt$. $J$ is real, so $\langle p\rangle$ is.

^der-b1-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^pr-b1-2-2|Principle §B1.2.2]], [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.4: Kinetic Energy Is Positive
> For a normalized $\Psi$ that is continuous, with $\partial\Psi/\partial x$ piecewise continuous and $\Psi\,\partial_x\Psi^* \to 0$ at $\pm\infty$,
>
> $$
> \langle p^2\rangle = \hbar^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Bigl|\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\Bigr|^2dx, \qquad \langle T\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Bigl|\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\Bigr|^2dx > 0 .
> $$
>
> These forms are manifestly positive and are the safe ones when $\Psi$ has kinks; if $\Psi$ itself jumps, $\langle T\rangle = \infty$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5, eq. (1.37) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 8, eqs. (2.29)–(2.31)*

^thm-b1-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Integrate $\langle T\rangle = -\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int\Psi^*\partial_x^2\Psi\,dx$ by parts once ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]): $-\int\Psi^*\Psi''\,dx = -\bigl[\Psi^*\Psi'\bigr]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int\Psi^{*\prime}\Psi'\,dx = \int|\Psi'|^2\,dx$. The integral is $\ge 0$, and it vanishes only if $\Psi' = 0$ everywhere, so that $\Psi$ is constant; a normalizable constant is $0$, which is not a state. Hence $\langle T\rangle > 0$. *Kinks.* If $\Psi'$ jumps by $\Delta$ at $x_j$, then $\Psi''$ contains $\Delta\,\delta(x - x_j)$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]], part 3), which the form $\int\Psi^*\Psi''$ must include and the form $\int|\Psi'|^2$ handles automatically. If $\Psi$ itself jumps, $\Psi'$ contains a $\delta$, whose square is not integrable: the kinetic energy is infinite.

## Ehrenfest's theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.5: Ehrenfest's Theorem
> Let $\Psi$ solve the Schrödinger equation with a real potential energy $V(x, t)$, and let $\Psi$, $\partial\Psi/\partial x$ and $V|\Psi|^2$ tend to zero as $x \to \pm\infty$. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = \Bigl\langle -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\Bigr\rangle, \qquad\text{and with Theorem §B1.2.3}\qquad m\,\frac{d^2\langle x\rangle}{dt^2} = \langle F(x, t)\rangle, \quad F = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x} .
> $$
>
> The mean values obey Newton's second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]) with the force averaged over the probability distribution, $\langle F\rangle = \int F(x, t)\,|\Psi|^2\,dx$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5, Problem 1.7, eq. (1.38) · Greensite ch. 5, eqs. (5.3), (5.15)*

^thm-b1-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]], $\langle p\rangle = -i\hbar\int\Psi^*\partial_x\Psi\,dx$. Differentiating, and integrating the term with $\partial_x\partial_t\Psi$ by parts ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]; the boundary term $\Psi^*\partial_t\Psi$ vanishes),
>
> $$
> \frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = -i\hbar\int\Bigl(\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} + \Psi^*\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x\,\partial t}\Bigr)dx = -i\hbar\int\Bigl(\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}\Bigr)dx .
> $$
>
> Insert $\partial_t\Psi = \tfrac{i\hbar}{2m}\Psi'' - \tfrac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi$ and its conjugate (primes are $x$-derivatives; $V$ real):
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t}\Psi' - \Psi^{*\prime}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\bigl(\Psi^{*\prime\prime}\Psi' + \Psi^{*\prime}\Psi''\bigr) + \frac{i}{\hbar}V\bigl(\Psi^*\Psi' + \Psi^{*\prime}\Psi\bigr) = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigl|\Psi'\bigr|^2 + \frac{i}{\hbar}V\frac{\partial|\Psi|^2}{\partial x} .
> $$
>
> The first term integrates to $\bigl[|\Psi'|^2\bigr]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = 0$. The second, integrated by parts with $V|\Psi|^2 \to 0$, gives $-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\int\partial_xV\,|\Psi|^2dx$. Therefore
>
> $$
> \frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = -i\hbar\cdot\Bigl(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\Bigr)\int\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}|\Psi|^2\,dx = -\int\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\,|\Psi|^2\,dx = \Bigl\langle -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\Bigr\rangle .
> $$

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]], [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^pr-b1-2-2|Principle §B1.2.2]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: When do mean values move classically?
> Ehrenfest's theorem says that $\langle x\rangle$ obeys $m\,d^2\langle x\rangle/dt^2 = \langle F(x)\rangle$, not $F(\langle x\rangle)$. The two agree for all states exactly when $F$ is a linear function of $x$, that is, when $V$ is at most quadratic: free particle, uniform force, harmonic oscillator ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^ex-b1-2-1|Example §B1.2.1]]). Otherwise expand about the mean (Taylor, [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]):
>
> $$
> \langle F(x)\rangle = F(\langle x\rangle) + \tfrac12F''(\langle x\rangle)\,\sigma_x^2 + \tfrac16F'''(\langle x\rangle)\,\bigl\langle(x - \langle x\rangle)^3\bigr\rangle + \cdots .
> $$
>
> The mean follows a classical trajectory as long as the packet is narrow compared with the distance over which the force changes appreciably, which is the classical limit for a macroscopic body, and as long as it stays narrow, which free spreading eventually spoils ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]]). Even then only the mean is classical; the individual measurement results scatter about it with spread $\sigma_x$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §1.5 (footnote 19: some authors reserve the name for the pair of equations) · Greensite ch. 5, §5.1*

^rem-b1-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: The general rate of change, and Hamilton's equations
> Theorems §B1.2.1 and §B1.2.5 are the cases $Q = x$ and $Q = p$ of the general formula
>
> $$
> \frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\bigl\langle[\hat H, \hat Q]\bigr\rangle + \Bigl\langle\frac{\partial\hat Q}{\partial t}\Bigr\rangle, \qquad [\hat A, \hat B] = \hat A\hat B - \hat B\hat A ,
> $$
>
> proved in [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]] (Griffiths' generalized Ehrenfest theorem; PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 4; Zwiebach Lecture 8, eq. (3.37)), with $[\hat H, \hat x] = -i\hbar\hat p/m$ and $[\hat H, \hat p] = i\hbar\,\partial V/\partial x$. Written as $d\langle x\rangle/dt = \langle\partial H/\partial p\rangle$, $d\langle p\rangle/dt = \langle-\partial H/\partial x\rangle$, the pair is Hamilton's equations ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-2|CM Theorem §B7.1.2]]) averaged over the state, and the commutator plays the part of the Poisson bracket in $df/dt = \{f, H\} + \partial f/\partial t$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|CM Theorem §B8.1.3]]).
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 8, §3 · PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 4 · Greensite ch. 5, eq. (5.2)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.2.1: Potentials in Which the Means Move Exactly Classically
> For $V$ at most quadratic in $x$, [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]] and [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]] form a closed pair of equations for $\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle p\rangle$, whatever the state.
> - *Free particle*, $V = 0$: $\langle p\rangle$ is constant and $\langle x\rangle(t) = \langle x\rangle_0 + \langle p\rangle_0t/m$.
> - *Uniform force*, $V = -F_0x$: $\langle p\rangle = \langle p\rangle_0 + F_0t$ and $\langle x\rangle = \langle x\rangle_0 + \langle p\rangle_0t/m + F_0t^2/2m$, the classical motion under constant force.
> - *Oscillator*, $V = \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$: $d\langle x\rangle/dt = \langle p\rangle/m$ and $d\langle p\rangle/dt = -m\omega^2\langle x\rangle$, so $d^2\langle x\rangle/dt^2 = -\omega^2\langle x\rangle$ and
>
> $$
> \langle x\rangle(t) = \langle x\rangle_0\cos\omega t + \frac{\langle p\rangle_0}{m\omega}\sin\omega t, \qquad \langle p\rangle(t) = \langle p\rangle_0\cos\omega t - m\omega\langle x\rangle_0\sin\omega t ,
> $$
>
> exactly the classical oscillation, at the classical frequency, for every state. This proves, for the means, what level A saw in one superposition ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^ex-a4-5-2|Example §A4.5.2]]) and inferred from the spacing of the levels ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^rem-a4-5-6|Remark: Superpositions oscillate at the classical frequency]]), without solving for a single stationary state.
> - *A quartic well*, $V = \lambda x^4$: $d\langle p\rangle/dt = -4\lambda\langle x^3\rangle$ and $\langle x^3\rangle = \langle x\rangle^3 + 3\langle x\rangle\sigma_x^2 + \langle(x - \langle x\rangle)^3\rangle$. For a packet that is not skewed, the mean feels a stronger restoring force than a classical particle at $\langle x\rangle$ would (the term $3\langle x\rangle\sigma_x^2$ has the sign of $\langle x\rangle$), and the equations no longer close: $d\langle p\rangle/dt$ depends on $\sigma_x(t)$, whose evolution involves higher moments, and so on.
>
> *Source: Griffiths eq. (1.38) applied (Griffiths §1.5; Problem 3.19 for the free particle and the oscillator; Greensite ch. 5, §5.5 for the free particle); worked here*

^ex-b1-2-1

> [!example] Example §B1.2.2: A Parabolic Wave Function
> At $t = 0$ a particle has $\Psi(x, 0) = A(a^2 - x^2)$ for $|x| \le a$ and $0$ otherwise ($A, a > 0$).
> - *Normalization.* $\int_{-a}^{a}(a^2 - x^2)^2dx = 2a^5\bigl(1 - \tfrac23 + \tfrac15\bigr) = \tfrac{16}{15}a^5$, so $A = \sqrt{15/16a^5}$.
> - *Position.* $|\Psi|^2$ is even, so $\langle x\rangle = 0$; $\langle x^2\rangle = A^2\int_{-a}^{a}x^2(a^2 - x^2)^2dx = A^2\cdot 2a^7\bigl(\tfrac13 - \tfrac25 + \tfrac17\bigr) = A^2\,\tfrac{16}{105}a^7 = a^2/7$, and $\sigma_x = a/\sqrt7$.
> - *Momentum.* $\langle p\rangle$ cannot be found from $m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt$: we know $\langle x\rangle$ only at one instant, and its rate of change needs the state at neighbouring times (or Theorem §B1.2.1, which here gives $\int J\,dx = 0$). The operator rule does it directly: $\Psi$ is real, so $\langle p\rangle = 0$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-5|Theorem §A3.4.5]]).
> - *Kinetic energy.* $\Psi$ has kinks at $x = \pm a$, where $\Psi'$ jumps from $\mp 2aA$ to $0$. By [[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]],
>
> $$
> \langle p^2\rangle = \hbar^2\int_{-a}^{a}|\Psi'|^2dx = \hbar^2A^2\int_{-a}^{a}4x^2\,dx = \hbar^2A^2\,\frac{8a^3}{3} = \frac{5\hbar^2}{2a^2}, \qquad \sigma_p = \sqrt{\frac52}\,\frac{\hbar}{a} .
> $$
>
> The form $-\hbar^2\int\Psi^*\Psi''\,dx$ gives the same here only because the delta functions in $\Psi''$ at $\pm a$ are multiplied by $\Psi(\pm a) = 0$.
