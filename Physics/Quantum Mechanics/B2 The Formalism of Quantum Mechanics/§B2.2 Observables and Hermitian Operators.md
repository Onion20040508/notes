Level A computed averages with operators, $\langle Q\rangle = \int\Psi^*\hat Q\Psi\,dx$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-5|Def. §A3.4.5]], [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]]), found the stationary states of each potential to be orthonormal by doing the integrals ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-3|Theorem §A4.2.3]]), and quoted their completeness ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^rem-a4-3-1|Remark: Every state is such a sum]]). This section asks which operators can stand for measurable quantities. Requiring real averages singles out the Hermitian operators; their eigenvalues are real and their eigenfunctions orthogonal, once and for all, for every observable; and a state with a sharp value of an observable is an eigenfunction of its operator. Completeness of the eigenfunctions cannot be proved in general and is postulated. Operators like position and momentum have continuous spectra, whose eigenfunctions lie outside Hilbert space; they are handled with Dirac orthonormality, whose rigorous meaning is in Functional Analysis ([[§24 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions|556 §24]]).

## Hermitian operators

> [!definition] Definition §B2.2.1: Hermitian Conjugate; Hermitian Operator
> The **Hermitian conjugate** (or **adjoint**) of an operator $\hat Q$ is the operator $\hat Q^\dagger$ with
>
> $$
> \langle f|\hat Qg\rangle = \langle\hat Q^\dagger f|g\rangle \qquad\text{for all } f, g .
> $$
>
> $\hat Q$ is **Hermitian** if $\hat Q^\dagger = \hat Q$, that is,
>
> $$
> \langle f|\hat Qg\rangle = \langle\hat Qf|g\rangle \qquad\text{for all } f, g\ \text{(in its domain)}:
> $$
>
> a Hermitian operator can be applied to either member of an inner product with the same result. In an orthonormal basis, the matrix of $\hat Q^\dagger$ is the conjugate transpose of the matrix of $\hat Q$, $(Q^\dagger)_{mn} = Q_{nm}^*$, so a Hermitian operator has a Hermitian matrix: real diagonal entries, and $Q_{nm} = Q_{mn}^*$. An operator with $\hat Q^\dagger = -\hat Q$ is **anti-Hermitian**. In Dirac notation, $\langle\hat Q^\dagger f|g\rangle = \langle f|\hat Q|g\rangle$, and the bra of $\hat Q|\psi\rangle$ is $\langle\psi|\hat Q^\dagger$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.2.1, eqs. (3.16)–(3.17), (3.20), Problems 3.24, 3.32; App. A.6, eq. (A.95) · Fleisch §2.3, eqs. (2.21)–(2.29) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 9, eq. (1.4)*

^def-b2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.1: Real Expectation Values and the Algebra of Adjoints
> 1. If $\hat Q$ is Hermitian, $\langle Q\rangle = \langle\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle$ is real for every state $\Psi$. Conversely, if $\langle h|\hat Qh\rangle$ is real for every $h$, then $\hat Q$ is Hermitian. Equivalently, the condition $\langle h|\hat Qh\rangle = \langle\hat Qh|h\rangle$ for all $h$ already implies $\langle f|\hat Qg\rangle = \langle\hat Qf|g\rangle$ for all $f, g$.
> 2. For operators $\hat Q, \hat R$ and a complex number $c$: $(\hat Q + \hat R)^\dagger = \hat Q^\dagger + \hat R^\dagger$, $(c\hat Q)^\dagger = c^*\hat Q^\dagger$, $(\hat Q\hat R)^\dagger = \hat R^\dagger\hat Q^\dagger$ (reversed order), $(\hat Q^\dagger)^\dagger = \hat Q$.
> 3. Hence sums of Hermitian operators are Hermitian; $c\hat Q$ (with $\hat Q \ne 0$ Hermitian) is Hermitian exactly when $c$ is real; and the product $\hat Q\hat R$ of two Hermitian operators is Hermitian exactly when they commute, $\hat Q\hat R = \hat R\hat Q$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.2.1, eqs. (3.13)–(3.17), Problems 3.3–3.5 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 9, Claim 1, eqs. (1.5)–(1.7) · Fleisch §2.3*

^thm-b2-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1, forward.* By conjugate symmetry ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]]) and Hermiticity, $\langle Q\rangle^* = \langle\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle^* = \langle\hat Q\Psi|\Psi\rangle = \langle\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle = \langle Q\rangle$. *Converse.* If $\langle h|\hat Qh\rangle$ is real, then $\langle h|\hat Qh\rangle = \langle h|\hat Qh\rangle^* = \langle\hat Qh|h\rangle$. Now polarize. With $h = f + g$, the terms $\langle f|\hat Qf\rangle = \langle\hat Qf|f\rangle$ and $\langle g|\hat Qg\rangle = \langle\hat Qg|g\rangle$ cancel from the two sides, leaving
>
> $$
> \langle f|\hat Qg\rangle + \langle g|\hat Qf\rangle = \langle\hat Qf|g\rangle + \langle\hat Qg|f\rangle .
> $$
>
> With $h = f + ig$, using $\langle ig| = -i\langle g|$, the same cancellation leaves $i\langle f|\hat Qg\rangle - i\langle g|\hat Qf\rangle = i\langle\hat Qf|g\rangle - i\langle\hat Qg|f\rangle$. Divide by $i$ and add to the first equation: $2\langle f|\hat Qg\rangle = 2\langle\hat Qf|g\rangle$. The same polarization in finite dimensions is [[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-14|LADR Thm. 7.14]].
>
> *2.* $\langle f|\hat Q\hat Rg\rangle = \langle\hat Q^\dagger f|\hat Rg\rangle = \langle\hat R^\dagger\hat Q^\dagger f|g\rangle$; $\langle f|c\hat Qg\rangle = c\langle\hat Q^\dagger f|g\rangle = \langle c^*\hat Q^\dagger f|g\rangle$ (the constant enters the first slot conjugated); $\langle f|\hat Q^\dagger g\rangle = \langle\hat Q^\dagger g|f\rangle^* = \langle g|\hat Qf\rangle^* = \langle\hat Qf|g\rangle$. Sums are immediate. These are [[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-5|LADR Thm. 7.5]].
>
> *3.* $(c\hat Q)^\dagger = c^*\hat Q$ equals $c\hat Q$ iff $c^* = c$. $(\hat Q\hat R)^\dagger = \hat R\hat Q$, which equals $\hat Q\hat R$ iff the two commute.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.2: Position, Momentum and the Hamiltonian Are Hermitian
> On square-integrable functions that vanish at $\pm\infty$ (or, on a finite interval, satisfy boundary conditions that kill the boundary terms below), the operators
>
> $$
> \hat x = x, \qquad \hat p = -i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}, \qquad \hat H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + V(x) \quad (V \text{ real})
> $$
>
> are Hermitian. The operator $d/dx$ by itself is anti-Hermitian, $(d/dx)^\dagger = -d/dx$, and does not represent an observable: the $i$ in $\hat p$ is what makes the momentum operator Hermitian.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.2.1, eq. (3.19), Problems 3.4(d), 3.5(a) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 9, eq. (1.1)*

^thm-b2-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Position and potential.* $\langle f|xg\rangle = \int f^*xg\,dx = \int(xf)^*g\,dx = \langle xf|g\rangle$ because $x$ is real; the same holds for multiplication by any real function $V(x)$.
>
> *Momentum.* Integrate by parts ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]):
>
> $$
> \langle f|\hat pg\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f^*\Bigl(-i\hbar\frac{dg}{dx}\Bigr)dx = -i\hbar\,f^*g\Big|_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Bigl(-i\hbar\frac{df}{dx}\Bigr)^*g\,dx = \langle\hat pf|g\rangle .
> $$
>
> The boundary term vanishes because square-integrable wave functions go to zero at $\pm\infty$ (pathological exceptions are excluded from the domain). The minus sign from the integration by parts is compensated by the conjugation of $i$: $(-i\hbar)^* = i\hbar$. Without the $i$, the same computation gives $\langle f|g'\rangle = -\langle f'|g\rangle$, so $d/dx$ is anti-Hermitian.
>
> *Hamiltonian.* $\hat p^2 = \hat p\hat p$ is Hermitian (a Hermitian operator commutes with itself), and so is $\hat p^2/2m$; adding the Hermitian $V(x)$ keeps it Hermitian ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], part 3).

*Uses:* [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]

> [!caution]- ★ Caution: Hermitian is not quite self-adjoint
> An operator is fixed not only by what it does but by its **domain**, the set of functions it may act on. Differential operators cannot act on all of $L^2$: $-i\hbar\,d/dx$ can carry a function out of Hilbert space ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^ex-b2-1-1|Example §B2.1.1]]). What observables need is more than Hermiticity (same action as the adjoint): the domains of $\hat Q$ and $\hat Q^\dagger$ must also coincide, and such operators are called **self-adjoint**. On the whole line, or on a ring with periodic functions, the momentum operator is self-adjoint. On a finite interval $[0, L]$ the boundary term $-i\hbar[f^*g]_0^L$ decides. If $\hat p$ acts only on functions with $g(0) = g(L) = 0$, the boundary term vanishes whatever $f$ is, so $\hat p$ is Hermitian but its adjoint accepts every differentiable $f$: the domains differ, and $\hat p$ is not self-adjoint. Allowing instead $g(L) = \lambda g(0)$ forces $f(L) = f(0)/\lambda^*$ on the adjoint's side, and the two domains agree exactly when $|\lambda| = 1$: there is a whole family of self-adjoint momentum operators on the interval, labelled by the phase of $\lambda$, and nothing singles out one of them. The infinite-well Hamiltonian is unaffected (with $\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0$ it is self-adjoint), but "the momentum of a particle in a box" has no unique operator; Griffiths avoids the issue by treating box states as functions on the whole line that vanish outside the box. Physicists say "Hermitian" for "self-adjoint" almost always, and so do these notes.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.48, ch. 3 footnotes 7, 9, 45–47*

## Determinate states and eigenvalues

> [!definition] Definition §B2.2.2: Determinate State; Eigenfunction and Eigenvalue; Spectrum; Degeneracy
> A state $\Psi$ is a **determinate state** of the observable $Q$ if every measurement of $Q$ on systems prepared in $\Psi$ gives the same value $q$. A nonzero function $\psi$ with
>
> $$
> \hat Q\psi = q\,\psi
> $$
>
> is an **eigenfunction** of $\hat Q$ with **eigenvalue** $q$ (a number; $\psi = 0$ never counts, while $q = 0$ is allowed). Any nonzero multiple of an eigenfunction is again one, with the same eigenvalue. The set of all eigenvalues is the **spectrum** of $\hat Q$. The spectrum is **degenerate** if two or more linearly independent eigenfunctions share one eigenvalue; they span the **eigenspace** of that eigenvalue.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.2.2, eqs. (3.21)–(3.23) · Fleisch §2.1 · [[§14 Invariant Subspaces#^ladr-5-5|LADR Def. 5.5]], [[§14 Invariant Subspaces#^ladr-5-8|LADR Def. 5.8]]*

^def-b2-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.3: Determinate States Are Eigenfunctions
> For a Hermitian $\hat Q$ and a normalized state $\Psi$,
>
> $$
> \sigma_Q^2 = \bigl\langle(\hat Q - \langle Q\rangle)^2\bigr\rangle = \bigl\|(\hat Q - \langle Q\rangle)\Psi\bigr\|^2 ,
> $$
>
> so $\sigma_Q = 0$ exactly when $\hat Q\Psi = \langle Q\rangle\Psi$. The determinate states of $Q$ are the eigenfunctions of $\hat Q$, and the value obtained with certainty is the eigenvalue. In particular the determinate states of energy satisfy
>
> $$
> \hat H\psi = E\psi ,
> $$
>
> which is the time-independent Schrödinger equation: level A's stationary states ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^rem-a4-1-6|Remark: Operator form]]) are the eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian, and solving that equation is an eigenvalue problem. The factor $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ does not change this.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.2.2, eqs. (3.21)–(3.24) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 9, §2, Claim 2, eqs. (2.44)–(2.50) · Greensite §7.3*

^thm-b2-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The variance is the mean squared deviation ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-4|Def. §A3.4.4]]), now for any observable: $\sigma_Q^2 = \langle\Psi|(\hat Q - \langle Q\rangle)^2\Psi\rangle$. Since $\langle Q\rangle$ is real ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]), $\hat Q - \langle Q\rangle$ is Hermitian, so one factor can be moved into the bra:
>
> $$
> \sigma_Q^2 = \bigl\langle(\hat Q - \langle Q\rangle)\Psi\bigm|(\hat Q - \langle Q\rangle)\Psi\bigr\rangle = \bigl\|(\hat Q - \langle Q\rangle)\Psi\bigr\|^2 .
> $$
>
> A norm vanishes only for the zero vector ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]]), so $\sigma_Q = 0$ iff $\hat Q\Psi = \langle Q\rangle\Psi$. Conversely, if $\hat Q\Psi = q\Psi$ with $\Psi$ normalized, then $\langle Q\rangle = \langle\Psi|q\Psi\rangle = q$ and the same identity gives $\sigma_Q = 0$. (That a zero spread means every single measurement gives $q$ is the statistical reading of $\sigma_Q$; it becomes exact with the measurement postulate, [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]].) Multiplying $\psi$ by $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ does not affect $\hat H\psi = E\psi$, since $\hat H$ acts on $x$ only.

> [!example] Example §B2.2.1: An Operator on a Ring
> A bead slides on a circular wire; its wave functions are functions $f(\phi)$ of the polar angle with $f(\phi + 2\pi) = f(\phi)$, since $\phi$ and $\phi + 2\pi$ are the same point. Is $\hat Q = i\,d/d\phi$ Hermitian, and what is its spectrum?
>
> *Hermitian.* Integrating by parts on $[0, 2\pi]$,
>
> $$
> \langle f|\hat Qg\rangle = \int_0^{2\pi}f^*\Bigl(i\frac{dg}{d\phi}\Bigr)d\phi = if^*g\Big|_0^{2\pi} - \int_0^{2\pi}i\,\frac{df^*}{d\phi}\,g\,d\phi = \langle\hat Qf|g\rangle :
> $$
>
> this time the boundary term disappears by periodicity, not by decay, and $(i\,df/d\phi)^* = -i\,df^*/d\phi$ supplies the sign.
>
> *Spectrum.* $i f' = qf$ has the solutions $f(\phi) = Ae^{-iq\phi}$. Periodicity requires $e^{-2\pi iq} = 1$, so $q = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots$. The spectrum is the set of integers, discrete because the ring is finite, and nondegenerate: each $q$ has one eigenfunction up to a factor. The eigenvalues are real and the eigenfunctions $e^{-iq\phi}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ orthonormal, as Theorem §B2.2.4 below guarantees. Up to the factor $-\hbar$ this is the operator $\hat L_z = -i\hbar\,\partial/\partial\phi$ of angular momentum about the axis, whose eigenvalues are therefore $m\hbar$ with integer $m$ ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-6|Theorem §B5.2.6]], [[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-7|Theorem §B5.2.7]]; level A: [[§A5.1 The Hydrogen Atom#^thm-a5-1-5|Theorem §A5.1.5]]). The same basis appears as the Fourier basis of a particle on a ring in [[§23 The Completeness Relation#^ex-23-2|556 Ex. §23.2]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 3.1, eqs. (3.25)–(3.29), Problem 3.8(a)*

## Discrete spectra

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.4: Eigenvalues and Eigenfunctions of a Hermitian Operator
> Let $\hat Q$ be Hermitian, and consider its normalizable eigenfunctions (those in Hilbert space).
> 1. Their eigenvalues are real.
> 2. Eigenfunctions belonging to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
> 3. Any linear combination of eigenfunctions with the same eigenvalue $q$ is again an eigenfunction with eigenvalue $q$; within each such eigenspace an orthonormal set of eigenfunctions can be chosen (Gram–Schmidt).
>
> Hence the eigenfunctions of a Hermitian operator with discrete spectrum can always be chosen **orthonormal**, $\langle f_m|f_n\rangle = \delta_{mn}$, and they are assumed so from now on. In particular the stationary states of any one-dimensional potential, being eigenfunctions of $\hat H$ with distinct energies, are orthogonal: level A's computation for the box ([[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^thm-a4-2-3|Theorem §A4.2.3]]) becomes an instance of a general theorem, and the same holds for the oscillator ([[§B4.1 Ladder Operators and the Spectrum#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]) and for the determinate states of every observable.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3.1 (Theorems 1 and 2), Problem 3.7(a); App. A.6, eqs. (A.96)–(A.98) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 9, Claims 2–3, eqs. (1.8)–(1.15) · Fleisch §2.3*

^thm-b2-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* Let $\hat Qf = qf$ with $f \ne 0$ in Hilbert space. Then, pulling $q$ out of the second slot and $q^*$ out of the first,
>
> $$
> q\langle f|f\rangle = \langle f|\hat Qf\rangle = \langle\hat Qf|f\rangle = q^*\langle f|f\rangle .
> $$
>
> $\langle f|f\rangle \ne 0$, so $q = q^*$. (Zwiebach's version: $q = \langle Q\rangle_f$, which is real by [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]].)
>
> *2.* Let $\hat Qf = qf$, $\hat Qg = q'g$ with $q \ne q'$. Then $q'\langle f|g\rangle = \langle f|\hat Qg\rangle = \langle\hat Qf|g\rangle = q^*\langle f|g\rangle = q\langle f|g\rangle$, using part 1. So $(q' - q)\langle f|g\rangle = 0$ and $\langle f|g\rangle = 0$.
>
> *3.* $\hat Q(af + bg) = aqf + bqg = q(af + bg)$ by linearity. The eigenspace is a subspace, and Gram–Schmidt ([[§20 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-32|LADR Thm. 6.32]]) turns any basis of it into an orthonormal one; since every step forms combinations of eigenfunctions with eigenvalue $q$, the results are again eigenfunctions. Different eigenspaces are already orthogonal by 2. All inner products used exist because the eigenfunctions are square-integrable; for non-normalizable eigenfunctions the argument fails, which is why continuous spectra need separate treatment (Def. §B2.2.3). In finite dimensions these are [[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-12|LADR Thm. 7.12]] and [[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-22|LADR Thm. 7.22]].

> [!example] Example §B2.2.2: A Degenerate Spectrum on the Ring
> On the same ring as [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^ex-b2-2-1|Example §B2.2.1]], consider $\hat Q = d^2/d\phi^2$.
>
> *Hermitian.* Integrating by parts twice, both boundary terms $[f^*g']_0^{2\pi}$ and $[f^{*\prime}g]_0^{2\pi}$ vanish by periodicity, so $\langle f|g''\rangle = \langle f''|g\rangle$. (Alternatively: $d^2/d\phi^2 = -(i\,d/d\phi)^2$ is minus the square of a Hermitian operator.)
>
> *Spectrum.* $f'' = qf$ with $q = -n^2$ has the solutions $e^{\pm in\phi}$, which are periodic exactly when $n$ is an integer; $q > 0$ gives real exponentials, which are not periodic. So the eigenvalues are $q = -n^2$, $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$. For $n \ge 1$ each is **doubly degenerate**, with eigenfunctions $e^{in\phi}$ and $e^{-in\phi}$; $n = 0$ (the constant) is nondegenerate.
>
> *Choosing a basis in the degenerate eigenspace.* Every combination $ae^{in\phi} + be^{-in\phi}$ is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue $-n^2$. The pair $e^{\pm in\phi}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ is orthonormal; so is the pair $\cos n\phi/\sqrt\pi$, $\sin n\phi/\sqrt\pi$; and a non-orthogonal pair such as $e^{in\phi}$ and $\cos n\phi$ is just as legitimate a basis of the eigenspace until Gram–Schmidt is applied. Theorem §B2.2.4 says only that some orthonormal choice exists. Which one is natural depends on a second observable: $e^{\pm in\phi}$ are also eigenfunctions of $i\,d/d\phi$ (Example §B2.2.1), with the different eigenvalues $\mp n$, and that removes the ambiguity ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]]).
>
> *Physics.* For a bead of moment of inertia $I$ the Hamiltonian is $-(\hbar^2/2I)\,d^2/d\phi^2$, with energies $n^2\hbar^2/2I$: the degeneracy is that between going round clockwise and counterclockwise.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 3.6, 3.7, 3.8(b)*

## Completeness

In finite dimensions the eigenvectors of a Hermitian matrix span the space: that is the spectral theorem ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]], [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]). The proof does not carry over to infinite dimensions, yet the property is what makes measurement probabilities add to one ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-5|Theorem §B2.3.5]]). Following Dirac, it is taken as an axiom.

> [!principle] Principle §B2.2.5: Completeness of the Eigenfunctions of an Observable
> The eigenfunctions of an operator that represents an observable are complete: every function in Hilbert space can be expanded in them. For a discrete spectrum this is an expansion $f = \sum_nc_nf_n$ in orthonormal eigenfunctions ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]]); for a continuous spectrum the sum becomes an integral over Dirac-orthonormalized eigenfunctions ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]]); for a mixed spectrum both appear. Equivalently, the axiom restricts which Hermitian operators may represent observables.
>
> *Domain: the observables of non-relativistic quantum mechanics treated at this level. It is a theorem in finite dimensions and for many specific operators; in infinite dimensions it is assumed in general (rigorously, the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators supplies it in the sense of a projection-valued measure, [[§24 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^rem-24-3|556 Remark, §24]]).*
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3.1 (Axiom), footnotes 12–13; App. A.6 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 9, Claim 4, eqs. (1.16)–(1.20) · Fleisch §2.3*

^pr-b2-2-5

> [!remark] Remark: When completeness is a theorem, and what it requires
> - **Proved cases.** Every Hermitian matrix (finite-dimensional systems such as spin, Chapter B6): the spectral theorem. The infinite-well states: Dirichlet's theorem on Fourier sine series ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-7|WO Theorem §B4.1.7]]; Griffiths' footnote 13 notes the awkwardness of calling a provable fact an axiom). The oscillator: [[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials]]. This is what level A quoted without proof ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^rem-a4-3-1|Remark: Every state is such a sum]]): for the box it is now a theorem; in general it is this principle.
> - **The continuum cannot be dropped.** For hydrogen, whose potential vanishes at infinity, the bound states alone are **not** complete: some states have zero overlap with every bound state, and their weight is the probability of ionization ([[§25 Bound States Need Not Be Complete꞉ Hydrogen#^thm-25-1|556 Thm. §25.1]], [[§25 Bound States Need Not Be Complete꞉ Hydrogen#^cor-25-2|556 Cor. §25.2]], [[§25 Bound States Need Not Be Complete꞉ Hydrogen#^rem-25-1|556 Remark, §25]]). Completeness refers to all the eigenfunctions, scattering states included. The same holds for the finite well and the delta well, which have both bound and scattering states. The user's Classical notes (§10.1) say that the eigenfunctions $\psi_n$ of $\hat H$ "form a complete set of solutions in the Hilbert space"; that is right for purely discrete spectra (box, oscillator), while for potentials with a continuum the scattering states must be added.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3, footnote 13; App. A.6 · the user's Classical notes, §10.1*

## Continuous spectra

> [!definition] Definition §B2.2.3: Continuous Spectrum; Dirac Orthonormality
> The spectrum of an operator is **continuous** if its eigenvalues fill out a range, labelled by a continuous variable $z$. Such eigenfunctions $f_z$ are not normalizable: they lie outside Hilbert space and do not represent physical states, though wave packets built from a range of them do. A family with real eigenvalues is **Dirac-orthonormal** if
>
> $$
> \langle f_{z'}|f_z\rangle = \delta(z - z') ,
> $$
>
> the continuous analogue of $\langle f_m|f_n\rangle = \delta_{mn}$, with the Dirac delta ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|WO Def. §B4.4.2]]) in place of the Kronecker delta. In Dirac notation such a family is written $\{|e_z\rangle\}$, $\langle e_z|e_{z'}\rangle = \delta(z - z')$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3.2, eq. (3.33); §3.6.2, eq. (3.95) · Fleisch §4.2*

^def-b2-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.6: Eigenfunctions of Momentum and of Position
> 1. **Momentum.** The eigenfunctions of $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$ on the whole line with real eigenvalue $p$, normalized as
>
> $$
> f_p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\,e^{ipx/\hbar} ,
> $$
>
> are Dirac-orthonormal, $\langle f_{p'}|f_p\rangle = \delta(p - p')$, and complete: every square-integrable $f$ is
>
> $$
> f(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}c(p)\,f_p(x)\,dp, \qquad c(p) = \langle f_p|f\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ipx/\hbar}f(x)\,dx ,
> $$
>
> a Fourier transform. None of them is square-integrable; eigenfunctions with complex $p$ blow up at one end and are not even Dirac-normalizable. Each $f_p$ is a sinusoid of wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi\hbar/p$: de Broglie's relation ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^pr-a3-1-1|Principle §A3.1.1]]) is the wavelength of a momentum eigenfunction.
> 2. **Position.** The eigenfunctions of $\hat x$ with eigenvalue $y$ (necessarily real) are
>
> $$
> g_y(x) = \delta(x - y), \qquad \langle g_{y'}|g_y\rangle = \delta(y - y'), \qquad f(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}c(y)\,g_y(x)\,dy \ \text{ with } c(y) = f(y) .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths Examples 3.2–3.3, eqs. (3.30)–(3.42), footnote 14*

^thm-b2-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Momentum.* $-i\hbar f' = pf$ is solved by $f = Ae^{ipx/\hbar}$, whose modulus is constant (real $p$) or grows exponentially at one end (complex $p$); in neither case is $\int|f|^2dx$ finite. For real $p, p'$, the Fourier representation of the delta function, $\int e^{iku}\,du = 2\pi\delta(k)$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|WO Theorem §B4.4.3]], part 4), with $k = (p - p')/\hbar$ and the scaling rule $\delta(k) = \hbar\,\delta(p - p')$, gives
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{p'}^*f_p\,dx = |A|^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i(p - p')x/\hbar}\,dx = |A|^2\,2\pi\hbar\,\delta(p - p') ,
> $$
>
> so $A = 1/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$ yields Dirac orthonormality. If $f = \int c(p)f_p\,dp$, then "Fourier's trick" works as in the discrete case: $\langle f_{p'}|f\rangle = \int c(p)\,\delta(p - p')\,dp = c(p')$. That every square-integrable $f$ has such an expansion is the Fourier inversion theorem ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|WO Theorem §B4.4.1]]; WO's transform $\tilde f(k) = \frac1{2\pi}\int fe^{-ikx}dx$ is related by $c(p) = \sqrt{2\pi/\hbar}\,\tilde f(p/\hbar)$). Finally $\lambda = 2\pi/k = 2\pi\hbar/p$.
>
> *Position.* $x\,g_y(x) = y\,g_y(x)$ for all $x$ forces $g_y$ to vanish except at $x = y$; the delta function does this, since $(x - y)\delta(x - y) = 0$ (part 2 of the same theorem). Then $\int\delta(x - y')\delta(x - y)\,dx = \delta(y - y')$ by the sifting property, and $\int f(y)\delta(x - y)\,dy = f(x)$. Rigorously, multiplication by $x$ has no eigenvectors at all in $L^2$ ([[§24 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-24-3|556 Prop. §24.3]]), and $\langle x_0|$, point evaluation, is not a bounded functional ([[§24 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-24-4|556 Prop. §24.4]]): the delta function is the symptom.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.7: Continuous Resolution of the Identity
> If $\{|e_z\rangle\}$ is a Dirac-orthonormal, complete family, then
>
> $$
> \int|e_z\rangle\langle e_z|\,dz = 1 ;
> $$
>
> in particular, for the eigenstates of position and of momentum,
>
> $$
> \int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x| = 1, \qquad \int dp\,|p\rangle\langle p| = 1, \qquad \langle x|p\rangle = f_p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}e^{ipx/\hbar} .
> $$
>
> For a spectrum with a discrete and a continuous part, $\sum_n|n\rangle\langle n| + \int dz\,|z\rangle\langle z| = 1$. Together with the discrete relation ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]) these are the tidiest statements of completeness.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.6.2, eqs. (3.95)–(3.96); §3.6.3, eq. (3.106), Example 3.9*

^thm-b2-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Completeness ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]]) writes any $|\alpha\rangle$ as $\int c(z)|e_z\rangle\,dz$, and Dirac orthonormality gives $c(z) = \langle e_z|\alpha\rangle$, as in [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]. Hence $\int|e_z\rangle\langle e_z|\alpha\rangle\,dz = |\alpha\rangle$ for every $|\alpha\rangle$, which is the statement. The kets $|x\rangle$, $|p\rangle$ are the eigenfunctions of Theorem §B2.2.6 written basis-free; the position-space components of $|p\rangle$ are $\langle x|p\rangle = f_p(x)$. For a mixed spectrum, expand in both parts; the discrete and continuous eigenfunctions are mutually orthogonal (distinct eigenvalues).
>
> *What the integral means.* No $|x\rangle$ is a vector of $L^2$, so $|x\rangle\langle x|$ is not an operator; but $\int_B|x\rangle\langle x|\,dx$, for a region $B$, is a genuine operator, multiplication by the indicator function of $B$, and these operators form a projection-valued measure whose total is $1$ ([[§24 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-24-5|556 Prop. §24.5]], [[§24 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^rem-24-3|556 Remark, §24]]). The physicist's identity $\sum_n\psi_n(x)\psi_n(x')^* = \delta(x - x')$ is the discrete completeness relation in this language ([[§24 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^rem-24-4|556 Remark, §24]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Discrete, continuous and mixed spectra
> - **Discrete only:** the harmonic oscillator and the infinite well ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^mod-a4-5-2|Model §A4.5.2]], [[§A4.2 The Particle in a Box#^mod-a4-2-1|Model §A4.2.1]]). Every eigenfunction is a physical state.
> - **Continuous only:** the free particle, and the step and barrier ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^def-a4-6-1|Def. §A4.6.1]]). The eigenfunctions are idealizations, like the plane wave that level A could not normalize ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^rem-a3-4-4|Remark: Acceptable wave functions]], [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^cau-a4-1-1|Caution: A plane wave is not a particle]]); they live in the "suburbs" of Hilbert space, having finite inner products with every genuine state though not with themselves, and are used for scattering ([[§B3.4 Scattering in One Dimension]]) and for wave packets ([[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-1|Theorem §B1.4.1]], [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-2|Theorem §B1.4.2]]).
> - **Both:** the finite well and the delta well (bound states below the top of the well, scattering states above; [[§B3.2 Square Wells Revisited]], [[§B3.3 The Delta-Function Potential]]), and hydrogen.
>
> The momentum eigenfunctions also sharpen de Broglie's relation: there is no such thing as a particle with determinate momentum, only normalizable packets with a narrow range of momenta, to which $\lambda = 2\pi\hbar/p$ applies. The lowest box state, for instance, is not a momentum eigenfunction ($\hat p\sin(\pi x/L) \propto \cos(\pi x/L)$), and its momentum spread is $\sigma_p = \pi\hbar/L \ne 0$ ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^ex-a3-4-2|Example §A3.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.3 (introduction), §3.3.2, Problems 3.9–3.10*
