Level A described a particle by a wave function $\Psi(x, t)$ that must be normalizable ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-2|Def. §A3.4.2]]), and expanded solutions in stationary states "much as any vector is a combination of basis vectors" ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^rem-a4-3-1|Remark: Every state is such a sum]], [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]). This section makes the analogy literal. Normalizable wave functions form a vector space with an inner product, Hilbert space; a state is a vector in it, written as a ket; the wave function $\Psi(x, t)$, the momentum-space wave function and the coefficients $c_n$ are three sets of components of the same vector; and operators become (infinite) matrices. Everything here is the physicist's working version. The rigorous statements, with the precise sense in which infinite sums converge, are in Functional Analysis, Chapter 7 ([[§22 Bras, Kets, and the Riesz Map|556 §22]], [[§23 The Completeness Relation|556 §23]], [[§24 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions|556 §24]]), to which the items below point.

*Notation for Chapter B2.* Potential energy is $V$, as in Griffiths ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^cau-b1-1-1|Caution: V, not U]]); operators carry hats where they could be confused with numbers; a state vector is $|\Psi(t)\rangle$ (Griffiths writes $|\mathcal S(t)\rangle$). "Hermitian" is capitalized here (Griffiths writes "hermitian").

## The state space

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.1: Square-Integrable Function; Hilbert Space
> A complex function $f$ on an interval $(a, b)$ (possibly $(-\infty, \infty)$) is **square-integrable** if
>
> $$
> \int_a^b |f(x)|^2\,dx < \infty .
> $$
>
> The square-integrable functions form a vector space, which mathematicians call $L^2(a, b)$ and physicists call **Hilbert space**. Two functions whose difference has $\int|f - g|^2\,dx = 0$ are identified, so strictly a vector of $L^2$ is a class of functions that agree except on a negligible set. In quantum mechanics, *wave functions live in Hilbert space*: a normalized wave function is a unit vector of $L^2$. Mathematically, a Hilbert space is any inner-product space that is complete in the norm the inner product induces ([[§17 Cauchy–Schwarz and the Induced Norm#^def-17-1|556 Def. §17.1]]); $L^2$ is the example that matters here ([[§16 Definition and Examples#^ex-16-3|556 Ex. §16.3]], [[§17 Cauchy–Schwarz and the Induced Norm#^ex-17-1|556 Ex. §17.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1, eqs. (3.4)–(3.5), footnotes 3, 6 · Greensite §4.2, eq. (4.15) · the user's Classical notes, §10.2*

^def-b2-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.2: Inner Product of Functions; Norm; Orthonormal Set
> The **inner product** of $f, g \in L^2(a, b)$ is
>
> $$
> \langle f|g\rangle = \int_a^b f(x)^*\,g(x)\,dx ,
> $$
>
> and the **norm** of $f$ is $\|f\| = \sqrt{\langle f|f\rangle}$. A function is **normalized** if $\langle f|f\rangle = 1$; two functions are **orthogonal** if $\langle f|g\rangle = 0$; a set $\{f_n\}$ is **orthonormal** if $\langle f_m|f_n\rangle = \delta_{mn}$. For column vectors of components in an orthonormal basis the same definition reads $\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle = a_1^*b_1 + \cdots + a_N^*b_N$: the function $f(x)$ is "a vector with a continuous index $x$", and the sum over the index becomes an integral.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1, eqs. (3.2), (3.6), (3.9)–(3.10); App. A.2, eqs. (A.22)–(A.24) · Greensite §4.2, eqs. (4.20)–(4.23) · Fleisch §1.3*

^def-b2-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.1: Properties of the Inner Product; Schwarz Inequality
> For $f, g, h \in L^2(a, b)$ and complex numbers $a, b$:
> 1. $\langle g|f\rangle = \langle f|g\rangle^*$;
> 2. $\langle f|\,a g + b h\rangle = a\langle f|g\rangle + b\langle f|h\rangle$ (linear in the second slot);
> 3. $\langle a g + b h|\,f\rangle = a^*\langle g|f\rangle + b^*\langle h|f\rangle$ (conjugate-linear in the first slot);
> 4. $\langle f|f\rangle \ge 0$, with $\langle f|f\rangle = 0$ only for $f = 0$ (for continuous $f$: $f(x) = 0$ at every $x$);
> 5. **Schwarz inequality:** the integral defining $\langle f|g\rangle$ converges, and
>
> $$
> \Bigl|\int_a^b f(x)^*g(x)\,dx\Bigr| \le \sqrt{\int_a^b |f(x)|^2\,dx\int_a^b |g(x)|^2\,dx}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad |\langle f|g\rangle| \le \|f\|\,\|g\| ,
> $$
>
> with equality exactly when one of $f, g$ is a multiple of the other;
> 6. $L^2(a, b)$ is a vector space: sums and multiples of square-integrable functions are square-integrable. The normalized functions alone do not form one.
>
> Properties 1, 2 and 4 are the axioms of an inner product (A.19)–(A.21); 3 follows from 1 and 2.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1, eqs. (3.7)–(3.9), Problem 3.1; App. A.2, eqs. (A.19)–(A.21), (A.27), Problem A.5 · PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 1*

^thm-b2-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1–2.* The complex conjugate of an integral is the integral of the conjugate, and $(f^*g)^* = g^*f$, so $\langle f|g\rangle^* = \int g^*f\,dx = \langle g|f\rangle$. Linearity of the integral ([[§33 Properties of the Riemann Integral#^thm-33-2|451 Thm. §33.2]]) gives $\int f^*(ag + bh) = a\int f^*g + b\int f^*h$.
>
> *3.* By 1 and 2, $\langle ag + bh|f\rangle = \langle f|ag + bh\rangle^* = (a\langle f|g\rangle + b\langle f|h\rangle)^* = a^*\langle g|f\rangle + b^*\langle h|f\rangle$: constants come out of the bra conjugated.
>
> *4.* $\langle f|f\rangle = \int|f|^2\,dx$ has a non-negative integrand. If $f$ is continuous and $f(x_0) \ne 0$, then $|f|^2 > \tfrac12|f(x_0)|^2$ on some interval around $x_0$, so the integral is positive; hence $\langle f|f\rangle = 0$ forces $f \equiv 0$. For general $L^2$ functions it forces $f = 0$ except on a negligible set, which is why such functions are identified with $0$ (Def. §B2.1.1).
>
> *5. Existence.* From $(|f| - |g|)^2 \ge 0$, $|f^*g| \le \tfrac12(|f|^2 + |g|^2)$ at every point, so $\int|f^*g|\,dx$ is finite and the (possibly improper) integral $\langle f|g\rangle$ converges absolutely ([[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-2|451 Def. §36.2]]). This step is needed first: the usual proof of the inequality below presupposes that the inner products exist.
>
> *5. Inequality.* If $g = 0$ both sides vanish. Otherwise put $\lambda = \langle g|f\rangle/\langle g|g\rangle$ and expand with 2–3:
>
> $$
> 0 \le \langle f - \lambda g|f - \lambda g\rangle = \langle f|f\rangle - \lambda\langle f|g\rangle - \lambda^*\langle g|f\rangle + |\lambda|^2\langle g|g\rangle = \langle f|f\rangle - \frac{|\langle g|f\rangle|^2}{\langle g|g\rangle} ,
> $$
>
> since each of the last three terms equals $\pm|\langle g|f\rangle|^2/\langle g|g\rangle$. Multiply by $\langle g|g\rangle$. Equality holds exactly when $f - \lambda g = 0$ (property 4).
>
> *6.* $|f + g|^2 \le |f + g|^2 + |f - g|^2 = 2|f|^2 + 2|g|^2$ pointwise, so $f + g$ is square-integrable, and $\int|cf|^2 = |c|^2\int|f|^2$. The sum of two normalized functions is not normalized in general (for orthogonal ones its norm is $\sqrt2$), and $0$ is not normalized, so normalized functions are not a subspace.

> [!caution] Caution: Which slot carries the complex conjugate
> Physics (Griffiths, Dirac notation, this vault's QM notes) makes the inner product **conjugate-linear in the first slot and linear in the second**: $\langle cf|g\rangle = c^*\langle f|g\rangle$, $\langle f|cg\rangle = c\langle f|g\rangle$. Axler's *Linear Algebra Done Right* does the opposite: its $\langle u, v\rangle$ is linear in the **first** slot and conjugate-linear in the second ([[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-2|LADR Def. 6.2]]), and so does the Functional Analysis course, whose dictionary is $\langle y|x\rangle = (x, y)$ ([[§22 Bras, Kets, and the Riesz Map#^rem-22-1|556 Remark: Conventions]]). Every theorem holds in both conventions, but formulas with constants pulled out of an inner product, and the definition of the adjoint written with the operator in a particular slot, must be translated before they are copied from one to the other. For example $\int f\,g^*\,dx$ (WO's Plancherel identity, [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|WO Theorem §B4.4.4]]) is $\langle g|f\rangle$ here.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1, eq. (3.6) · PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 1*

^cau-b2-1-1

> [!example] Example §B2.1.1: Which Powers Are in Hilbert Space?
> On the interval $(0, 1)$, take $f(x) = x^\nu$ with real $\nu$. For $\nu \ne -\tfrac12$,
>
> $$
> \int_0^1 x^{2\nu}\,dx = \lim_{\epsilon\to0^+}\frac{1 - \epsilon^{2\nu + 1}}{2\nu + 1} ,
> $$
>
> which is $1/(2\nu + 1)$ if $\nu > -\tfrac12$ and diverges if $\nu < -\tfrac12$; at $\nu = -\tfrac12$ the integral is $\int_0^1 dx/x$, which diverges logarithmically. So $x^\nu \in L^2(0, 1)$ exactly when $\nu > -\tfrac12$: a function may blow up at an end and still be a legal state, provided it does so slowly enough.
>
> Now take $\nu = \tfrac12$. Then $f = \sqrt x$ is in Hilbert space ($\|f\|^2 = \tfrac12$), and so is $x f = x^{3/2}$; but
>
> $$
> \frac{df}{dx} = \tfrac12\,x^{-1/2}
> $$
>
> is not ($\nu = -\tfrac12$). Applying the momentum operator $-i\hbar\,d/dx$ to a perfectly good state has carried it **out** of Hilbert space. Correspondingly $\langle p^2\rangle = \hbar^2\int_0^1|f'|^2\,dx$ is infinite for the normalized state $\sqrt{2x}$: a legitimate state with infinite mean kinetic energy. Differential operators cannot act on all of $L^2$; their domain has to be restricted ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^cau-b2-2-1|Caution: Hermitian is not quite self-adjoint]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.2; footnote 7 of ch. 3*

## Orthonormal bases

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.3: Complete Orthonormal Set (Orthonormal Basis)
> An orthonormal set $\{f_n\}$ in Hilbert space is **complete** if every function $f$ in the space can be written as a linear combination of its members,
>
> $$
> f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n\,f_n(x) ,
> $$
>
> the infinite sum converging in the norm: $\|f - \sum_{n\le N}c_nf_n\| \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$. A complete orthonormal set is an **orthonormal basis**. The stationary states of the infinite square well are an orthonormal basis of $L^2(0, L)$ ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]]; Fourier sine series, [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-7|WO Theorem §B4.1.7]]), and those of the harmonic oscillator are one of $L^2(-\infty, \infty)$ ([[§B4.2 The Analytic Method and Hermite Polynomials#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1, eq. (3.11) · Fleisch §1.5–1.6*

^def-b2-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Two meanings of "complete"
> A *complete orthonormal set* (this definition) spans the space. A *complete space* has no holes: every Cauchy sequence in it converges to an element of the space ([[§17 Cauchy–Schwarz and the Induced Norm#^def-17-1|556 Def. §17.1]]); $L^2$ is complete, the polynomials (like the rationals) are not. The two have nothing to do with each other, although the second is what makes the first work: in a complete space, coefficients with $\sum|c_n|^2 < \infty$ always add up to a vector of the space. Mathematicians say "orthonormal basis" ([[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^def-20-4|556 Def. §20.4]]; Wu's "complete orthonormal set", [[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^def-20-2|556 Def. §20.2]]) and reserve "complete" for the space.
>
> *Source: Griffiths ch. 3, footnote 3*

^rem-b2-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.2: Expansion in an Orthonormal Basis
> Let $\{f_n\}$ be an orthonormal basis. Every $f$ in Hilbert space has the expansion $f = \sum_n c_nf_n$ with the coefficients ("Fourier's trick")
>
> $$
> c_n = \langle f_n|f\rangle = \int f_n(x)^*\,f(x)\,dx ,
> $$
>
> and if $g = \sum_n d_nf_n$, then
>
> $$
> \langle f|g\rangle = \sum_n c_n^*\,d_n, \qquad \|f\|^2 = \sum_n |c_n|^2 \quad\text{(Parseval)} .
> $$
>
> In particular $f$ is normalized exactly when $\sum|c_n|^2 = 1$. Level A proved this for the stationary states of one potential ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-2|Theorem §A4.3.2]]); it holds for every orthonormal basis.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1, eqs. (3.11)–(3.12); App. A.2, eqs. (A.24)–(A.26) · Fleisch §1.6 · Greensite ch. 21*

^thm-b2-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the inner product of $f = \sum_m c_mf_m$ with $f_n$ and use orthonormality: $\langle f_n|f\rangle = \sum_m c_m\langle f_n|f_m\rangle = \sum_m c_m\delta_{nm} = c_n$. Likewise, using conjugate-linearity in the first slot ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]]),
>
> $$
> \langle f|g\rangle = \sum_{m, n}c_m^*\,d_n\,\langle f_m|f_n\rangle = \sum_{m, n}c_m^*\,d_n\,\delta_{mn} = \sum_n c_n^*d_n ,
> $$
>
> and $g = f$ gives Parseval. Pulling the infinite sums out of the inner product is the step that needs justification: it holds because the inner product is continuous in the norm and the partial sums converge in the norm. That is the content of [[§23 The Completeness Relation#^thm-23-2|556 Thm. §23.2]] and [[§23 The Completeness Relation#^cor-23-4|556 Cor. §23.4]]; in finite dimensions there is nothing to justify ([[§20 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-30|LADR Thm. 6.30]]).

## Dirac notation

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.4: Ket; Bra; Dual Space
> A vector of Hilbert space is written as a **ket** $|\alpha\rangle$. The **bra** $\langle\beta|$ is the linear function that turns a ket into a number by taking the inner product with $|\beta\rangle$: $\langle\beta|$ applied to $|\alpha\rangle$ is $\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle$. The bras form a vector space of their own, the **dual space**. In a function space the bra is an instruction to integrate,
>
> $$
> \langle f| = \int f^*\,[\cdots]\,dx ,
> $$
>
> with the slot $[\cdots]$ waiting for the function in the ket. In components with respect to an orthonormal basis, kets are columns and bras are rows of complex-conjugated components:
>
> $$
> |\alpha\rangle \to \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_N\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \langle\beta| \to \bigl(b_1^*\ \cdots\ b_N^*\bigr), \qquad \langle\beta|\alpha\rangle = b_1^*a_1 + \cdots + b_N^*a_N .
> $$
>
> The bra belonging to $c|\alpha\rangle$ is $c^*\langle\alpha|$. A ket $|\alpha\rangle$ is basis-independent; a column of components needs a basis.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.6.2, eqs. (3.89)–(3.90) · Fleisch §1.2, eqs. (1.11)–(1.18) · Greensite §4.2*

^def-b2-1-4

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: What a bra is, rigorously
> That every ket has a bra, and every reasonable linear function of kets is a bra, is the Riesz representation theorem: the map $|a\rangle \mapsto \langle a|$ is a conjugate-linear isometric bijection from $H$ onto the space of bounded linear functionals ([[§22 Bras, Kets, and the Riesz Map#^thm-22-2|556 Thm. §22.2]], [[§22 Bras, Kets, and the Riesz Map#^def-22-2|556 Def. §22.2]], [[§22 Bras, Kets, and the Riesz Map#^rem-22-3|556 Remark, §22]]; in finite dimensions [[§20 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-42|LADR Thm. 6.42]]). The word "bounded" matters: the functional $f \mapsto f(x_0)$ is linear but unbounded, so it is **not** a bra, and the position eigenstate $|x_0\rangle$ is not a ket of $L^2$ ([[§24 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^prop-24-4|556 Prop. §24.4]], [[§24 Position Eigenstates and Continuous Resolutions#^rem-24-2|556 Remark, §24]]). Physics uses such objects all the time; [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^def-b2-2-3|Def. §B2.2.3]] says how.
>
> The symbol inside a ket is only a **name** (Griffiths: "Alice" would do). When kets are named after the functions they represent, $\langle\hat Qf|$ means "the bra of the ket $\hat Q|f\rangle$"; it equals $\langle f|\hat Q^\dagger$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.6.2 (p. 153), App. A.6 · Fleisch §1.2, §2.3*

^rem-b2-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.5: Outer Product; Projection Operator
> For kets $|\alpha\rangle$, $|\beta\rangle$ the **outer product** $|\alpha\rangle\langle\beta|$ is the operator that sends $|\gamma\rangle$ to $\langle\beta|\gamma\rangle\,|\alpha\rangle$. If $|\alpha\rangle$ is normalized,
>
> $$
> \hat P = |\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|, \qquad \hat P|\beta\rangle = \bigl(\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle\bigr)|\alpha\rangle ,
> $$
>
> is the **projection operator** onto the one-dimensional subspace spanned by $|\alpha\rangle$: it keeps the part of $|\beta\rangle$ that "lies along" $|\alpha\rangle$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.6.2, eq. (3.91) · Fleisch §2.4 · [[§22 Bras, Kets, and the Riesz Map#^def-22-2|556 Def. §22.2]]*

^def-b2-1-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.3: Projections and the Completeness Relation
> 1. A projection operator is idempotent, $\hat P^2 = \hat P$; its eigenvalues are $1$ (eigenvectors: the multiples of $|\alpha\rangle$) and $0$ (eigenvectors: the vectors orthogonal to $|\alpha\rangle$).
> 2. If $\{|e_n\rangle\}$ is an orthonormal basis, $\langle e_m|e_n\rangle = \delta_{mn}$, then
>
> $$
> \sum_n |e_n\rangle\langle e_n| = 1 ,
> $$
>
> the identity operator. This **completeness relation** (resolution of the identity) is the tidiest statement that the basis is complete. Inserting it between a bra and a ket gives $\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle = \sum_n\langle\beta|e_n\rangle\langle e_n|\alpha\rangle$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.6.2, eqs. (3.92)–(3.94), Problem 3.23 · Fleisch §2.4*

^thm-b2-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\hat P^2|\beta\rangle = \langle\alpha|\beta\rangle\,\hat P|\alpha\rangle = \langle\alpha|\beta\rangle\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle|\alpha\rangle = \hat P|\beta\rangle$, since $\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle = 1$. If $\hat P|\beta\rangle = \lambda|\beta\rangle$ with $|\beta\rangle \ne 0$, then $\lambda^2|\beta\rangle = \hat P^2|\beta\rangle = \hat P|\beta\rangle = \lambda|\beta\rangle$, so $\lambda = 0$ or $1$; $\hat P|\alpha\rangle = |\alpha\rangle$, and $\hat P|\beta\rangle = 0$ exactly when $\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle = 0$.
>
> *2.* Apply the operator to any $|\alpha\rangle$: $\sum_n|e_n\rangle\langle e_n|\alpha\rangle = \sum_n a_n|e_n\rangle = |\alpha\rangle$ by [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]. An operator is determined by what it does to every vector, so the sum is the identity. Applying $\langle\beta|$ gives the second formula.
>
> *How the sum converges.* For each fixed $|\alpha\rangle$ the partial sums $\sum_{n\le N}|e_n\rangle\langle e_n|\alpha\rangle$ converge to $|\alpha\rangle$; but in infinite dimensions the operators $\sum_{n\le N}|e_n\rangle\langle e_n|$ never come uniformly close to $1$, since each misses $|e_{N+1}\rangle$ entirely. The relation holds "strongly", not in operator norm ([[§23 The Completeness Relation#^thm-23-2|556 Thm. §23.2]], [[§23 The Completeness Relation#^prop-23-3|556 Prop. §23.3]]). Physics only ever applies it to vectors or sandwiches it between them, which is exactly what strong convergence allows.

The continuous version, $\int|e_z\rangle\langle e_z|\,dz = 1$ for eigenvectors of position or momentum, needs Dirac orthonormality: [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]].

## Operators as matrices

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.6: Linear Operator; Matrix Elements; Functions of Operators
> A **linear operator** $\hat Q$ sends each ket $|\alpha\rangle$ (in its domain) to a ket $\hat Q|\alpha\rangle$, with $\hat Q(a|\alpha\rangle + b|\beta\rangle) = a\hat Q|\alpha\rangle + b\hat Q|\beta\rangle$. Sums and products are defined by
>
> $$
> (\hat Q + \hat R)|\alpha\rangle = \hat Q|\alpha\rangle + \hat R|\alpha\rangle, \qquad \hat Q\hat R|\alpha\rangle = \hat Q\bigl(\hat R|\alpha\rangle\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> first $\hat R$, then $\hat Q$: order matters. With respect to an orthonormal basis $\{|e_n\rangle\}$, the **matrix elements** of $\hat Q$ are
>
> $$
> Q_{mn} = \langle e_m|\hat Q|e_n\rangle .
> $$
>
> A function of an operator is defined by the power series of the function, e.g. $e^{\hat Q} = 1 + \hat Q + \tfrac12\hat Q^2 + \tfrac1{3!}\hat Q^3 + \cdots$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.6.1, eqs. (3.81)–(3.83); §3.6.2, eqs. (3.97)–(3.100) · Greensite §4.2, eqs. (4.24)–(4.32) · Fleisch §2.2 · the user's Classical notes, §10.2*

^def-b2-1-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.4: Operators Act on Components as Matrices
> Let $|\alpha\rangle = \sum_n a_n|e_n\rangle$ and $|\beta\rangle = \hat Q|\alpha\rangle = \sum_m b_m|e_m\rangle$ in an orthonormal basis. Then
>
> $$
> b_m = \sum_n Q_{mn}\,a_n, \qquad (\hat Q\hat R)_{mn} = \sum_k Q_{mk}R_{kn}, \qquad \hat Q = \sum_{m, n}|e_m\rangle\,Q_{mn}\,\langle e_n| .
> $$
>
> The column of components of $\hat Q|\alpha\rangle$ is the matrix $(Q_{mn})$ times the column of $|\alpha\rangle$, and the product of operators is represented by the product of matrices.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.6.1, eqs. (3.84)–(3.86), footnote 31; App. A.3 · Greensite §4.2, eq. (4.26) · Fleisch §2.2*

^thm-b2-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply $\hat Q$ to $|\alpha\rangle = \sum_na_n|e_n\rangle$ (linearity) and take the inner product with $|e_m\rangle$:
>
> $$
> b_m = \langle e_m|\beta\rangle = \sum_n a_n\langle e_m|\hat Q|e_n\rangle = \sum_n Q_{mn}a_n .
> $$
>
> For the product insert the completeness relation ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]) between the two operators: $\langle e_m|\hat Q\hat R|e_n\rangle = \sum_k\langle e_m|\hat Q|e_k\rangle\langle e_k|\hat R|e_n\rangle$. Inserting it on both sides of $\hat Q$, $\hat Q = 1\,\hat Q\,1 = \sum_{m, n}|e_m\rangle\langle e_m|\hat Q|e_n\rangle\langle e_n|$. In finite dimensions these are the statements that a linear map acts like its matrix ([[§10 Invertibility and Isomorphisms#^ladr-3-76|LADR Thm. 3.76]]) and that composition corresponds to matrix multiplication ([[§9 Matrices#^ladr-3-43|LADR Thm. 3.43]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.5: Change of Orthonormal Basis
> Let $\{|e_n\rangle\}$ and $\{|e'_m\rangle\}$ be two orthonormal bases and put $U_{mn} = \langle e'_m|e_n\rangle$. The components of a ket and the matrix elements of an operator in the two bases are related by
>
> $$
> a'_m = \sum_n U_{mn}\,a_n, \qquad Q'_{mn} = \sum_{k, l}U_{mk}\,Q_{kl}\,U^*_{nl}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \mathsf a' = \mathsf U\mathsf a, \quad \mathsf Q' = \mathsf U\mathsf Q\,\mathsf U^\dagger ,
> $$
>
> and the matrix $\mathsf U$ is **unitary**, $\mathsf U^\dagger\mathsf U = \mathsf U\mathsf U^\dagger = 1$. Matrices representing one operator in two orthonormal bases are unitarily similar; the vector itself does not change, only its components.
>
> *Source: Griffiths App. A.4, eqs. (A.61)–(A.64), Problem A.16; §3.6.3 (in Dirac notation) · Fleisch §1.2*

^thm-b2-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert the completeness relation of the old basis ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]): $a'_m = \langle e'_m|\alpha\rangle = \sum_n\langle e'_m|e_n\rangle\langle e_n|\alpha\rangle = \sum_nU_{mn}a_n$. For the operator insert it twice: $Q'_{mn} = \langle e'_m|\hat Q|e'_n\rangle = \sum_{k, l}\langle e'_m|e_k\rangle\langle e_k|\hat Q|e_l\rangle\langle e_l|e'_n\rangle$, and $\langle e_l|e'_n\rangle = \langle e'_n|e_l\rangle^* = U^*_{nl}$ ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]]), which is the $(l, n)$ entry of $\mathsf U^\dagger$. Unitarity: $(\mathsf U\mathsf U^\dagger)_{mn} = \sum_k\langle e'_m|e_k\rangle\langle e_k|e'_n\rangle = \langle e'_m|e'_n\rangle = \delta_{mn}$, and in the same way $\mathsf U^\dagger\mathsf U = 1$ with the roles of the bases exchanged. This is the change-of-basis formula of linear algebra ([[§10 Invertibility and Isomorphisms#^ladr-3-84|LADR Thm. 3.84]]) for orthonormal bases, where the change-of-basis matrix is unitary ([[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-51|LADR Def. 7.51]]).

> [!remark] Remark: One state, many wave functions
> A vector in the plane is the same arrow whether you describe it in the $xy$ axes or in rotated axes; only its components differ (Griffiths' Fig. 3.3). In the same way the state $|\Psi(t)\rangle$ is one vector of Hilbert space, and the familiar objects are its components in three different bases:
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, t) = \langle x|\Psi(t)\rangle, \qquad \Phi(p, t) = \langle p|\Psi(t)\rangle, \qquad c_n(t) = \langle n|\Psi(t)\rangle ,
> $$
>
> in the eigenbases of position, of momentum and of the Hamiltonian. They contain exactly the same information. The position and momentum "bases" are continuous and their members are not normalizable ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]); the momentum-space wave function and the passage between $\Psi$ and $\Phi$ are in [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]]. Strictly, "the wave function is the state" should always be completed by "in the position basis"; working in position space, as one mostly does, no harm comes from the shorthand. Fleisch stresses the same point: a wave function is the projection of a basis-independent state onto a chosen basis, and a column of components means nothing until the basis is named.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.6.1, eqs. (3.77)–(3.80) · Fleisch §1.2, §4.2*

> [!example] Example §B2.1.2: A Two-State System in Matrix Form
> A system has only two independent states, $|1\rangle \to (1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ and $|2\rangle \to (0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$, and Hamiltonian matrix
>
> $$
> \mathsf H = \begin{pmatrix} h & g \\ g & h\end{pmatrix}, \qquad g, h \text{ real}.
> $$
>
> It starts in $|1\rangle$. Find $|\Psi(t)\rangle$.
>
> *Stationary states.* Solve $\mathsf H|s\rangle = E|s\rangle$, the time-independent Schrödinger equation in matrix form: $\det(\mathsf H - E) = (h - E)^2 - g^2 = 0$ gives $E_\pm = h \pm g$, and $h\alpha + g\beta = (h \pm g)\alpha$ gives $\beta = \pm\alpha$, so $|s_\pm\rangle = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, \pm1)^{\mathsf T}$.
>
> *Expand and attach phases.* $|1\rangle = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(|s_+\rangle + |s_-\rangle)$, each stationary component acquires its factor $e^{-iE_\pm t/\hbar}$ ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]), and
>
> $$
> |\Psi(t)\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Bigl[e^{-i(h + g)t/\hbar}|s_+\rangle + e^{-i(h - g)t/\hbar}|s_-\rangle\Bigr] = e^{-iht/\hbar}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(gt/\hbar) \\ -i\sin(gt/\hbar)\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> The probability of finding the system in $|2\rangle$ is $\sin^2(gt/\hbar)$: the off-diagonal element $g$ makes the system oscillate between the two basis states with angular frequency $2|g|/\hbar = (E_+ - E_-)/\hbar$ in probability. (Griffiths mentions neutrino oscillation as a crude instance.)
>
> *Level A in matrix form.* The particle hopping between two wells ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^ex-a4-3-2|Example §A4.3.2]]) is this system, in the basis $|1\rangle = $ "left well", $|2\rangle = $ "right well", with $h = \tfrac32\hbar\omega$ and $g = -\tfrac12\hbar\omega$: then $E_+ = \hbar\omega$ belongs to the symmetric state $\psi_a$, $E_- = 2\hbar\omega$ to the antisymmetric $\psi_b$, and $P_{\text{right}} = \sin^2(\omega t/2)$, as found there by integrating wave functions. In the left/right basis the Hamiltonian is not diagonal; in the basis $\{|s_+\rangle, |s_-\rangle\}$ it is $\operatorname{diag}(E_+, E_-)$, and the unitary $\mathsf U$ of [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]] has rows $\langle s_\pm|$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Example 3.8, eqs. (3.87)–(3.88), footnote 34*
