Level A stated Heisenberg's relation $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2$ with an idea-level derivation from Fourier analysis that took two facts on trust ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^rem-a3-5-1|Remark: Principle or theorem?]]), and an energy–time relation that it could only use as an estimate ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]]). Both rest on the fact that operators, unlike numbers, need not commute. This section develops the commutator, proves the canonical relation $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$, and derives from the postulates an uncertainty principle for every pair of observables; Heisenberg's relation becomes one corollary, with the Gaussian as the only state of minimum uncertainty. The same commutator controls how expectation values change in time, which gives Ehrenfest's theorem, the quantum conservation laws, and a precise energy–time relation.

## Commutators

> [!definition] Definition §B2.4.1: Commutator; Compatible and Incompatible Observables
> The **commutator** of two operators $\hat A$ and $\hat B$ is
>
> $$
> [\hat A, \hat B] = \hat A\hat B - \hat B\hat A ,
> $$
>
> a measure of how badly they fail to commute; $\hat A$ and $\hat B$ **commute** if $[\hat A, \hat B] = 0$. Commutators are best computed by letting them act on a test function $f$ and dropping $f$ at the end. Two observables are **compatible** if their operators commute and **incompatible** otherwise.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.1, eq. (2.49); §3.5.1 · Greensite §7.4, eq. (7.94) · the user's Classical notes, §11.1*

^def-b2-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.1: Algebra of Commutators
> For operators $\hat A$, $\hat B$, $\hat C$ and a number $c$:
> 1. **Antisymmetry:** $[\hat B, \hat A] = -[\hat A, \hat B]$; in particular $[\hat A, \hat A] = 0$, and every operator commutes with numbers.
> 2. **Linearity:** $[\hat A + \hat B, \hat C] = [\hat A, \hat C] + [\hat B, \hat C]$ and $[c\hat A, \hat B] = c[\hat A, \hat B]$, and likewise in the second slot.
> 3. **Product rule:** $[\hat A\hat B, \hat C] = \hat A[\hat B, \hat C] + [\hat A, \hat C]\hat B$ and $[\hat A, \hat B\hat C] = [\hat A, \hat B]\hat C + \hat B[\hat A, \hat C]$, with the operators kept in the order shown.
> 4. **Jacobi identity:**
>
> $$
> [\hat A, [\hat B, \hat C]] + [\hat C, [\hat A, \hat B]] + [\hat B, [\hat C, \hat A]] = 0 .
> $$
>
> 5. **Hermitian operators:** if $\hat A$ and $\hat B$ are Hermitian, $[\hat A, \hat B]$ is anti-Hermitian. Hence $i[\hat A, \hat B]$ is Hermitian, and $\langle[\hat A, \hat B]\rangle$ is purely imaginary in every state.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.14(a), eqs. (3.64)–(3.65); Problem 3.32(d); ch. 3 footnote 19 · PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 2 (Jacobi identity)*

^thm-b2-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1–2.* $\hat B\hat A - \hat A\hat B = -(\hat A\hat B - \hat B\hat A)$; operator products distribute over sums and commute with numbers.
>
> *3.* Add and subtract $\hat A\hat C\hat B$: $\hat A\hat B\hat C - \hat C\hat A\hat B = \hat A(\hat B\hat C - \hat C\hat B) + (\hat A\hat C - \hat C\hat A)\hat B$. The second form follows the same way, or from the first with antisymmetry.
>
> *4.* Expand each double commutator into four products of three operators:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> [\hat A, [\hat B, \hat C]] &= \hat A\hat B\hat C - \hat A\hat C\hat B - \hat B\hat C\hat A + \hat C\hat B\hat A, \\
> [\hat C, [\hat A, \hat B]] &= \hat C\hat A\hat B - \hat C\hat B\hat A - \hat A\hat B\hat C + \hat B\hat A\hat C, \\
> [\hat B, [\hat C, \hat A]] &= \hat B\hat C\hat A - \hat B\hat A\hat C - \hat C\hat A\hat B + \hat A\hat C\hat B .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> Each of the six orderings appears once with $+$ and once with $-$, so the sum is zero. Nothing but associativity of operator products is used: the identity holds for any operators, and for matrices.
>
> *5.* By the reversal rule $(\hat Q\hat R)^\dagger = \hat R^\dagger\hat Q^\dagger$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]), $[\hat A, \hat B]^\dagger = \hat B\hat A - \hat A\hat B = -[\hat A, \hat B]$. Then $(i[\hat A, \hat B])^\dagger = (-i)(-[\hat A, \hat B]) = i[\hat A, \hat B]$, whose expectation value is real; so $\langle[\hat A, \hat B]\rangle = -i\langle i[\hat A, \hat B]\rangle$ is imaginary.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.2: Canonical Commutation Relations
> Position and momentum satisfy
>
> $$
> [\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar .
> $$
>
> More generally, for a differentiable function $f(x)$ and a power series $g(p)$,
>
> $$
> [f(x), \hat p] = i\hbar\,\frac{df}{dx}, \qquad [\hat x, g(\hat p)] = i\hbar\,g'(\hat p), \qquad\text{e.g.}\quad [x^n, \hat p] = i\hbar\,nx^{n-1}, \quad [\hat x, \hat p^n] = i\hbar\,n\hat p^{n-1} .
> $$
>
> For $\hat H = \hat p^2/2m + V(x)$ this gives
>
> $$
> [\hat x, \hat H] = \frac{i\hbar}{m}\,\hat p, \qquad [\hat H, \hat p] = i\hbar\,\frac{dV}{dx} .
> $$
>
> In three dimensions, $[\hat r_i, \hat p_j] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}$ and $[\hat r_i, \hat r_j] = [\hat p_i, \hat p_j] = 0$, with $\hat p_j = -i\hbar\,\partial/\partial r_j$: a coordinate fails to commute only with its own momentum.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §2.3.1, eqs. (2.51)–(2.52); Problem 3.14(b)–(c), eq. (3.66) · Greensite §7.4, eqs. (7.95)–(7.100); §21.4, eqs. (21.208), (21.218) · the user's Classical notes, §11.1*

^thm-b2-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Act on a test function $g(x)$. By the product rule of differentiation,
>
> $$
> [f(x), \hat p]\,g = f\Bigl(-i\hbar\frac{dg}{dx}\Bigr) - \Bigl(-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}\Bigr)(fg) = -i\hbar\Bigl(f\frac{dg}{dx} - f\frac{dg}{dx} - \frac{df}{dx}g\Bigr) = i\hbar\,\frac{df}{dx}\,g .
> $$
>
> Drop $g$: $[f(x), \hat p] = i\hbar f'$. With $f = x$ this is $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$, and with $f = x^n$ it gives $i\hbar nx^{n-1}$. For powers of $\hat p$ use the product rule of [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]] and induction: $[\hat x, \hat p^n] = [\hat x, \hat p]\hat p^{n-1} + \hat p[\hat x, \hat p^{n-1}] = i\hbar\hat p^{n-1} + \hat p\,i\hbar(n-1)\hat p^{n-2} = i\hbar n\hat p^{n-1}$; by linearity this extends to power series $g(\hat p)$. Then $[\hat x, \hat H] = [\hat x, \hat p^2]/2m = i\hbar\hat p/m$ ($\hat x$ commutes with $V(x)$), and $[\hat H, \hat p] = [V, \hat p] = i\hbar V'$ ($\hat p$ commutes with $\hat p^2$). In three dimensions $x\,\partial g/\partial y = \partial(xg)/\partial y$ because $x$ does not depend on $y$, so $[\hat x, \hat p_y] = 0$; derivatives with respect to different variables commute (equality of mixed partials, [[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]), so $[\hat p_i, \hat p_j] = 0$; and multiplications commute.

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: From Poisson brackets to commutators
> Classical mechanics has a bracket with the same algebra: the Poisson bracket is bilinear, antisymmetric, obeys a Leibniz rule and the Jacobi identity ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-2|CM Theorem §B8.1.2]]), and the canonical variables have $\{q_i, p_j\} = \delta_{ij}$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|CM Theorem §B8.1.1]]). Dirac's rule $\{f, g\} \to [\hat f, \hat g]/i\hbar$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^rem-b8-1-5|CM Remark: From brackets to commutators]]) turns these into Theorem §B2.4.2, and the classical equation of motion $df/dt = \{f, H\} + \partial f/\partial t$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|CM Theorem §B8.1.3]]) into [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]] for expectation values. Greensite runs the logic the other way. He takes the basis-free relation $[\hat X, \hat P] = i\hbar$ as the fundamental postulate ("canonical quantization"), motivates it by requiring Ehrenfest's equations $\langle\partial H/\partial P\rangle = d\langle X\rangle/dt$, $\langle-\partial H/\partial X\rangle = d\langle P\rangle/dt$, which lead to $[\hat X, \hat H] = i\hbar\,\partial\hat H/\partial\hat P$ and $[\hat P, \hat H] = -i\hbar\,\partial\hat H/\partial\hat X$, and then recovers $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$ as the only form of $\hat P$ in the basis of position eigenstates compatible with the commutator. Either way, the step from classical to quantum is a prescription, not a derivation.
>
> *Source: Greensite §21.4–21.5, eqs. (21.197)–(21.218)*

## The generalized uncertainty principle

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.3: Generalized Uncertainty Principle
> For any two observables $A$, $B$ and any normalized state $\Psi$ (in the domains of the operators),
>
> $$
> \sigma_A^2\,\sigma_B^2 \ge \Bigl(\frac{1}{2i}\bigl\langle[\hat A, \hat B]\bigr\rangle\Bigr)^2 ,
> $$
>
> where $\sigma_A$, $\sigma_B$ are the standard deviations in the state $\Psi$. The quantity in parentheses is real (the $i$ cancels the $i$ carried by the commutator of two Hermitian operators), so the right side is non-negative. Every pair of incompatible observables has an uncertainty relation; for compatible ones the bound is zero.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.5.1, eqs. (3.59)–(3.62), footnote 19 · Greensite §7.4, eq. (7.116) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 9, §2*

^thm-b2-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Variances as norms.* Put $f = (\hat A - \langle A\rangle)\Psi$ and $g = (\hat B - \langle B\rangle)\Psi$. As in [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]], $\sigma_A^2 = \langle f|f\rangle$ and $\sigma_B^2 = \langle g|g\rangle$.
>
> *Schwarz.* By the Schwarz inequality ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]]; Griffiths' eq. (3.59)),
>
> $$
> \sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2 = \langle f|f\rangle\langle g|g\rangle \ge |\langle f|g\rangle|^2 .
> $$
>
> *Keep the imaginary part* (Griffiths' eqs. (3.60)–(3.61)). For any complex $z$, $|z|^2 = (\operatorname{Re}z)^2 + (\operatorname{Im}z)^2 \ge (\operatorname{Im}z)^2 = \bigl[\tfrac{1}{2i}(z - z^*)\bigr]^2$. With $z = \langle f|g\rangle$ and $z^* = \langle g|f\rangle$:
>
> $$
> \sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2 \ge \Bigl(\frac{1}{2i}\bigl[\langle f|g\rangle - \langle g|f\rangle\bigr]\Bigr)^2 .
> $$
>
> *Evaluate.* $\hat A - \langle A\rangle$ is Hermitian, so it can be moved to the ket; $\langle A\rangle$, $\langle B\rangle$ are numbers and $\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle = 1$:
>
> $$
> \langle f|g\rangle = \bigl\langle\Psi\bigm|(\hat A - \langle A\rangle)(\hat B - \langle B\rangle)\Psi\bigr\rangle = \langle\hat A\hat B\rangle - \langle B\rangle\langle A\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle + \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle = \langle\hat A\hat B\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle .
> $$
>
> Likewise $\langle g|f\rangle = \langle\hat B\hat A\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle$, so $\langle f|g\rangle - \langle g|f\rangle = \langle[\hat A, \hat B]\rangle$. Substituting gives the theorem. That the bracket is real is part 5 of [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-1|Theorem §B2.4.1]].

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: A stronger form
> The proof threw away $(\operatorname{Re}z)^2$. Keeping it, $\operatorname{Re}\langle f|g\rangle = \tfrac12(\langle f|g\rangle + \langle g|f\rangle) = \tfrac12\langle\hat A\hat B + \hat B\hat A\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle$, which gives the stronger (Robertson–Schrödinger) inequality
>
> $$
> \sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2 \ge \frac14\bigl(\langle\hat C\rangle^2 + \langle\hat D\rangle^2\bigr), \qquad \hat C = -i[\hat A, \hat B], \quad \hat D = \hat A\hat B + \hat B\hat A - 2\langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle .
> $$
>
> The extra term measures the correlation of $A$ and $B$ in the state; Greensite's proof arrives at this form before discarding it. For $B = A$ it reduces to $\sigma_A^4 \ge \sigma_A^4$, which says nothing new.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.43, eq. (3.115) · Greensite §7.4, eqs. (7.123)–(7.126)*

^rem-b2-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.4: Heisenberg's Uncertainty Relation as a Corollary
> For every state (in the domain of $\hat x$ and $\hat p$),
>
> $$
> \sigma_x\,\sigma_p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2} .
> $$
>
> Equivalently, without $\hbar$: for every square-integrable $f(x)$ whose densities $|f(x)|^2$ and $|\tilde f(k)|^2$ have finite standard deviations $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_k$ (with $\tilde f$ the Fourier transform), $\sigma_x\sigma_k \ge \tfrac12$. Components along different axes are unconstrained: $[\hat x, \hat p_y] = 0$ gives the bound $\sigma_x\sigma_{p_y} \ge 0$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.5.1, eq. (3.63) · Greensite §7.4, eq. (7.127); §6.2 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 9 · the user's Classical notes, §11.2*

^thm-b2-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take $\hat A = \hat x$, $\hat B = \hat p$ in [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]] and use $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]]): $\sigma_x^2\sigma_p^2 \ge \bigl(\tfrac{1}{2i}\,i\hbar\bigr)^2 = (\hbar/2)^2$; standard deviations are non-negative, so take the square root. For the Fourier form, normalize $f$ and regard it as a wave function. Its momentum density is $|\Phi(p)|^2$ ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-6|Theorem §B2.3.6]]), and $\Phi(p) \propto \tilde f(p/\hbar)$, so the distribution of $p$ is the distribution of $k$ rescaled by $\hbar$: $\sigma_p = \hbar\sigma_k$. Divide the first inequality by $\hbar$. (The overall factor and the sign convention of the transform change neither the shape of $|\tilde f|^2$ nor its width.) The last statement is the theorem with $[\hat x, \hat p_y] = 0$.

> [!remark] Remark: What changed since level A
> Level A's [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]] was a theorem only in outline: it borrowed from Fourier analysis the sharp inequality $\sigma_x\sigma_k \ge \tfrac12$ ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-5|Theorem §A3.3.5]], where it was stated) and the reading of $|\phi(p)|^2$ as the momentum density, and promised a proof "from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality". Both gaps are now closed: the momentum density is [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-6|Theorem §B2.3.6]], and the relation is the special case $\hat A = \hat x$, $\hat B = \hat p$ of the generalized uncertainty principle, which rests only on the measurement postulate, Hermiticity, the canonical commutator and the Schwarz inequality. The **layer changes**: Heisenberg's relation is a corollary, the Fourier inequality is the same corollary divided by $\hbar$, and nothing about uncertainty needs to be assumed. As Griffiths stresses, the uncertainty principle is "not an extra assumption in quantum theory, but rather a consequence of the statistical interpretation". Its $\hbar$-free form is the duration–bandwidth inequality $\Delta t\,\Delta\omega \ge \tfrac12$ for all square-integrable signals, which Oscillations, Waves and Optics quoted without proof ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^rem-b4-4-1|WO Remark: The general inequality, and the quantum case]]); with $t$, $\omega$ in place of $x$, $k$ the proof above applies word for word.
>
> Why can't one measure both? A position measurement collapses the state to a narrow spike ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]), which contains a broad range of wavelengths; a following momentum measurement collapses it to a long, nearly sinusoidal packet, and the position just found is lost. The second measurement makes the first obsolete. It could leave the first value intact only if the state were an eigenstate of both observables, which for incompatible observables is impossible in general (Theorem §B2.4.6).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.5.1 (pp. 139), footnote 21*

^rem-b2-4-3

> [!example] Example §B2.4.1: Position and Energy
> Find the uncertainty relation between position and energy for a particle with $\hat H = \hat p^2/2m + V(x)$.
>
> By [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]], $[\hat x, \hat H] = i\hbar\hat p/m$, so [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]] gives
>
> $$
> \sigma_x^2\sigma_H^2 \ge \Bigl(\frac{1}{2i}\,\frac{i\hbar}{m}\langle p\rangle\Bigr)^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sigma_x\,\sigma_H \ge \frac{\hbar}{2m}\,|\langle p\rangle| .
> $$
>
> The bound depends on the state: unlike $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$, the commutator here is an operator, not a number. In a stationary state it says nothing: there $\sigma_H = 0$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]), and consistently $\langle p\rangle = m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt = 0$ because every expectation value is constant ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-5|Theorem §A4.1.5]], [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]]), so the inequality reads $0 \ge 0$. Only a state that is moving, a superposition of different energies, has $\langle p\rangle \ne 0$, and then it must have an energy spread. This is the energy–time relation for $Q = x$ in disguise (Theorem §B2.4.9).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 3.15, 3.22; solved here*

^ex-b2-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.5: Minimum-Uncertainty Wave Packets
> A state has $\sigma_x\sigma_p = \hbar/2$ exactly when it is a Gaussian,
>
> $$
> \Psi(x) = A\,e^{-a(x - \langle x\rangle)^2/2\hbar}\,e^{i\langle p\rangle x/\hbar}, \qquad a > 0 \text{ real},
> $$
>
> with any centre $\langle x\rangle$ and mean momentum $\langle p\rangle$. Its widths are $\sigma_x^2 = \hbar/2a$ and $\sigma_p^2 = \hbar a/2$; with $a = \hbar/2\sigma_x^2$ the envelope is $e^{-(x - \langle x\rangle)^2/4\sigma_x^2}$, the form used in [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]]. Minimality is a property of the state at one instant: a state that is Gaussian at $t = 0$ need not stay of this form.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.5.2, eqs. (3.68)–(3.70), Problem 3.17, footnote 22*

^thm-b2-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Equality in [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]] requires equality at both inequalities of its proof. (i) In the Schwarz inequality, equality holds exactly when one vector is a multiple of the other ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]]): $g = cf$ for a complex $c$ ($f = 0$ would mean $\sigma_x = 0$, impossible for a normalizable state, [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]). (ii) Dropping $(\operatorname{Re}z)^2$ costs nothing exactly when $\operatorname{Re}\langle f|g\rangle = \operatorname{Re}(c\langle f|f\rangle) = 0$; since $\langle f|f\rangle > 0$, $c$ must be imaginary, $c = ia$ with $a$ real. For $\hat A = \hat x$, $\hat B = \hat p$ the condition $g = iaf$ is the differential equation
>
> $$
> \Bigl(-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx} - \langle p\rangle\Bigr)\Psi = ia\,(x - \langle x\rangle)\Psi \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{d\Psi}{dx} = \Bigl[\frac{i\langle p\rangle}{\hbar} - \frac{a}{\hbar}(x - \langle x\rangle)\Bigr]\Psi ,
> $$
>
> whose solution (integrate $\Psi'/\Psi$) is the Gaussian stated. It is normalizable only for $a > 0$. Conversely, for $a > 0$ the Gaussian satisfies $g = iaf$ with the constants $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle p\rangle$ equal to its actual means ($|\Psi|^2$ is symmetric about $\langle x\rangle$, and the real envelope times $e^{i\langle p\rangle x/\hbar}$ has mean momentum $\langle p\rangle$ by [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-5|Theorem §A3.4.5]]), so both steps are equalities. *Widths.* $|\Psi|^2 \propto e^{-a(x - \langle x\rangle)^2/\hbar}$ is a normal density with variance $\hbar/2a$; then $\sigma_p = \hbar/2\sigma_x$ gives $\sigma_p^2 = \hbar a/2$.

> [!remark] Remark: Which states are minimal

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.6: Compatible Observables Have Common Eigenfunctions
> Let $\hat A$ and $\hat B$ be Hermitian with discrete spectra and finite-dimensional eigenspaces.
> 1. If $\hat A$ and $\hat B$ have a complete set of common eigenfunctions, they commute.
> 2. Conversely, if $[\hat A, \hat B] = 0$, then $\hat B$ maps every eigenspace of $\hat A$ into itself, and there is a complete orthonormal set of common eigenfunctions.
>
> So incompatible observables cannot have a complete set of common eigenfunctions (position and momentum have none at all), while compatible ones can be given sharp values together. In hydrogen, the Hamiltonian, the square of the angular momentum and its $z$-component are mutually compatible, and the states are labelled by all three eigenvalues ([[§B5.2 Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]], [[§B5.4 The Hydrogen Atom]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.5.1 (p. 139), Problem 3.16, footnote 20 · Greensite §7.4 ("the commutator theorem", eqs. (7.101)–(7.115))*

^thm-b2-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* If $\hat Af_n = a_nf_n$ and $\hat Bf_n = b_nf_n$ for a complete set $\{f_n\}$, then $[\hat A, \hat B]f_n = (a_nb_n - b_na_n)f_n = 0$. Every $f$ in Hilbert space is $\sum c_nf_n$, so by linearity $[\hat A, \hat B]f = 0$ for all $f$: the commutator is the zero operator.
>
> *2.* Let $\hat Af = af$. Then $\hat A(\hat Bf) = \hat B\hat Af = a(\hat Bf)$, so $\hat Bf$ is again in the eigenspace $E_a$ of $\hat A$ (or zero). If $a$ is nondegenerate, $E_a$ is one-dimensional and $\hat Bf = bf$: every eigenfunction of $\hat A$ is one of $\hat B$ (Greensite's case). In general, $\hat B$ restricted to the finite-dimensional space $E_a$ is a Hermitian operator on it, so by the spectral theorem ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]], [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]) $E_a$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $\hat B$; each is still an eigenvector of $\hat A$ with eigenvalue $a$. Collecting these bases over all eigenvalues $a$ gives an orthonormal set ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]) that contains a basis of every eigenspace of $\hat A$, hence is complete because the eigenfunctions of $\hat A$ are ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]]). This is the infinite-dimensional version of [[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-75|LADR Thm. 5.75]] and [[§18 Commuting Operators#^ladr-5-76|LADR Thm. 5.76]]. *Position and momentum:* a common eigenfunction would satisfy $[\hat x, \hat p]f = 0$, but $[\hat x, \hat p]f = i\hbar f \ne 0$.

## Time dependence and conservation laws

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.7: Time Evolution of Expectation Values
> For any operator $\hat Q$, possibly depending explicitly on time, and a state obeying the Schrödinger equation ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-4|Principle §B2.3.4]]),
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\langle Q\rangle = \frac{i}{\hbar}\bigl\langle[\hat H, \hat Q]\bigr\rangle + \Bigl\langle\frac{\partial\hat Q}{\partial t}\Bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> Only the Hermiticity of $\hat H$ is used, so the formula holds also for non-Hermitian $\hat Q$. Special cases ($\hat H = \hat p^2/2m + V$):
> - $\hat Q = 1$: $d\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle/dt = 0$, normalization is conserved ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]);
> - $\hat Q = \hat H$: $d\langle H\rangle/dt = \langle\partial\hat H/\partial t\rangle$, so energy is conserved when $V$ does not depend on time;
> - $\hat Q = \hat x$ and $\hat p$: $\dfrac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \dfrac{\langle p\rangle}{m}$, $\dfrac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = -\Bigl\langle\dfrac{dV}{dx}\Bigr\rangle$, which is Ehrenfest's theorem ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]).
>
> Griffiths calls this the **generalized Ehrenfest theorem**. It is the quantum counterpart of $df/dt = \{f, H\} + \partial f/\partial t$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|CM Theorem §B8.1.3]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.5.3, eq. (3.73), footnote 24, Problems 3.18–3.19 · PHY 308, HW 2, Problem 4 · Greensite §21.4, eqs. (21.201)–(21.207)*

^thm-b2-4-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate $\langle Q\rangle = \langle\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle$ with the product rule (under the integral sign; legitimate for the smooth, decaying wave functions considered):
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\langle Q\rangle = \Bigl\langle\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}\Bigm|\hat Q\Psi\Bigr\rangle + \Bigl\langle\Psi\Bigm|\frac{\partial\hat Q}{\partial t}\Psi\Bigr\rangle + \Bigl\langle\Psi\Bigm|\hat Q\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}\Bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> The Schrödinger equation gives $\partial\Psi/\partial t = \hat H\Psi/i\hbar$. In the first term the constant $1/i\hbar$ leaves the bra conjugated, as $(1/i\hbar)^* = -1/i\hbar$ ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\langle Q\rangle = -\frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle\hat H\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle + \frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle\Psi|\hat Q\hat H\Psi\rangle + \Bigl\langle\frac{\partial\hat Q}{\partial t}\Bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> $\hat H$ is Hermitian, so $\langle\hat H\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle = \langle\Psi|\hat H\hat Q\Psi\rangle$, and the first two terms combine to $\frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle[\hat Q, \hat H]\rangle = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[\hat H, \hat Q]\rangle$. *Special cases.* $[\hat H, 1] = [\hat H, \hat H] = 0$. With [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]], $[\hat H, \hat x] = -i\hbar\hat p/m$ gives $\frac{i}{\hbar}\cdot\frac{-i\hbar}{m}\langle p\rangle = \langle p\rangle/m$, and $[\hat H, \hat p] = i\hbar V'$ gives $\frac{i}{\hbar}\cdot i\hbar\langle V'\rangle = -\langle V'\rangle$.

*Uses:* [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-4|Principle §B2.3.4]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]], [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-1|Theorem §B2.1.1]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.8: Conservation Laws
> Let $\hat Q$ have no explicit time dependence and commute with the Hamiltonian, $[\hat H, \hat Q] = 0$. Then $\langle Q\rangle$ is constant in time, and so is the probability of every outcome $q$ of a measurement of $Q$: the whole distribution of $Q$ is conserved, and $Q$ is a **conserved quantity**. In particular:
> - if $V$ does not depend on time, the energy probabilities $|c_n|^2$ are constant ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-4|Theorem §A4.3.4]] for the mean);
> - for a free particle ($V$ constant), $[\hat H, \hat p] = 0$, and the momentum distribution $|\Phi(p, t)|^2$ does not change (as found directly in [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]]);
> - in a stationary state every $\langle Q\rangle$ with $\partial\hat Q/\partial t = 0$ is constant, whether $Q$ is conserved or not, because $\langle[\hat H, \hat Q]\rangle = E\langle\hat Q\rangle - E\langle\hat Q\rangle = 0$ there ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.5.3 (after eq. (3.73)), Problem 3.12 · Greensite ch. 6*

^thm-b2-4-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Constancy of $\langle Q\rangle$ is [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]] with both terms zero. For the probabilities (discrete spectrum), let $\hat P_q = \sum_j|f_{q,j}\rangle\langle f_{q,j}|$ project onto the eigenspace $E_q$ of $\hat Q$; then $P(q) = \langle\Psi|\hat P_q\Psi\rangle$ ([[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]]). Because $\hat H$ commutes with $\hat Q$, it maps $E_q$ into itself ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]], part 2), and, being Hermitian, it also maps the orthogonal complement into itself: for $g \perp E_q$ and $f \in E_q$, $\langle f|\hat Hg\rangle = \langle\hat Hf|g\rangle = 0$. Splitting any $\psi = \hat P_q\psi + (1 - \hat P_q)\psi$ then shows $\hat P_q\hat H\psi = \hat H\hat P_q\psi$, so $[\hat H, \hat P_q] = 0$ and Theorem §B2.4.7 applied to $\hat P_q$ gives $dP(q)/dt = 0$. For the stationary state $\Psi = \psi e^{-iEt/\hbar}$: $\langle\Psi|\hat H\hat Q\Psi\rangle = \langle\hat H\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle = E\langle Q\rangle$ (Hermiticity) and $\langle\Psi|\hat Q\hat H\Psi\rangle = E\langle Q\rangle$.

*Uses:* [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-6|Theorem §B2.4.6]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]], [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Conservation laws, classical and quantum
> Classically a function $f(q, p)$ is a constant of the motion exactly when $\{f, H\} = 0$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|CM Theorem §B8.1.3]]), and Noether's theorem ties each such constant to a symmetry ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|CM Theorem §B6.4.2]]). Quantum mechanically the condition is $[\hat H, \hat Q] = 0$, and the conclusion is stronger: not just a number but a whole probability distribution stays fixed. The sharpest version concerns stationary states: since $\hat H$ and a conserved $\hat Q$ have common eigenfunctions (Theorem §B2.4.6), stationary states can be labelled by the eigenvalues of conserved quantities, as hydrogen's are by $n$, $\ell$, $m$. Symmetries as generators of unitary transformations, and the quantum Noether theorem, are level C (planned).

> [!example]- ★ Example §B2.4.2: The Virial Theorem
> Apply Theorem §B2.4.7 to $\hat Q = \hat x\hat p$ (not Hermitian, which does not matter here). With the product rule and [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]],
>
> $$
> [\hat H, \hat x\hat p] = [\hat H, \hat x]\,\hat p + \hat x\,[\hat H, \hat p] = -\frac{i\hbar}{m}\hat p^2 + i\hbar\,x\frac{dV}{dx} ,
> $$
>
> so, with $T = p^2/2m$ the kinetic energy,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\langle xp\rangle = \frac{i}{\hbar}\Bigl(-\frac{i\hbar}{m}\langle p^2\rangle + i\hbar\Bigl\langle x\frac{dV}{dx}\Bigr\rangle\Bigr) = 2\langle T\rangle - \Bigl\langle x\frac{dV}{dx}\Bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> In a stationary state every expectation value is constant (Theorem §B2.4.8), so the left side vanishes and
>
> $$
> 2\langle T\rangle = \Bigl\langle x\frac{dV}{dx}\Bigr\rangle ,
> $$
>
> the quantum **virial theorem**. For the oscillator, $V = \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$ gives $x\,dV/dx = 2V$, hence $\langle T\rangle = \langle V\rangle = \tfrac12E_n$ in every stationary state: kinetic and potential energy share the energy equally on average, as for a classical oscillator over a period ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-6|CM Theorem §B3.1.6]], where the average is over time instead of over the state). For the ground state this checks with $\langle p^2\rangle/2m = m\omega\hbar/4m = \tfrac14\hbar\omega$ from [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^ex-b2-3-2|Example §B2.3.2]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.37, eqs. (3.112)–(3.113); solved here*

## The energy–time uncertainty principle

Position, momentum and energy are dynamical variables, measured on the system at a given time; time is the parameter on which they depend. Nobody measures "the time of a particle", so the $\Delta t$ of an energy–time relation cannot be a standard deviation of time measurements, and the relation is not a case of Theorem §B2.4.3 with $\hat B = \hat t$. In a relativistic theory it would accompany the position–momentum relation, $x$ and $ct$ forming one four-vector as $p$ and $E/c$ do, but the Schrödinger equation treats $x$ and $t$ unequally, and there it must be derived separately.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.4.9: Energy–Time Uncertainty Principle
> For any observable $Q$ without explicit time dependence,
>
> $$
> \sigma_H\,\sigma_Q \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}\,\Bigl|\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt}\Bigr| .
> $$
>
> With $\Delta E = \sigma_H$ and $\Delta t = \sigma_Q/|d\langle Q\rangle/dt|$, the time it takes $\langle Q\rangle$ to change by one standard deviation,
>
> $$
> \Delta E\,\Delta t \ge \frac{\hbar}{2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.5.3, eqs. (3.74)–(3.76), footnote 25 · Greensite §7.5*

^thm-b2-4-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take $\hat A = \hat H$, $\hat B = \hat Q$ in [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]]. By [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]] with $\partial\hat Q/\partial t = 0$, $\langle[\hat H, \hat Q]\rangle = (\hbar/i)\,d\langle Q\rangle/dt$, so
>
> $$
> \sigma_H^2\sigma_Q^2 \ge \Bigl(\frac{1}{2i}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{i}\,\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt}\Bigr)^2 = \Bigl(\frac{\hbar}{2}\Bigr)^2\Bigl(\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt}\Bigr)^2 .
> $$
>
> Take square roots and divide by $|d\langle Q\rangle/dt|$.

*Uses:* [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]], [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-7|Theorem §B2.4.7]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the Δt means
> - $\Delta t$ depends on the observable: some quantities change quickly, others slowly. If $\Delta E$ is small, every observable changes slowly; if any observable changes fast, the energy spread must be large. A stationary state has $\Delta E = 0$, and indeed nothing in it changes ($\Delta t = \infty$).
> - Griffiths' three readings of $\Delta t$: the period of a two-state oscillation, $\tau = 2\pi\hbar/(E_2 - E_1)$, with $\Delta E \approx E_2 - E_1$ (then $\Delta E\,\Delta t \approx 2\pi\hbar$; Example §B2.4.3 below does it exactly); the time a free packet takes to pass a point, $\Delta t = m\Delta x/p$, with $\Delta E = p\Delta p/m$, so $\Delta E\,\Delta t = \Delta x\,\Delta p$; and the lifetime of an unstable particle: the $\Delta(1232)$ lives about $10^{-23}\ \mathrm s$, and its measured mass spreads over about $120\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, so $\Delta E\,\Delta t \approx (60\ \mathrm{MeV})(10^{-23}\ \mathrm s) = 6\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{MeV\,s}$, close to $\hbar/2 = 3.3\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{MeV\,s}$. (Griffiths admits the logic is backwards: such lifetimes are in practice inferred from the width.)
> - **Layer change.** Level A's [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]] rested on the Fourier duration–bandwidth idea and gave $\Delta t$ no precise meaning; here $\Delta t$ is defined and the inequality proved. The natural line width ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^ex-a3-5-4|Example §A3.5.4]]) uses the lifetime as $\Delta t$, in the spirit of the third reading; deriving the decay itself needs time-dependent perturbation theory (level C, planned).
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.5.3, Examples 3.5–3.7, footnote 26*

^rem-b2-4-6

> [!caution] Caution: Energy is not borrowed
> A popular reading says the energy–time relation lets energy conservation be violated by $\Delta E$ for a time of order $\hbar/\Delta E$. Griffiths: "there are many legitimate readings of the energy–time uncertainty principle, but this is not one of them". Nothing in the derivation licenses a violation, and quantum mechanics conserves energy (Theorem §B2.4.8). Level A used this language for virtual pions and the range of the nuclear force ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^rem-a3-5-5|Remark: Borrowing energy for a short time]]); read that as an order-of-magnitude estimate of the range, which the uncertainty principle is robust enough to give even when misused, not as a statement about energy conservation.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.5.3 (p. 145)*

> [!example] Example §B2.4.3: The Energy–Time Relation Tested Exactly
> A particle in the infinite well is in the equal superposition $\Psi = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl[\psi_1e^{-i\omega t} + \psi_2e^{-4i\omega t}\bigr]$, $\omega = E_1/\hbar$, whose position level A worked out ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^ex-a4-3-1|Example §A4.3.1]]). Test Theorem §B2.4.9 with $Q = x$.
>
> *Energy spread.* $E_1$ and $4E_1$ each have probability $\tfrac12$: $\langle H\rangle = \tfrac52E_1$, $\langle H^2\rangle = \tfrac{17}2E_1^2$, so $\sigma_H = \tfrac32E_1 = \tfrac32\hbar\omega$.
>
> *Position.* From level A, $\langle x\rangle = \dfrac L2 - \dfrac{16L}{9\pi^2}\cos3\omega t$, so $\dfrac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \dfrac{16L\omega}{3\pi^2}\sin3\omega t$. For $\langle x^2\rangle$, use $\langle x^2\rangle_n = L^2\bigl(\tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{2n^2\pi^2}\bigr)$ for the box states and the cross term $\int_0^Lx^2\psi_1\psi_2\,dx = \tfrac1L\int_0^Lx^2\bigl[\cos\tfrac{\pi x}{L} - \cos\tfrac{3\pi x}{L}\bigr]dx = -\tfrac{16L^2}{9\pi^2}$ (with $\int_0^Lx^2\cos\tfrac{j\pi x}{L}dx = \tfrac{2L^3(-1)^j}{j^2\pi^2}$, two integrations by parts):
>
> $$
> \langle x^2\rangle = L^2\Bigl(\frac13 - \frac{5}{16\pi^2}\Bigr) - \frac{16L^2}{9\pi^2}\cos3\omega t, \qquad \sigma_x^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2 = L^2\Bigl[\frac1{12} - \frac{5}{16\pi^2} - \frac{256}{81\pi^4}\cos^23\omega t\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The terms linear in $\cos3\omega t$ cancel. So $\sigma_x$ oscillates between $0.139L$ and $0.227L$.
>
> *The test.* Since $\tfrac94\cdot\tfrac{256}{81\pi^4} = \tfrac14\bigl(\tfrac{16}{3\pi^2}\bigr)^2 = \tfrac{64}{9\pi^4}$, the time dependence drops out of the difference of the two sides:
>
> $$
> \sigma_H^2\sigma_x^2 - \frac{\hbar^2}{4}\Bigl(\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt}\Bigr)^2 = \hbar^2\omega^2L^2\Bigl[\frac{3}{16} - \frac{45}{64\pi^2} - \frac{64}{9\pi^4}\Bigr] = 0.0433\,\hbar^2\omega^2L^2 > 0 ,
> $$
>
> at every instant. The inequality holds with a constant margin (figure). In terms of $\Delta t = \sigma_x/|d\langle x\rangle/dt|$, $\Delta E\,\Delta t$ is smallest when $\langle x\rangle$ moves fastest, $\sin3\omega t = \pm1$, where it equals $0.631\,\hbar$; at the turning points of $\langle x\rangle$, $\Delta t \to \infty$. Griffiths' rough estimate $\Delta E \approx E_2 - E_1$, $\Delta t \approx$ period gives $2\pi\hbar$, an order of magnitude more: the precise relation is much closer to its bound.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.20 (with the state of Problem 2.5); solved here*
