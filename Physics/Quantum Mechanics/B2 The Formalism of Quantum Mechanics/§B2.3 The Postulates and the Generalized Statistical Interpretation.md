Level A assumed three separate rules for reading a wave function: the Born rule for position ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-1|Principle §A3.4.1]]), the sandwich rule for averages of momentum and energy ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]]), and the rule that an energy measurement on a superposition gives $E_n$ with probability $|c_n|^2$ and leaves the particle in $\psi_n$ ([[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^pr-a4-3-3|Principle §A4.3.3]]); the Schrödinger equation ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]]) moved the wave function in time. With Hilbert space ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation|§B2.1]]) and Hermitian operators ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators|§B2.2]]) in hand, all of this follows from four postulates: what a state is, what an observable is, what a measurement yields, and how a state evolves. The third, Griffiths' *generalized statistical interpretation*, covers every observable at once; level A's three rules become special cases, and the average rule becomes a theorem. Applied to momentum it gives the momentum-space wave function $\Phi(p, t)$, which makes precise level A's "momentum language" ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^rem-a3-4-6|Remark: Why this rule, and why its layer]]).

## The postulates

> [!principle] Principle §B2.3.1: States
> The state of a system at time $t$ is a normalized vector $|\Psi(t)\rangle$ of a Hilbert space ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]]); two vectors that differ only by a constant phase factor $e^{i\alpha}$ describe the same state. For one particle in one dimension the space is $L^2(\mathbb R)$, and the wave function of level A is the position-space component of the state,
>
> $$
> \Psi(x, t) = \langle x|\Psi(t)\rangle .
> $$
>
> The state contains everything that can be known about the system: there are no further "hidden" variables.
>
> *Domain: non-relativistic quantum mechanics; states known completely ("pure states"; statistical mixtures need density operators, level C: [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^def-c11-1-1|Def. §C11.1.1]], [[§C11.1 Pure and Mixed States and the Density Operator#^def-c11-1-2|Def. §C11.1.2]]). Spin adds components to the state (Chapter B6); several particles need product spaces (Chapter B7).*
> *Source: Griffiths §3.1, eq. (3.5); §3.6.1, eq. (3.77), footnote 27 · Greensite §21.4, item 2 · Fleisch §4.1 ("information content"), §4.2*

^pr-b2-3-1

> [!principle] Principle §B2.3.2: Observables
> Every observable $Q$ is represented by a Hermitian (self-adjoint) operator $\hat Q$ whose eigenfunctions are complete ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]]). For an observable $Q(x, p, t)$ of classical mechanics, $\hat Q$ is obtained, in position space, by the replacements
>
> $$
> x \to \hat x = x, \qquad p \to \hat p = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
> $$
>
> of [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^def-a3-4-5|Def. §A3.4.5]]. The basis-free form of this rule is the canonical commutator $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$ ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-2|Theorem §B2.4.2]]).
>
> *Domain: as Principle §B2.3.1. When $Q$ contains a product such as $xp$, the order of the noncommuting $\hat x$, $\hat p$ is ambiguous and must be fixed by another consideration (such observables are rare). Observables without a classical counterpart, such as spin (Chapter B6), are represented by Hermitian operators not obtained by this replacement.*
> *Source: Griffiths §3.2.1, eq. (3.18); §3.4, footnote 15 · Greensite §21.4, item 3, eqs. (21.217)–(21.218)*

^pr-b2-3-2

> [!principle] Principle §B2.3.3: The Generalized Statistical Interpretation
> If an observable $Q$ is measured on a system in the normalized state $\Psi$:
> 1. **Outcomes.** The result is certain to be one of the eigenvalues of $\hat Q$.
> 2. **Discrete spectrum.** The probability of the eigenvalue $q_n$ with orthonormal eigenfunction $f_n$ is
>
> $$
> P(q_n) = |c_n|^2, \qquad c_n = \langle f_n|\Psi\rangle = \int f_n(x)^*\,\Psi(x, t)\,dx .
> $$
>
>    If $q$ is degenerate, with orthonormal eigenfunctions $f_{q,1}, \dots, f_{q,g}$ spanning its eigenspace, $P(q) = \sum_{j=1}^{g}|\langle f_{q,j}|\Psi\rangle|^2$.
> 3. **Continuous spectrum.** If the eigenvalues $q(z)$ are labelled by a continuous variable with Dirac-orthonormal eigenfunctions $f_z$, the probability of a result in the range $dz$ is
>
> $$
> |c(z)|^2\,dz, \qquad c(z) = \langle f_z|\Psi\rangle .
> $$
>
> 4. **Collapse.** Immediately after a measurement that gave $q_n$, the state is the eigenfunction $f_n$ (for a degenerate $q$: the normalized part of $\Psi$ in its eigenspace, $\sum_j c_{q,j}f_{q,j}/\sqrt{\sum_j|c_{q,j}|^2}$; for a continuous spectrum: a narrow packet around the value found, as narrow as the precision of the apparatus). An immediately repeated measurement of $Q$ gives the same result.
>
> *Domain: ideal measurements on a single system in a pure state, in the Copenhagen interpretation, which is the one the sources use. How a measurement produces the collapse is not explained by the postulate and remains debated.*
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4, eqs. (3.43)–(3.44), footnotes 16, 18 · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 9, Measurement Postulate, eqs. (1.21)–(1.24) · Fleisch §4.1–4.2, eq. (4.1) · Greensite §7.1*

^pr-b2-3-3

> [!principle] Principle §B2.3.4: Time Evolution
> Between measurements the state evolves by the Schrödinger equation
>
> $$
> i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi(t)\rangle = \hat H|\Psi(t)\rangle ,
> $$
>
> where $\hat H$ is the Hamiltonian, the operator of the total energy. For one particle in one dimension, $\hat H = \hat p^2/2m + V(x, t)$, and in position space the equation is level A's Schrödinger equation ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|Principle §A4.1.1]], in the operator form of [[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^rem-a4-1-6|Remark: Operator form]]).
>
> *Domain: as Principle §B2.3.1, with a given Hamiltonian; the evolution is continuous and deterministic, in contrast with the random jump of Principle §B2.3.3.*
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4 (introduction); §3.6.1, eq. (3.87), footnote 33 · Greensite §21.4, item 4, eq. (21.219) · Fleisch §4.1 ("time evolution")*

^pr-b2-3-4

> [!remark] Remark: How the postulates fit together, and where they come from
> - Griffiths calls the generalized statistical interpretation, together with the Schrödinger equation, "the foundation of quantum mechanics"; Greensite packs the same content into "canonical quantization": start from classical coordinates and momenta, make states vectors in Hilbert space, observables Hermitian operators with $[\hat q_a, \hat p_b] = i\hbar\delta_{ab}$, evolve by the Schrödinger equation, and compute expectation values as $\langle\Psi|\hat Q|\Psi\rangle$. Fleisch lists the tenets usually attributed to the Copenhagen interpretation: complete information in the state, smooth Schrödinger evolution unless a measurement is made, collapse to an eigenstate, eigenvalues as results, the Born rule, the uncertainty principle, correspondence and complementarity. In the list above the uncertainty principle is not a postulate: it is derived ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-3|Theorem §B2.4.3]]).
> - PHY 308 presented "the six postulates of quantum mechanics" in lecture (syllabus, Chapter 4); the lectures exist only in a form without typed text, so the instructor's list is not reproduced here, and the postulates above follow Griffiths, Greensite and Zwiebach.
> - The interpretation of measurement, already flagged at level A ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^rem-a3-4-3|Remark: Measurement and the Copenhagen interpretation]]), is left at that: the postulates say what is measured and with what probability, not how.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4 (introduction) · Greensite §21.4, eqs. (21.217)–(21.220) · Fleisch §4.1 · PHY 308, syllabus*

^rem-b2-3-1

> [!remark]- ★ Remark: Time evolution is unitary
> For a time-independent $\hat H$ with discrete energy eigenbasis $\{|n\rangle\}$, each energy component simply turns its phase ([[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]], [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]):
>
> $$
> |\Psi(t)\rangle = \sum_n c_n\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle = \hat U(t)|\Psi(0)\rangle, \qquad \hat U(t) = \sum_n e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle\langle n| = e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar} .
> $$
>
> $\hat U(t)$ is diagonal in the energy basis, with entries of modulus $1$, so $\hat U^\dagger\hat U = \sum_n|n\rangle\langle n| = 1$: it is **unitary** and preserves every inner product, in particular the norm, so a normalized state stays normalized ([[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-51|LADR Def. 7.51]]); that the Schrödinger equation preserves all overlaps $\int\Psi_1^{\ast}\Psi_2\,dx$, for any real $V$, is [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]. The exponential of an operator with a complete eigenbasis can be defined by its spectral decomposition, $f(\hat H) = \sum_n f(E_n)|n\rangle\langle n|$, which agrees with the power series of [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^def-b2-1-6|Def. §B2.1.6]]. Unitary evolution generated by $\hat H$, and symmetries as generators, are developed at level C: evolution in [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^pr-c3-1-1|Principle §C3.1.1]] and [[§C3.1 The Time-Evolution Operator and the Schrödinger Equation#^thm-c3-1-3|Theorem §C3.1.3]], translations generated by momentum in [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^pr-c2-2-1|Principle §C2.2.1]] and [[§C2.2 Translation and Momentum as Its Generator#^thm-c2-2-2|Theorem §C2.2.2]], symmetries and conserved generators in general in [[§C8.1★ Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Degeneracies#^thm-c8-1-2|Theorem §C8.1.2]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problems 3.27, A.30, Example 3.8*

^rem-b2-3-2

## Consistency, and level A as special cases

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.5: Probabilities Add to One and Average to the Expectation Value
> For a normalized state and an observable with discrete spectrum, the probabilities of Principle §B2.3.3 satisfy
>
> $$
> \sum_n|c_n|^2 = 1, \qquad \sum_n q_n|c_n|^2 = \langle\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle = \langle Q\rangle ;
> $$
>
> for a continuous spectrum, $\int|c(z)|^2\,dz = 1$ and $\int q(z)\,|c(z)|^2\,dz = \langle\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle$. The mean of the measured values, weighted by their probabilities, is the sandwich $\langle\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle$.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4, eqs. (3.45)–(3.51) · Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 9, eqs. (1.16)–(1.26) · Fleisch §4.2*

^thm-b2-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By completeness ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]]) the orthonormal eigenfunctions of $\hat Q$ form a basis, so $\Psi = \sum_nc_nf_n$ with $c_n = \langle f_n|\Psi\rangle$, and Parseval ([[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]) gives $1 = \langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle = \sum_n|c_n|^2$. Next, $\hat Q\Psi = \sum_nc_nq_nf_n$, so by the same theorem
>
> $$
> \langle\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle = \sum_{n', n}c_{n'}^*\,c_n\,q_n\,\langle f_{n'}|f_n\rangle = \sum_n q_n|c_n|^2 .
> $$
>
> Degenerate eigenvalues change nothing: summing $|c_n|^2$ over an orthonormal basis of an eigenspace gives $P(q)$, and all those terms carry the same $q$. For a continuous spectrum replace sums by integrals and $\delta_{n'n}$ by $\delta(z - z')$ (Dirac orthonormality, [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^def-b2-2-3|Def. §B2.2.3]]).

*Uses:* [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^pr-b2-2-5|Principle §B2.2.5]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]], [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Level A's rules as special cases
> - **Born rule = measuring $x$.** The eigenfunctions of $\hat x$ are $g_y = \delta(x - y)$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]), so $c(y) = \langle g_y|\Psi\rangle = \int\delta(x - y)\Psi(x, t)\,dx = \Psi(y, t)$, and part 3 of Principle §B2.3.3 gives probability $|\Psi(y, t)|^2\,dy$ for a result in $dy$: exactly [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-1|Principle §A3.4.1]]. Its collapse to a narrow spike is [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^rem-a3-4-3|Remark: Measurement and the Copenhagen interpretation]] and [[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^rem-b1-1-1|Remark: Collapse, and the Born rule at level B]].
> - **The energy rule = measuring $H$.** With $\hat Q = \hat H$ and $f_n = \psi_n$, parts 2 and 4 are [[§A4.3 Superposition of Stationary States#^pr-a4-3-3|Principle §A4.3.3]], including the collapse to $\psi_n$, now also for degenerate levels.
> - **The average rule is now a theorem.** Level A postulated $\langle p\rangle = \int\Psi^{\ast}(-i\hbar\,\partial_x\Psi)\,dx$ and its analogues ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^pr-a3-4-4|Principle §A3.4.4]]) and promised the reason ([[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^rem-a3-4-6|Remark: Why this rule, and why its layer]]); Chapter B1 kept the general operator rule as a principle of wave mechanics ([[§B1.2 Momentum, Operators and Ehrenfest's Theorem#^pr-b1-2-2|Principle §B1.2.2]]). Theorem §B2.3.5 derives it: the probability-weighted mean of the outcomes is the sandwich. For momentum the weights are $|\Phi(p, t)|^2$ (Theorem §B2.3.6 below), which is the exact form of the plane-wave argument sketched at level A. The layer changes from principle to theorem, for level A's rule and for Principle §B1.2.2 alike.
>
> What remains assumed is Principle §B2.3.3 itself, for every observable at once.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4, eq. (3.52)*

^rem-b2-3-3

> [!caution] Caution: Three common misreadings
> - $|c_n|^2$ is **not** "the probability that the particle is in the state $f_n$": the particle is in the state $\Psi$, period. $|c_n|^2$ is the probability that a measurement of $Q$ gives $q_n$ (after which, but only after which, the particle is in $f_n$).
> - **Applying an operator is not making a measurement.** $\hat Q\Psi = \sum_nc_nq_nf_n$ is a new vector, in general not normalized and not a possible post-measurement state; a measurement instead replaces $\Psi$ by one eigenfunction $f_n$, at random. The operator's job is computational: sandwiched with $\Psi$, it produces $\langle Q\rangle$.
> - **A wave function is obtained by projecting, not by operating.** $\Psi(x) = \langle x|\Psi\rangle$ and $\Phi(p) = \langle p|\Psi\rangle$ are inner products of the state with basis vectors; neither is found by applying $\hat x$ or $\hat p$ to the state, and they are related by a Fourier transform, not by an operator.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4, footnote 18 · Fleisch §4.2, eq. (4.6)*

## The momentum-space wave function

> [!definition] Definition §B2.3.1: Momentum-Space Wave Function
> The **momentum-space wave function** of the state $|\Psi(t)\rangle$ is its component along the momentum eigenstate $|p\rangle$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]]):
>
> $$
> \Phi(p, t) = \langle p|\Psi(t)\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ipx/\hbar}\,\Psi(x, t)\,dx, \qquad \Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{ipx/\hbar}\,\Phi(p, t)\,dp .
> $$
>
> It is the Fourier transform of the position-space wave function, and $\Psi$ is its inverse transform. In terms of the wave-number amplitude of [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^def-b1-4-1|Def. §B1.4.1]] (conventions: [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^cau-b1-4-1|Caution: Fourier conventions in QM and in WO]]), $\Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\phi(k, t)e^{ikx}\,dk$, the substitution $p = \hbar k$ gives
>
> $$
> \Phi(p, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt\hbar}\,\phi(p/\hbar, t) \quad\text{at every } t, \qquad\text{and for a free particle}\qquad \Phi(p, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt\hbar}\,\phi(p/\hbar)\,e^{-ip^2t/2m\hbar} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4, eqs. (3.53)–(3.55), Problem 3.12; §3.6.1, eq. (3.78); §3.6.3, Example 3.9 · Fleisch §4.2, eqs. (4.4)–(4.5)*

^def-b2-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.6: Momentum Probabilities and Operators in Momentum Space
> 1. A measurement of momentum gives a result in the range $dp$ with probability $|\Phi(p, t)|^2\,dp$, and $\int|\Phi|^2\,dp = \int|\Psi|^2\,dx = 1$.
> 2. Averages can be computed in either space:
>
> $$
> \langle p\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p\,|\Phi(p, t)|^2\,dp = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi^*\Bigl(-i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\Bigr)dx, \qquad \langle x\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi^*\Bigl(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\Bigr)\Phi\,dp .
> $$
>
>    In momentum space $\hat p$ acts as multiplication by $p$ and $\hat x$ as $i\hbar\,\partial/\partial p$; more generally $\langle Q(x, p, t)\rangle = \int\Phi^*\,\hat Q\bigl(i\hbar\,\partial_p, p, t\bigr)\,\Phi\,dp$.
>
> This theorem is the home of the momentum density. The agreement of the two expressions for $\langle p\rangle$, and for every $\langle p^n\rangle$, is the Fourier identity of [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-3|Theorem §B1.4.3]] written in the variable $p$; that theorem shows only that the operator rule and the moments of $|\phi|^2$ agree, while part 1 here makes $|\Phi|^2$ the probability density by the postulate, so that the operator rule follows.
>
> *Source: Griffiths §3.4, eqs. (3.56)–(3.58), Problem 3.13; §3.6.3, eqs. (3.109)–(3.110), Example 3.10*

^thm-b2-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* By Principle §B2.3.3, part 3, with the Dirac-orthonormal momentum eigenfunctions $f_p$, the amplitude is $c(p) = \langle f_p|\Psi\rangle = \Phi(p, t)$. The total probability is one by [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-5|Theorem §B2.3.5]], or directly by Plancherel: with WO's transform $\tilde\Psi(k) = \frac1{2\pi}\int\Psi e^{-ikx}dx$ one has $\Phi(p) = \sqrt{2\pi/\hbar}\,\tilde\Psi(p/\hbar)$, and $\int|\Phi|^2dp = 2\pi\int|\tilde\Psi(k)|^2dk = \int|\Psi|^2dx$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|WO Theorem §B4.4.4]]).
>
> *2, momentum.* The first expression for $\langle p\rangle$ is Theorem §B2.3.5 with $q(p) = p$. Its equality with the position-space sandwich is [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-3|Theorem §B1.4.3]] with $n = 1$, rewritten with $p = \hbar k$: by Def. §B2.3.1, $|\Phi(p)|^2dp = |\phi(k)|^2dk$, so $\int(\hbar k)^n|\phi|^2dk = \int p^n|\Phi|^2dp$. In the language of [[§B2.1 Hilbert Space and Dirac Notation#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]], the transform is a unitary change of basis (with a continuous index) that turns $\hat p$ into multiplication by $p$.
>
> *2, position.* Insert $\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x| = 1$ ([[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]]) and use $\hat x|x\rangle = x|x\rangle$:
>
> $$
> \langle p|\hat x|\Psi\rangle = \int\langle p|x\rangle\,x\,\langle x|\Psi\rangle\,dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int x\,e^{-ipx/\hbar}\,\Psi(x)\,dx = i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\Bigl[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int e^{-ipx/\hbar}\,\Psi(x)\,dx\Bigr] = i\hbar\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial p} ,
> $$
>
> since $x\,e^{-ipx/\hbar} = i\hbar\,\partial_pe^{-ipx/\hbar}$. Taking the inner product with $\Phi$ gives $\langle x\rangle$. A product of powers of $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ is represented by the same product of $i\hbar\,\partial_p$ and $p$, in the same order, which gives the general formula.

*Uses:* [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^pr-b2-3-3|Principle §B2.3.3]], [[§B2.3 The Postulates and the Generalized Statistical Interpretation#^thm-b2-3-5|Theorem §B2.3.5]], [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-3|Theorem §B1.4.3]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-6|Theorem §B2.2.6]], [[§B2.2 Observables and Hermitian Operators#^thm-b2-2-7|Theorem §B2.2.7]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|WO Theorem §B4.4.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: What this settles from level A
> Level A's heuristic proof of Heisenberg's relation took two facts on trust ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^der-a3-5-1|derivation of Theorem §A3.5.1]]): that the momentum density is $|\phi(p)|^2$ for the Fourier transform of $\Psi$, and a sharp Fourier inequality. The first is Theorem §B2.3.6, part 1; the second, and the relation itself, follow from the commutator in [[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-4|Theorem §B2.4.4]]. The momentum density of a Gaussian drawn at level A ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^ex-a3-5-1|Example §A3.5.1]]) is $|\Phi|^2$. For a free particle $|\Phi(p, t)|^2 = |\phi(p/\hbar)|^2/\hbar$ does not change in time (Definition §B2.3.1; [[§B1.4 The Free Particle and Wave Packets#^thm-b1-4-3|Theorem §B1.4.3]]): the momentum distribution of a free particle is conserved, the quantum form of momentum conservation ([[§B2.4 Commutators and the Generalized Uncertainty Principle#^thm-b2-4-8|Theorem §B2.4.8]]).
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.12*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.3.1: Measuring Momentum on a Ring
> A particle moves on a circle of circumference $L$ (the points $x = 0$ and $x = L$ identified, so $\Psi(x + L) = \Psi(x)$). At some instant
>
> $$
> \Psi(x) = \sqrt{\frac2L}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\sin\frac{2\pi x}{L} + \sqrt{\frac23}\cos\frac{6\pi x}{L}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Which momenta can a measurement give, with which probabilities?
>
> *Momentum eigenstates.* $e^{ikx}$ is periodic only if $e^{ikL} = 1$, i.e. $k = 2\pi m/L$ with $m \in \mathbb Z$. On the ring the momentum spectrum is therefore discrete, and the eigenfunctions are normalizable:
>
> $$
> \psi_m(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt L}e^{2\pi imx/L}, \qquad \hat p\,\psi_m = \frac{2\pi m\hbar}{L}\,\psi_m .
> $$
>
> *Expand.* With $\sin u = (e^{iu} - e^{-iu})/2i$ and $\cos u = (e^{iu} + e^{-iu})/2$,
>
> $$
> \Psi = \frac{1}{\sqrt6\,i}\bigl(\psi_1 - \psi_{-1}\bigr) + \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\bigl(\psi_3 + \psi_{-3}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Probabilities* (Principle §B2.3.3): $p = \pm2\pi\hbar/L$ each with probability $\tfrac16$, $p = \pm6\pi\hbar/L$ each with probability $\tfrac13$; they add to $1$. So $\langle p\rangle = 0$ (as for any real wave function, [[§A3.4 Wave Functions and Probability#^thm-a3-4-5|Theorem §A3.4.5]]) and, by Theorem §B2.3.5,
>
> $$
> \langle p^2\rangle = \frac13\Bigl(\frac{2\pi\hbar}{L}\Bigr)^2 + \frac23\Bigl(\frac{6\pi\hbar}{L}\Bigr)^2 = \frac{19}{3}\,\frac{4\pi^2\hbar^2}{L^2} ,
> $$
>
> which agrees with $\hbar^2\int_0^L|\Psi'|^2\,dx$ computed directly. *After the measurement.* If the result is $6\pi\hbar/L$, the state collapses to $\psi_3$, whose position density $|\psi_3|^2 = 1/L$ is uniform: the measurement erased the structure that $|\Psi|^2$ had, and a repeated momentum measurement now gives $6\pi\hbar/L$ with certainty.
>
> *Source: Zwiebach 8.04, Lecture 9, eqs. (1.27)–(1.36)*

> [!example] Example §B2.3.2: Momentum of the Oscillator Ground State
> A particle is in the ground state of the harmonic oscillator, $\psi_0 = (m\omega/\pi\hbar)^{1/4}e^{-m\omega x^2/2\hbar}$, energy $E_0 = \tfrac12\hbar\omega$ ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-4|Theorem §A4.5.4]]). Find $\Phi(p)$ and the probability that a momentum measurement gives a value outside the classical range for this energy.
>
> *Transform.* With $\int e^{-\alpha x^2 - i\beta x}dx = \sqrt{\pi/\alpha}\,e^{-\beta^2/4\alpha}$ (complete the square; [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-7|452 Ex. §15.7]], or the Gaussian rule of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|WO Theorem §B4.4.2]]) for $\alpha = m\omega/2\hbar$, $\beta = p/\hbar$:
>
> $$
> \Phi_0(p) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\Bigl(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\Bigr)^{1/4}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar}{m\omega}}\,e^{-p^2/2m\omega\hbar} = \Bigl(\frac{1}{\pi m\omega\hbar}\Bigr)^{1/4}e^{-p^2/2m\omega\hbar} ,
> $$
>
> again a Gaussian, normalized as Theorem §B2.3.6 requires.
>
> *Classical range.* A classical oscillator with energy $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ has $|p| \le p_{\max} = \sqrt{2mE_0} = \sqrt{m\hbar\omega}$. With $u = p/\sqrt{m\hbar\omega}$, $|\Phi_0|^2dp = \pi^{-1/2}e^{-u^2}du$, and
>
> $$
> P(|p| > p_{\max}) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_1^\infty e^{-u^2}\,du = \operatorname{erfc}(1) = 0.157 .
> $$
>
> This is the same $16\%$ that level A found for finding the particle beyond the classical turning points ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^rem-a4-5-2|Remark: Three departures from the classical oscillator]]). That is no accident: in units $x/\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$ and $p/\sqrt{m\hbar\omega}$ the oscillator Hamiltonian is symmetric between $x$ and $p$, and so is its ground state.
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.11; solved here*

> [!example] Example §B2.3.3: Sequential Measurements
> Observable $A$ has two normalized eigenstates $\psi_1, \psi_2$ with eigenvalues $a_1, a_2$; observable $B$ has eigenstates $\phi_1, \phi_2$ with eigenvalues $b_1, b_2$, and
>
> $$
> \psi_1 = \tfrac15(3\phi_1 + 4\phi_2), \qquad \psi_2 = \tfrac15(4\phi_1 - 3\phi_2) .
> $$
>
> $A$ is measured and gives $a_1$; then $B$ is measured; then $A$ again. What happens?
>
> *After the first measurement* the state is $\psi_1$ (collapse, Principle §B2.3.3, part 4).
>
> *Measuring $B$.* Expand $\psi_1$ in the eigenstates of $\hat B$: the coefficients are $\tfrac35$ and $\tfrac45$, so $b_1$ occurs with probability $\tfrac{9}{25}$ and $b_2$ with probability $\tfrac{16}{25}$. The state becomes $\phi_1$ or $\phi_2$.
>
> *Measuring $A$ again.* Inverting the relations, $\phi_1 = \tfrac15(3\psi_1 + 4\psi_2)$ and $\phi_2 = \tfrac15(4\psi_1 - 3\psi_2)$. From $\phi_1$, $a_1$ comes out with probability $\tfrac{9}{25}$; from $\phi_2$, with probability $\tfrac{16}{25}$. Not knowing the result of the $B$ measurement,
>
> $$
> P(a_1) = \frac{9}{25}\cdot\frac{9}{25} + \frac{16}{25}\cdot\frac{16}{25} = \frac{337}{625} = 0.539 .
> $$
>
> Had the $B$ measurement been skipped, the second $A$ measurement would have given $a_1$ with certainty; the intervening measurement of the incompatible observable $B$ ($\psi_1$ is not an eigenstate of $\hat B$) destroyed that. Had we been told the $B$ result, the answer would be $0.36$ (after $b_1$) or $0.64$ (after $b_2$). The PHY 308 homework example of the same kind, an energy measurement followed by a position measurement in the box, is worked in [[§B1.3 Stationary States and Expansion in Energy Eigenstates#^ex-b1-3-2|Example §B1.3.2]].
>
> *Source: Griffiths Problem 3.33; solved here*
