How does $\vec F = m\vec a$ become a definite prediction, and what does it look like in the coordinates a problem suggests? Level A stated Newton's laws as principles ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^pr-a3-2-1|Principle §A3.2.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|Principle §A3.3.3]], [[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|Principle §A3.5.1]]) for inertial frames ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]), and used polar unit vectors only for motion on a circle ([[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-3|Theorem §A2.7.3]]). Here the second law is a differential equation: which initial data fix the motion, which one-dimensional forces can be integrated by separating variables, and what the acceleration is in polar, cylindrical and spherical components, where the moving unit vectors produce the centripetal and Coriolis-type terms.

## Newton's laws as the starting point

> [!remark] Remark: What the laws assert, seen from level B
> - **Layer.** Newton's three laws stay the principles of Classical Mechanics through Chapters B1–B4; the theorems of these chapters are derived from them. From Chapter B6 on, Hamilton's principle ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action]]) becomes the starting point, and Newton's second law is recovered from it for systems with a potential.
> - **First law.** It defines an inertial frame as one in which a free particle keeps a constant velocity ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]). Its content is that one frame works for *all* free particles at once. Such frames turn out to be the ones neither accelerating nor rotating relative to the distant stars (H&S §1.2).
> - **Second law.** It is more than a definition of force: it asserts that there is a quantity $\vec F$, determined by the surroundings (a spring stretched by a given amount, a given mass at a given distance), that produces the same $m\vec a$ in *every* particle placed there. A made-up quantity such as $m^2\vec a$ has no such property. Since accelerations, masses and forces are the same in all inertial frames, the law has the same form in each ([[§A1.1 Classical Relativity#^thm-a1-1-4|REL Theorem §A1.1.4]]).
> - **Third law.** For two particles interacting only with each other it is equivalent to conservation of their total momentum: $d(\vec p_1 + \vec p_2)/dt = \vec F_1 + \vec F_2$. It is not universal: magnetic forces between moving charges ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|EM Principle §A7.1.1]]) need not be equal and opposite, the missing momentum being carried by the field. Between particles with contact, spring or gravitational forces it holds.
>
> *Source: Morin §3.1 · H&S §1.1–1.2*

^rem-b1-1-1

> [!model] Model §B1.1.1: Point Particle
> A body is treated as a **particle** if its size and internal structure can be ignored: it is described by one position vector $\vec r(t)$ in an inertial frame and a constant mass $m$. Its **state** at time $t$ is the pair $(\vec r, \vec v)$, $\vec v = \dot{\vec r}$.
> - *Assumes:* the body is small compared with the distances over which the forces on it change; it has no internal motion (rotation, vibration) that exchanges energy or momentum with its translation; speeds are much less than $c$.
> - *Fails:* when the body's own rotation matters (a planet considered as a spinning body rather than an orbiting point, a rolling ball: rigid bodies, Chapters B3 and B10); when its mass changes (rockets, [[§A5.5 Rocket Propulsion#^thm-a5-5-2|Theorem §A5.5.2]]); at relativistic speeds.
>
> Whether a body is a particle depends on the question: a planet is one for its orbit about the Sun, not for its rotation about its axis.
>
> *Source: Landau §1 · H&S §1.1*

^mod-b1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.2: The Equation of Motion as an Initial-Value Problem
> For a particle ([[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^mod-b1-1-1|Model §B1.1.1]]) on which the net force is a given function $\vec F(\vec r, \vec v, t)$, the second law is the system of three second-order differential equations
>
> $$
> m\,\ddot{\vec r} = \vec F(\vec r, \dot{\vec r}, t) .
> $$
>
> If $\vec F$ is continuous and has continuous partial derivatives with respect to the components of $\vec r$ and $\vec v$, then for every initial state $\vec r(t_0) = \vec r_0$, $\vec v(t_0) = \vec v_0$ there is exactly one solution on some time interval around $t_0$. The general solution contains six constants, and the state at one instant determines the motion.
>
> *Source: Landau §1 (coordinates and velocities determine the accelerations: "known from experience") · Morin §3.3, footnote 11 (an $n$-th order equation needs $n$ initial conditions)*

^thm-b1-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Introduce the six-component variable $y = (\vec r, \vec v)$. The second law is equivalent to the first-order system
>
> $$
> \dot y = G(y, t), \qquad G(y, t) = \Bigl(\vec v,\ \tfrac1m\vec F(\vec r, \vec v, t)\Bigr),
> $$
>
> since its first three equations say $\vec v = \dot{\vec r}$ and the last three say $m\dot{\vec v} = \vec F$. Under the stated smoothness, $G$ is continuous and locally Lipschitz in $y$, and the Picard–Lindelöf theorem (Mathematical Methods, planned) gives a unique solution through each initial point $y(t_0)$ on some interval around $t_0$. The free constants are the six components of $y(t_0)$. For a linear force the solution space is described completely in [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-2|WO Theorem §B1.1.2]].
>
> The existence is only local: a solution can leave every bounded region in finite time ([[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^ex-b1-3-3|Example §B1.3.3]]) or run into a point where $\vec F$ is singular, as at $r = 0$ for an inverse-square force.

## One-dimensional forces of one variable

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.3: Integrable Cases in One Dimension
> Let $m\ddot x = F$, with initial data $x(t_0) = x_0$, $v(t_0) = v_0$, where $F$ depends on only one of $t$, $x$, $v$. Then the motion follows from integrals and an inversion:
> 1. $F = F(t)$: $\displaystyle v(t) = v_0 + \frac1m\int_{t_0}^{t} F(t')\,dt'$, $\displaystyle x(t) = x_0 + \int_{t_0}^{t} v(t')\,dt'$.
> 2. $F = F(x)$: $\displaystyle \tfrac12 mv^2 - \tfrac12 mv_0^2 = \int_{x_0}^{x} F(x')\,dx'$ gives $v(x)$, and then $\displaystyle t - t_0 = \int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{dx'}{v(x')}$, inverted for $x(t)$.
> 3. $F = F(v)$: $\displaystyle t - t_0 = m\int_{v_0}^{v}\frac{dv'}{F(v')}$, inverted for $v(t)$ and integrated once more; or directly $\displaystyle x - x_0 = m\int_{v_0}^{v}\frac{v'\,dv'}{F(v')}$.
>
> In case 2 the formula for $t$ holds on intervals where $v$ keeps one sign; in case 3, on intervals where $F(v) \neq 0$.
>
> *Source: Morin §3.3, eqs. (3.17)–(3.27)*

^thm-b1-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The whole method is the choice between two expressions for the acceleration,
>
> $$
> \ddot x = \frac{dv}{dt} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \ddot x = \frac{dv}{dx}\,\frac{dx}{dt} = v\,\frac{dv}{dx} ,
> $$
>
> the second by the chain rule, valid while $x(t)$ is strictly monotonic so that $v$ can be regarded as a function of $x$. Pick the one in which only two of the three variables $t$, $x$, $v$ appear, separate them and integrate.
> 1. $m\,dv/dt = F(t)$: integrate in $t$ twice.
> 2. $mv\,dv/dx = F(x)$: separating, $m\,v\,dv = F(x)\,dx$, and integrating from $(x_0, v_0)$ gives the first formula. Then $dt = dx/v(x)$.
> 3. $m\,dv/dt = F(v)$: $dt = m\,dv/F(v)$. Alternatively $mv\,dv/dx = F(v)$ gives $dx = mv\,dv/F(v)$.
>
> Each integral is evaluated by the fundamental theorem of calculus.

^der-b1-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where the three cases lead
> Case 2 is energy conservation in one dimension; [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method|§B1.3]] develops it into a complete method. Case 3 contains linear and quadratic drag ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^thm-a3-10-4|Theorem §A3.10.4]], [[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^ex-a3-10-3|Example §A3.10.3]]). A force depending on two of the variables, such as $-kx - b\dot x$, does not separate; when it is linear the exponential method of [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|WO Theorem §B1.1.3]] takes over.
>
> *Source: Morin §3.3, §4.1*

^rem-b1-1-2

> [!example] Example §B1.1.1: A Beach Ball Thrown Upward, with Linear Drag
> A ball is thrown straight up at speed $v_0$; the drag is $F_d = -m\alpha v$ (Morin's notation, $\alpha = b/m$). Find the speed $v_f$ with which it returns, and compare its time in the air with that in vacuum.
>
> With $y$ up, $m\ddot y = -mg - m\alpha v$ on both legs (on the way down $v < 0$ and the drag points up). This is case 3 of [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]. Write $\ddot y = v\,dv/dy$ and compute the maximum height $h$ twice. Going up, from $v_0$ to $0$:
>
> $$
> h = -\int_{v_0}^{0}\frac{v\,dv}{g + \alpha v} = \frac{v_0}{\alpha} - \frac{g}{\alpha^2}\ln\Bigl(1 + \frac{\alpha v_0}{g}\Bigr),
> $$
>
> using $v/(g + \alpha v) = \bigl[1 - g/(g + \alpha v)\bigr]/\alpha$. Coming down, from $0$ to $-v_f$, the same integral gives $h$ with $v_0$ replaced by $-v_f$. Equating,
>
> $$
> v_0 + v_f = \frac g\alpha\,\ln\frac{g + \alpha v_0}{g - \alpha v_f} ,
> $$
>
> an implicit equation for $v_f$. For $\alpha v_0 \ll g$ both expressions for $h$ reduce to $v_0^2/2g$ and $v_f^2/2g$; for large $\alpha$, $v_f \to g/\alpha$, the terminal speed.
>
> *Time in the air.* Integrate $m\,dv/dt = -mg - m\alpha v$ over the whole flight, $0 \le t \le T$: $-v_f - v_0 = -gT - \alpha\int_0^T v\,dt = -gT$, because $\int_0^T v\,dt$ is the net displacement, zero. So $T = (v_0 + v_f)/g$. Drag removes energy, so $v_f < v_0$, and $T < 2v_0/g$, the time in vacuum: the ball spends *less* time in the air, although it is slower on the way down.
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 3.12 (solved here; checked against Morin's solution)*

## Curvilinear components

> [!definition] Definition §B1.1.1: Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinates and Their Unit Vectors
> - **Cylindrical** coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$: $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, $z = z$, with $r \ge 0$ the distance from the $z$-axis. In the plane $z = 0$ they are **polar** coordinates ([[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^def-a1-3-7|Def. §A1.3.7]], where the angle is called $\varphi$ and $\hat\theta$ is called $\hat t$). The unit vectors are
>
> $$
> \hat r = \cos\theta\,\hat x + \sin\theta\,\hat y, \qquad \hat\theta = -\sin\theta\,\hat x + \cos\theta\,\hat y, \qquad \hat z .
> $$
>
> - **Spherical** coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$: $x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z = r\cos\theta$, with $r \ge 0$ the distance from the origin, $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ the polar angle from the $+z$ axis and $\phi$ the azimuth. The unit vectors are
>
> $$
> \hat r = (\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ \sin\theta\sin\phi,\ \cos\theta), \quad \hat\theta = (\cos\theta\cos\phi,\ \cos\theta\sin\phi,\ -\sin\theta), \quad \hat\phi = (-\sin\phi,\ \cos\phi,\ 0).
> $$
>
> In both systems the unit vectors point in the directions of increasing coordinates, are orthonormal and right-handed ($\hat r\times\hat\theta = \hat z$, resp. $\hat r\times\hat\theta = \hat\phi$), and change from point to point. When the two systems are used together, write $\rho$ for the cylindrical distance $r\sin\theta$.
>
> *Source: Morin §3.5, eq. (3.46) · H&S §1.6 (spherical and cylindrical unit vectors are given in its Appendix A, not among the sources) · Landau §9, Problems 1–2 (same conventions)*

^def-b1-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Angle names
> Physics uses $\theta$ for the polar angle and $\phi$ for the azimuth; the vault's Multivariable Analysis notes use the mathematicians' convention, $x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta$, with the two angles swapped ([[Polar and spherical coordinates]]). Level A writes the plane polar angle as $\varphi$; level B follows Morin and H&S and writes $\theta$.

^cau-b1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.4: Second Law in Polar and Cylindrical Components
> In cylindrical coordinates the velocity and acceleration of a particle are
>
> $$
> \vec v = \dot r\,\hat r + r\dot\theta\,\hat\theta + \dot z\,\hat z, \qquad
> \vec a = \bigl(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\bigr)\hat r + \bigl(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\bigr)\hat\theta + \ddot z\,\hat z ,
> $$
>
> so the second law $\vec F = m\vec a$ reads
>
> $$
> F_r = m\bigl(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\bigr), \qquad F_\theta = m\bigl(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\bigr) = \frac1r\,\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(mr^2\dot\theta\bigr), \qquad F_z = m\ddot z .
> $$
>
> For plane motion drop the $z$-terms.
>
> *Source: Morin §3.5, eqs. (3.48)–(3.51) · H&S §1.6, eqs. (1.57)–(1.59)*

^thm-b1-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The unit vectors depend on time through $\theta(t)$. Differentiating the components in [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]] with the chain rule,
>
> $$
> \frac{d\hat r}{dt} = \dot\theta\,(-\sin\theta\,\hat x + \cos\theta\,\hat y) = \dot\theta\,\hat\theta, \qquad \frac{d\hat\theta}{dt} = -\dot\theta\,(\cos\theta\,\hat x + \sin\theta\,\hat y) = -\dot\theta\,\hat r ,
> $$
>
> and $\hat z$ is constant. The position is $\vec r = r\,\hat r + z\,\hat z$. By the product rule,
>
> $$
> \vec v = \dot r\,\hat r + r\dot\theta\,\hat\theta + \dot z\,\hat z ,
> $$
>
> $$
> \vec a = \ddot r\,\hat r + \dot r\dot\theta\,\hat\theta + \bigl(\dot r\dot\theta + r\ddot\theta\bigr)\hat\theta + r\dot\theta\,(-\dot\theta\,\hat r) + \ddot z\,\hat z = \bigl(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2\bigr)\hat r + \bigl(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta\bigr)\hat\theta + \ddot z\,\hat z .
> $$
>
> Since the unit vectors are orthonormal, $\vec F = m\vec a$ holds component by component ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], in a basis that happens to move). Finally $\frac{d}{dt}(r^2\dot\theta) = 2r\dot r\dot\theta + r^2\ddot\theta = r\,(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)$.

^der-b1-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the four terms
> - $m\ddot r$: the second law along a fixed radial line.
> - $mr\ddot\theta$: the second law along the circle, since $r\theta$ is the arc length.
> - $-mr\dot\theta^2 = -mv_\theta^2/r$: the centripetal term; for $r$ and $\dot\theta$ constant this is [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]].
> - $2m\dot r\dot\theta$: present whenever the particle moves radially while turning. The form $F_\theta = \frac1r\frac{d}{dt}(mr^2\dot\theta)$ shows its meaning: without a tangential force the angular momentum $mr^2\dot\theta$ stays constant ([[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]), so a particle moving outward must slow its turning. In a frame rotating with the particle this term becomes the Coriolis force ([[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames]]).
>
> *Source: Morin §3.5*

^rem-b1-1-3

> [!caution] Caution: Components of the acceleration are not second derivatives of the coordinates
> $a_r \neq \ddot r$ and $a_\theta \neq r\ddot\theta$: the extra terms come from the turning of $\hat r$ and $\hat\theta$. A particle moving uniformly along a straight line not through the origin has $\vec a = \vec 0$ but $\ddot r \ne 0$ and $\ddot\theta \ne 0$; in polar form the equations $\ddot r = r\dot\theta^2$, $r^2\dot\theta = \text{const}$ describe this straight line (Morin Problem 3.24 shows it gives $r\cos\theta = r_0$).

^cau-b1-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.5: Second Law in Spherical Components
> In spherical coordinates
>
> $$
> \vec v = \dot r\,\hat r + r\dot\theta\,\hat\theta + r\sin\theta\,\dot\phi\,\hat\phi ,
> $$
>
> and the components of the acceleration, each equal to the corresponding component of $\vec F/m$, are
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> a_r &= \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 - r\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2,\\
> a_\theta &= r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta - r\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2 = \frac1r\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(r^2\dot\theta\bigr) - r\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2,\\
> a_\phi &= r\sin\theta\,\ddot\phi + 2\dot r\dot\phi\sin\theta + 2r\dot\theta\dot\phi\cos\theta = \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\,\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi\bigr).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> *Source: derived here (H&S §1.6 refers to its Appendix A, which is not among the sources); the speed $v^2 = \dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2$ is used in Landau §9, Problem 2, and H&S Problem 1.35*

^thm-b1-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate the components of [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]] with respect to $\theta$ and $\phi$:
>
> $$
> \partial_\theta\hat r = \hat\theta,\quad \partial_\phi\hat r = \sin\theta\,\hat\phi; \qquad \partial_\theta\hat\theta = -\hat r,\quad \partial_\phi\hat\theta = \cos\theta\,\hat\phi; \qquad \partial_\theta\hat\phi = \vec 0,\quad \partial_\phi\hat\phi = (-\cos\phi, -\sin\phi, 0) = -\sin\theta\,\hat r - \cos\theta\,\hat\theta .
> $$
>
> (For the last: $\sin\theta\,\hat r + \cos\theta\,\hat\theta = (\cos\phi, \sin\phi, \sin\theta\cos\theta - \cos\theta\sin\theta)$.) By the chain rule,
>
> $$
> \dot{\hat r} = \dot\theta\,\hat\theta + \dot\phi\sin\theta\,\hat\phi, \qquad \dot{\hat\theta} = -\dot\theta\,\hat r + \dot\phi\cos\theta\,\hat\phi, \qquad \dot{\hat\phi} = -\dot\phi\,(\sin\theta\,\hat r + \cos\theta\,\hat\theta) .
> $$
>
> With $\vec r = r\hat r$, $\vec v = \dot r\,\hat r + r\dot{\hat r}$ gives the velocity. Write $\vec v = v_r\hat r + v_\theta\hat\theta + v_\phi\hat\phi$ with $v_r = \dot r$, $v_\theta = r\dot\theta$, $v_\phi = r\sin\theta\,\dot\phi$, and differentiate:
>
> $$
> \vec a = \dot v_r\hat r + \dot v_\theta\hat\theta + \dot v_\phi\hat\phi + v_r\dot{\hat r} + v_\theta\dot{\hat\theta} + v_\phi\dot{\hat\phi} .
> $$
>
> Collect each component:
> - $\hat r$: $\ddot r - v_\theta\dot\theta - v_\phi\dot\phi\sin\theta = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 - r\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2$;
> - $\hat\theta$: $(\dot r\dot\theta + r\ddot\theta) + v_r\dot\theta - v_\phi\dot\phi\cos\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta - r\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2$;
> - $\hat\phi$: $(\dot r\sin\theta\,\dot\phi + r\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\phi + r\sin\theta\,\ddot\phi) + v_r\dot\phi\sin\theta + v_\theta\dot\phi\cos\theta = r\sin\theta\,\ddot\phi + 2\dot r\dot\phi\sin\theta + 2r\dot\theta\dot\phi\cos\theta$.
>
> The two total-derivative forms follow by expanding: $\frac{d}{dt}(r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi) = r\sin\theta\,\bigl(2\dot r\sin\theta\,\dot\phi + 2r\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\phi + r\sin\theta\,\ddot\phi\bigr)$, and similarly for $r^2\dot\theta$.

^der-b1-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Checks on the spherical formulas
> - At $\theta = \pi/2$, $\dot\theta = 0$ (motion in the equatorial plane) they reduce to the polar formulas of [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]], with $\phi$ in the role of $\theta$.
> - The velocity components are $h_i\dot q_i$ with the scale factors $h_r = 1$, $h_\theta = r$, $h_\phi = r\sin\theta$ that also build the Laplacian in these coordinates ([[§19 The Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates#^rem-19-3|452 §19]]).
> - $a_\phi$ is a total derivative divided by $r\sin\theta$: $r\sin\theta\,F_\phi$ is the torque about the $z$-axis and $mr^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi$ the angular momentum about it ([[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]).
> - Deriving these components from $\vec F = m\vec a$ is laborious. From the kinetic energy $T = \tfrac12 m\,(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2)$ Lagrange's equations give them in a few lines ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.1.2: A Payload Slung from a Rotating Boom
> A boom of length $R$ turns in a horizontal plane at constant angular velocity $\omega$ about a vertical axis. A payload, released at rest relative to the boom at radius $r_0$, slides without friction along it and flies off the end. Find $r(t)$, the force the boom exerts, and the launch speed.
>
> Use polar coordinates with $\theta = \omega t$. Gravity and the vertical normal force cancel. The boom is frictionless, so it pushes only perpendicular to itself: $F_r = 0$, $F_\theta \ne 0$. By [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]] with $\dot\theta = \omega$, $\ddot\theta = 0$:
>
> $$
> \ddot r - \omega^2 r = 0, \qquad F_\theta = 2m\omega\dot r .
> $$
>
> With $r(0) = r_0$, $\dot r(0) = 0$ the solution is $r = r_0\cosh\omega t$. Then $\dot r = r_0\omega\sinh\omega t = \omega\sqrt{r^2 - r_0^2}$, and the boom's push is
>
> $$
> F_\theta = 2m\omega^2 r_0\sinh\omega t = 2m\omega^2\sqrt{r^2 - r_0^2},
> $$
>
> largest at the tip: it is entirely the $2\dot r\dot\theta$ term, needed to keep the payload turning at $\omega$ while it moves out. At the tip, $\vec v = \omega\bigl(\sqrt{R^2 - r_0^2}\,\hat r + R\,\hat\theta\bigr)$, of magnitude $v_f = \omega\sqrt{2R^2 - r_0^2}$.
>
> As a launcher on the airless Moon (escape speed about $2.4$ km/s), with $\omega = 2\pi\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $r_0 = 1$ m, $v_f \approx \sqrt2\,\omega R$ requires $R \approx 2400/(2\pi\sqrt2)\ \mathrm m \approx 270$ m.
>
> *Source: H&S Example 1.3*
