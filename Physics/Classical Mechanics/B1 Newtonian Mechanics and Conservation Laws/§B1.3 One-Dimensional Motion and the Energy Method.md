When the force on a particle moving along a line depends only on its position, energy conservation is a first-order equation and the motion follows from one integration. Level A drew potential-energy diagrams with their turning points ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^def-a4-7-1|Def. §A4.7.1]], [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|Theorem §A4.7.1]]), classified equilibria ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|Theorem §A4.7.2]]) and gave $t(x)$ for motion in one direction ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-3|Theorem §A4.7.3]]); small oscillations about a minimum are [[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-3|WO Theorem §A1.2.3]]. This section completes the method: the general solution with its two constants, what happens at a turning point (a simple one is reached in finite time, a maximum of $U$ only asymptotically), the period of every bounded motion as a function of energy and its small-amplitude limit, the motion near an unstable equilibrium, and how the period of an anharmonic oscillator depends on its amplitude. The same reduction governs radial motion in a central field ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential]]).

## Kinds of motion

> [!definition] Definition §B1.3.1: Finite and Infinite Motion; Separatrix Energy
> Let a particle of mass $m$ move on the $x$-axis with potential energy $U(x)$ and energy $E$. The motion takes place in one connected component of the **allowed region** $\{x : U(x) \le E\}$ ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|Theorem §A4.7.1]]). It is **finite** (bounded) if that component is an interval $[x_1(E), x_2(E)]$ between two turning points, and **infinite** if the component is unbounded on one or both sides, so that the particle goes off to infinity. If $x_m$ is a local maximum of $U$, the energy $E = U(x_m)$ is a **separatrix energy**: it separates motions of different kinds, such as oscillation in a well and passage over the barrier.
>
> *Source: Landau §11 (finite and infinite motion; Fig. 6)*

## Solution by quadrature

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.1: Solution by Quadrature
> Let $U$ be twice continuously differentiable and let only the force $-U'(x)$ act. On any time interval on which $\dot x$ does not vanish, its sign $\sigma = \pm1$ is constant and
>
> $$
> t - t_0 = \sigma\sqrt{\frac m2}\int_{x_0}^{x}\frac{dx'}{\sqrt{E - U(x')}} .
> $$
>
> The general solution depends on the two constants $E$ and $t_0$. The motion is symmetric about each instant $t_*$ at which the particle is at rest: $x(t_* + s) = x(t_* - s)$.
>
> *Source: Landau §11, eq. (11.3) · H&S §1.8 (Example 1.12: "reduced to quadrature") · Morin §5.1*

^thm-b1-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Energy is conserved ([[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]): $\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + U(x) = E$, so $\dot x = \sigma\sqrt{2(E - U)/m}$; separating variables gives the formula ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-3|Theorem §A4.7.3]]). The constants are $E$ and the time $t_0$ at which $x = x_0$, matching the two initial data of [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]].
>
> *Reversal.* The equation $m\ddot x = -U'(x)$ contains no first time derivative, so if $x(t)$ is a solution so is $y(t) = x(2t_* - t)$. At $t = t_*$ both have the same position and zero velocity, so by uniqueness ([[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]) $y = x$, which is the stated symmetry. The particle retraces its approach after a turning point.

> [!remark] Remark: Beyond a Cartesian coordinate
> Nothing changes if the coordinate is any $q$ with energy $E = \tfrac12 a\,\dot q^2 + U(q)$ and constant $a$, for instance the angle of a pendulum with $a = ml^2$: replace $m$ by $a$. Landau writes the general one-dimensional Lagrangian with a coefficient $a(q)$; then $dt = \sqrt{a(q)/2}\;dq/\sqrt{E - U(q)}$. Radial motion in a central field is of this form once the angular momentum is eliminated ([[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^ex-b1-2-1|Example §B1.2.1]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: Landau §11, eq. (11.1)*

^rem-b1-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.2: Behaviour near a Turning Point
> Let $x_2$ be a right-hand end of the allowed region at energy $E$: $U(x_2) = E$ and $U < E$ just to the left of $x_2$.
> 1. If $U'(x_2) > 0$ (a **simple turning point**), the particle reaches $x_2$ in a finite time and turns back. Near the turning instant $t_2$,
>
> $$
> x_2 - x(t) \approx \frac{U'(x_2)}{2m}\,(t - t_2)^2 :
> $$
>
> it decelerates uniformly into the turning point and returns.
> 2. If $U'(x_2) = 0$ and $U''(x_2) < 0$ ($x_2$ is a maximum of $U$ and $E$ the separatrix energy), the time needed to reach $x_2$ is infinite: the particle approaches $x_2$ asymptotically, with
>
> $$
> x_2 - x(t) \propto e^{-\kappa t}, \qquad \kappa = \sqrt{\lvert U''(x_2)\rvert/m} .
> $$
>
> Part 2 qualifies the level-A description of a turning point as a place where the velocity changes sign ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^def-a4-7-1|Def. §A4.7.1]]): at a maximum of $U$ the particle never gets there, and its velocity never changes sign.
>
> *Source: Landau §11, eq. (11.3) (the integral); the two estimates are derived here*

^thm-b1-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]] the time to go from $x < x_2$ to $x_2$ is $\sqrt{m/2}\int_x^{x_2}dx'/\sqrt{E - U(x')}$, an improper integral whose convergence is decided by the behaviour of $E - U$ near $x_2$. By Taylor's theorem, with $E = U(x_2)$:
> 1. $E - U(x') = U'(x_2)(x_2 - x') + O\bigl((x_2 - x')^2\bigr)$. The integrand behaves like $(x_2 - x')^{-1/2}$, and $\int_0^{\delta}s^{-1/2}\,ds = 2\sqrt\delta$ converges, so the time is finite. Keeping the leading term,
>
> $$
> \sqrt{\frac m2}\int_x^{x_2}\frac{dx'}{\sqrt{U'(x_2)(x_2 - x')}} = \sqrt{\frac{2m(x_2 - x)}{U'(x_2)}} = t_2 - t ,
> $$
>
> which inverts to $x_2 - x \approx U'(x_2)(t_2 - t)^2/2m$; after $t_2$ the motion is the mirror image (reversal in Theorem §B1.3.1). Equivalently: the force at $x_2$ is $-U'(x_2) \neq 0$, so the particle cannot stay there.
> 2. $E - U(x') = \tfrac12\lvert U''(x_2)\rvert(x_2 - x')^2\bigl(1 + o(1)\bigr)$ as $x' \to x_2$. The integrand behaves like $1/\bigl(\kappa\,(x_2 - x')\bigr)$; close enough to $x_2$ it is at least half of that, and $\int_0^\delta ds/s$ diverges, so by comparison the time is infinite. With the leading term, $t = -\kappa^{-1}\ln(x_2 - x) + \text{const}$, i.e. $x_2 - x \propto e^{-\kappa t}$.

^der-b1-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]], [[§36 Improper Integrals#^ex-36-1|451 Ex. §36.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.3: Period of Finite Motion
> If at energy $E$ the particle is confined between simple turning points $x_1(E) < x_2(E)$ with $U < E$ between them, its motion is periodic, with period
>
> $$
> T(E) = \sqrt{2m}\int_{x_1(E)}^{x_2(E)}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{E - U(x)}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Landau §11, eq. (11.5)*

^thm-b1-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Starting at $x_1$ at rest, the particle moves right and reaches $x_2$ after the finite time $t_{12} = \sqrt{m/2}\int_{x_1}^{x_2}dx/\sqrt{E - U}$ ([[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]], part 1, at both ends). It turns there, and by the reversal symmetry of [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]] retraces its path back to $x_1$ in the same time. It is then in its initial state (position $x_1$, velocity zero), so by uniqueness the motion repeats with period $2t_{12}$.

*Uses:* [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]], [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]

## Near an equilibrium

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.4: Motion near an Equilibrium
> Let $U'(x_0) = 0$ and $U''(x_0) \neq 0$, and write $\xi = x - x_0$. To first order in $\xi$ the equation of motion is $m\ddot\xi = -U''(x_0)\,\xi$, with solutions:
> 1. at a minimum, $U''(x_0) > 0$: $\xi = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$ with $\omega = \sqrt{U''(x_0)/m}$ ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-3|WO Theorem §A1.2.3]]); and the exact period of [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]] tends to $2\pi/\omega$ as $E \to U(x_0)$ from above;
> 2. at a maximum, $U''(x_0) < 0$: $\xi = Ae^{\kappa t} + Be^{-\kappa t}$ with $\kappa = \sqrt{-U''(x_0)/m}$. Unless $A = 0$ exactly, a small displacement grows exponentially until the linear approximation fails; $A = 0$ is the asymptotic approach of [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]], part 2.
>
> Level A classified the equilibria and gave the effective spring constant $U''(x_0)$ ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|Theorem §A4.7.2]]); this theorem solves the linearized motion in both cases and connects it with the exact period.
>
> *Source: Morin §5.2, eq. (5.20) · Morin §4.1 (Example 2: $\ddot x = ax$ for either sign of $a$) · Landau §11*

^thm-b1-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Taylor's theorem about $x_0$, with $U'(x_0) = 0$, gives $U = U(x_0) + \tfrac12U''(x_0)\xi^2 + O(\xi^3)$, so the force is $-U''(x_0)\xi + O(\xi^2)$ (Morin eq. (5.19)). The linear equation $\ddot\xi = -(U''/m)\,\xi$ has the characteristic roots $\pm i\omega$ or $\pm\kappa$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|WO Theorem §B1.1.3]]), which give the two forms.
>
> *Small-amplitude period.* With $k = U''(x_0)$ and the quadratic potential, the turning points are $\xi = \pm A$, $A = \sqrt{2(E - U(x_0))/k}$, and the substitution $\xi = A\sin s$ in [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]] gives
>
> $$
> T = \sqrt{2m}\int_{-A}^{A}\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{\tfrac12 k\,(A^2 - \xi^2)}} = \sqrt{2m}\,\sqrt{\frac2k}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}ds = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac mk} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega},
> $$
>
> independent of $A$. For the true $U$ the corrections are of relative order $A^2$, as [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^ex-b1-3-1|Example §B1.3.1]] shows for the pendulum.

^der-b1-3-4

*Uses:* [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|WO Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: When the curvature vanishes
> If $U''(x_0) = 0$, the linear approximation has no restoring term and the formula $\omega = \sqrt{U''/m}$ gives zero (Morin's footnote). The oscillations are then not harmonic at any amplitude; for $U \propto x^4$ the period grows without bound as the amplitude shrinks ([[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^ex-b1-3-2|Example §B1.3.2]]).

^cau-b1-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Can the period determine the potential?
> Only partly. Landau inverts the period integral, an Abel integral equation (Mathematical Methods, planned): for a well with a single minimum at $x = 0$, $U(0) = 0$, the width of the well at height $U$ is
>
> $$
> x_2(U) - x_1(U) = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{2m}}\int_0^U\frac{T(E)\,dE}{\sqrt{U - E}} .
> $$
>
> So $T(E)$ fixes the width at each height but not the shape: wells sheared sideways have the same periods. If the well is required to be symmetric, $x(U)$ is half the width and $U(x)$ is determined. Check: for $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ the integral gives width $2\sqrt{2U/k}$, the parabola $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$.
>
> *Source: Landau §12, eqs. (12.1)–(12.2)*

^rem-b1-3-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.3.1: The Pendulum at Finite Amplitude
> Find the period of a simple pendulum of length $l$ swinging with amplitude $\varphi_0$.
>
> With $\varphi$ the angle from the vertical, $E = \tfrac12 ml^2\dot\varphi^2 - mgl\cos\varphi = -mgl\cos\varphi_0$ ([[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^rem-b1-3-1|Remark: Beyond a Cartesian coordinate]], $a = ml^2$). Using $\cos\varphi - \cos\varphi_0 = 2\bigl(k^2 - \sin^2\tfrac\varphi2\bigr)$ with $k = \sin\tfrac{\varphi_0}{2}$, the angular speed is $\dot\varphi = 2\sqrt{g/l}\,\sqrt{k^2 - \sin^2(\varphi/2)}$, and four quarter-swings give
>
> $$
> T = 4\int_0^{\varphi_0}\frac{d\varphi}{\dot\varphi} = 2\sqrt{\frac lg}\int_0^{\varphi_0}\frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{k^2 - \sin^2(\varphi/2)}} .
> $$
>
> Substitute $\sin(\varphi/2) = k\sin\xi$: then $d\varphi = 2k\cos\xi\,d\xi/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\xi}$, the square root in the denominator becomes $k\cos\xi$, and $\xi$ runs from $0$ to $\pi/2$:
>
> $$
> T = 4\sqrt{\frac lg}\,K(k), \qquad K(k) = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\xi}} ,
> $$
>
> the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. Expanding the integrand, $K = \tfrac\pi2\bigl(1 + \tfrac14k^2 + \dots\bigr)$, and $k \approx \varphi_0/2$:
>
> $$
> T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac lg}\,\Bigl(1 + \frac{\varphi_0^2}{16} + \dots\Bigr).
> $$
>
> The period grows with amplitude: by $0.43\%$ at $15^\circ$ (the "$1\%$ at $15^\circ$" of [[§A1.4 Pendulums#^rem-a1-4-1|WO Remark: How small is small?]] is a safe bound), $7.3\%$ at $60^\circ$, $18\%$ at $90^\circ$. As $\varphi_0 \to 180^\circ$ the pendulum approaches the unstable upright position, $k \to 1$, and $K$ diverges logarithmically, as [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]], part 2, requires.
>
> *Source: Landau §11, Problem 1 (solved here)*

> [!example] Example §B1.3.2: Power-Law Wells
> Find the period of oscillation in $U = A\lvert x\rvert^n$ ($A > 0$, $n > 0$) as a function of the energy.
>
> The turning points are $\pm x_m$, $x_m = (E/A)^{1/n}$. By symmetry the period is four times the time from $0$ to $x_m$; with $x = x_m y$,
>
> $$
> T = 4\sqrt{\frac m2}\int_0^{x_m}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{E - Ax^n}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{2m}{E}}\Bigl(\frac EA\Bigr)^{1/n}\int_0^1\frac{dy}{\sqrt{1 - y^n}} = \frac2n\sqrt{\frac{2\pi m}{E}}\Bigl(\frac EA\Bigr)^{1/n}\frac{\Gamma(1/n)}{\Gamma(1/n + 1/2)} ,
> $$
>
> since $u = y^n$ turns the last integral into the beta integral $\tfrac1n B(\tfrac1n, \tfrac12) = \tfrac1n\Gamma(\tfrac1n)\Gamma(\tfrac12)/\Gamma(\tfrac1n + \tfrac12)$ (Mathematical Methods, planned). So $T \propto E^{1/n - 1/2}$:
> - $n = 2$: $T = \pi\sqrt{2m/A}$, independent of $E$; with $A = k/2$ this is $2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$. Only the harmonic well is isochronous.
> - $n = 4$: $T \propto E^{-1/4}$; higher energy, shorter period, and arbitrarily slow oscillation as $E \to 0$ ([[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^cau-b1-3-1|Caution: When the curvature vanishes]]).
> - $n \to \infty$ with $A = E_0/L^n$: the well becomes a box of half-width $L$, $\Gamma(1/n) \approx n$, and $T \to 2L\sqrt{2m/E} = 4L/v$, the time to bounce across and back.
>
> The power of $E$ follows from scaling alone (Landau's mechanical similarity, §10): if $U$ is homogeneous of degree $n$, stretching lengths by $\lambda$ multiplies energies by $\lambda^n$ and times by $\lambda^{1 - n/2}$. H&S's power-law example gives the companion result for the amplitude: for $U \propto x^{n+1}$, $x_{\max} \propto v_{\max}^{2/(n+1)}$.
>
> *Source: Landau §11, Problem 2(a) (solved here) · H&S Example 1.12*

> [!example] Example §B1.3.3: Over the Hump
> A particle moves under $F = -kx + kx^3/a^2$ ($k, a > 0$). Find $U$ with $U(0) = 0$ and its equilibria; describe the motion for a small energy $E > 0$; and describe what happens at $E = \tfrac14 ka^2$ when the particle, at rest, is nudged in either direction.
>
> *Potential and equilibria.* $U = \tfrac12 kx^2 - kx^4/4a^2$. $U'(x) = kx(1 - x^2/a^2)$ vanishes at $x = 0$, where $U'' = k > 0$ (stable), and at $x = \pm a$, where $U'' = k - 3k = -2k < 0$ (unstable), $U(\pm a) = \tfrac14 ka^2$.
>
> *Small $E$.* By [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]], harmonic oscillation about $0$ with $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ and amplitude $\sqrt{2E/k}$. A particle with the same small $E$ outside the humps ($\lvert x\rvert > a$) is in infinite motion: it runs off.
>
> *At the separatrix energy* $E_s = \tfrac14 ka^2$, $E_s - U = \frac{k}{4a^2}(a^2 - x^2)^2$, so $\tfrac12m\dot x^2 = E_s - U$ gives $\dot x = \pm\frac{\omega_0}{\sqrt2\,a}\lvert a^2 - x^2\rvert$. The only states at rest are the humps $x = \pm a$.
> - Nudged from $x = -a$ toward the centre: $\int dx/(a^2 - x^2) = \frac1a\operatorname{artanh}(x/a)$ gives
>
> $$
> x(t) = a\tanh\frac{\omega_0 t}{\sqrt2},
> $$
>
> with $t = 0$ at the centre. The particle crosses the well and creeps up to the other hump, reaching it only as $t \to \infty$; near it $a - x \approx 2a\,e^{-\sqrt2\,\omega_0 t}$, the rate $\kappa = \sqrt{2k/m} = \sqrt2\,\omega_0$ of [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]]. (Strictly, a particle exactly at $E_s$ needs infinite time to leave the hump too: $x(t)$ came from $-a$ at $t = -\infty$.)
> - Nudged outward from $x = a$: now $\dot x = \frac{\omega_0}{\sqrt2\,a}(x^2 - a^2)$, and integration gives $x(t) = a\coth\bigl(\omega_0(t_\infty - t)/\sqrt2\bigr)$, which reaches infinity at the **finite** time $t_\infty$, because $\int^\infty dx/x^2$ converges. The force grows like $x^3$, and the solution ceases to exist in finite time, the possibility left open in [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]].
>
> *Source: PHY 421, Midterm 1 (Fall 2021), Problem 2 (statement; solved here)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Convergence at simple turning points and divergence at a maximum are the improper integrals $\int_0 s^{-p}\,ds$ with $p = \tfrac12$ and $p = 1$ ([[§36 Improper Integrals#^def-36-1|451 Def. §36.1]]; the divergent case is [[§36 Improper Integrals#^ex-36-1|451 Ex. §36.1]]); the local analysis is Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).

The pendulum's phase portrait, with libration, rotation and its separatrix, is [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^ex-b7-2-1|Example §B7.2.1]].
> - Damped and driven oscillations about a minimum are [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator|WO §B1.2]] and [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator|WO §B1.3]]; small oscillations of systems with several coordinates are [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^thm-b6-5-3|Theorem §B6.5.3]].
> - Quantum side: finite and infinite motion (Def. §B1.3.1) become bound and scattering states, and bound energies become discrete — [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^rem-b3-1-5|QM Remark: Bound and scattering states, and why bound energies are discrete]]; no stationary state lies at or below the minimum of the potential — [[§B3.1 General Properties of One-Dimensional Eigenstates#^thm-b3-1-3|QM Theorem §B3.1.3]].
