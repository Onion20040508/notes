*Sources: Morin, Introduction to Classical Mechanics, §5.1, §5.3, §5.5 · Helliwell & Sahakian, Modern Classical Mechanics, §1.8 · Landau & Lifshitz, Mechanics, §6–9 · PHY 421 (Zhou, Fall 2023).*

Which combinations of a particle's position and velocity stay constant while it moves, and under exactly which conditions? Level A proved conservation of momentum for an isolated system ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]), of angular momentum under zero external torque ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-4|Theorem §A6.6.4]]), and of mechanical energy when only conservative forces do work ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]), with the closed-path and two-dimensional tests for conservative forces ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-1|Theorem §A4.5.1]], [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]). This section states the three theorems for a single particle with their exact hypotheses: conservation of single components, the dependence of angular momentum on the origin, the curl criterion and the condition on the region it needs, and the energy balance when forces do work or the potential energy depends on time. Throughout, $U$ is the potential energy (Morin writes $V$).

## Integrals of the motion

> [!definition] Definition §B1.2.1: Integral of the Motion
> A function $f(\vec r, \vec v, t)$ is an **integral of the motion** (a **constant of the motion**, a **conserved quantity**) if $f\bigl(\vec r(t), \dot{\vec r}(t), t\bigr)$ is constant along every solution of the equations of motion. Each integral replaces one of the second-order equations by a first-order one (a **first integral**).
>
> *Source: Landau §6 · H&S §1.8*

^def-b1-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: How many, and which ones matter
> The motion of a particle in space depends on six constants ([[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]). For a system with $s$ degrees of freedom whose equations do not contain $t$ explicitly, Landau counts $2s - 1$ independent time-independent integrals: solve for the $2s$ constants of the general solution in terms of $(q, \dot q)$, and discard the one that only shifts the origin of time. Most of these are useless in practice. The three that matter are energy, momentum and angular momentum: they are **additive** (for non-interacting parts they are sums over the parts), which is what lets one compare a system before and after an interaction, as in collisions ([[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames|§B4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Landau §6*

^rem-b1-2-1

## Momentum and angular momentum

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.1: Momentum Theorem for a Particle
> Along any motion, $d\vec p/dt = \vec F$, the net force. Hence:
> 1. if $\vec F = \vec 0$ throughout a time interval, $\vec p$ is constant on it;
> 2. if $\hat n$ is a fixed unit vector and $\vec F\cdot\hat n = 0$ at every point of the motion, the component $\vec p\cdot\hat n$ is constant, whatever the other components do;
> 3. in particular, if $\vec F = -\nabla U$ and $U$ does not depend on the Cartesian coordinate $x$, then $p_x$ is conserved.
>
> *Source: H&S §1.8 (Momentum) · Landau §7*

^thm-b1-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $d\vec p/dt = \vec F$ is the second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|Principle §A3.3.3]]). For a constant $\hat n$, $\frac{d}{dt}(\vec p\cdot\hat n) = \vec F\cdot\hat n$, which vanishes under hypothesis 2; hypothesis 1 is the case of all three directions. For 3, $F_x = -\partial U/\partial x = 0$.

^der-b1-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|Principle §A3.3.3]], [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: For one particle this is the first law
> For a single particle, part 1 is Newton's first law. Momentum conservation has real content only for systems, where internal forces change the individual momenta but not their sum ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]], [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]]). Part 2 is the useful part for a particle: in a uniform gravitational field $-mg\,\hat z$, $p_x$ and $p_y$ are conserved while $p_z$ is not.
>
> *Source: H&S §1.8 · Landau §7*

^rem-b1-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.2: Angular Momentum Theorem about a Fixed Point
> Let $O$ be a point fixed in an inertial frame, and measure $\vec r$ from $O$. The angular momentum $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p$ and torque $\vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F$ about $O$ (written $\vec l$ for a particle at level A, [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-1|Def. §A6.6.1]]) satisfy $d\vec L/dt = \vec\tau$. Hence:
> 1. $\vec L$ is conserved exactly when $\vec F$ is parallel or antiparallel to $\vec r$ at every instant (or zero). This holds for a **central force** $\vec F = f\,\hat r$ about its centre, and in general about no other point.
> 2. If $\hat z$ is a fixed direction and $\tau_z = 0$, then $L_z$ is conserved. For $\vec F = -\nabla U$, with $\varphi$ the azimuth about the $z$-axis through $O$ (cylindrical coordinates),
>
> $$
> \tau_z = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial\varphi},
> $$
>
> so $L_z$ is conserved whenever $U$ is symmetric under rotations about that axis.
> 3. For another fixed point $O' = O + \vec a$, $\vec L_{O'} = \vec L_O - \vec a\times\vec p$: angular momentum depends on the point, unless $\vec p = \vec 0$.
> 4. In coordinates centred at $O$:
>
> $$
> L_z = mr^2\dot\varphi \ \ \text{(cylindrical)}, \qquad L_z = mr^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi, \quad L^2 = m^2r^4\bigl(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2\bigr) \ \ \text{(spherical)} .
> $$
>
> *Source: H&S §1.8 (Angular momentum; Example 1.6) · Landau §9, eqs. (9.4), (9.8), Problems 1–2*

^thm-b1-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $d\vec L/dt = \vec\tau$ is [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-1|Theorem §A6.6.1]].
> 1. $\vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F = \vec 0$ exactly when $\vec F$ is parallel to $\vec r$ or zero. For a central force about $O$ this holds; about $O'$ the torque is $(\vec r - \vec a)\times\vec F = -\vec a\times\vec F$, nonzero unless $\vec F \parallel \vec a$, which fails as soon as the particle moves off the line through $O$ and $O'$.
> 2. $\frac{d}{dt}(\vec L\cdot\hat z) = \vec\tau\cdot\hat z$. With $\vec r = r\hat r + z\hat z$, the $z$-component of $\vec r\times\vec F$ is $rF_\varphi$ ($\hat r\times\hat\varphi = \hat z$; the other cross products have no $z$-component). The cylindrical gradient has $\varphi$-component $\frac1r\,\partial U/\partial\varphi$, so $\tau_z = rF_\varphi = -\partial U/\partial\varphi$.
> 3. $\vec r_{O'} = \vec r - \vec a$, so $\vec L_{O'} = (\vec r - \vec a)\times\vec p = \vec L_O - \vec a\times\vec p$.
> 4. Cylindrical: in $\vec r\times m\vec v = (r\hat r + z\hat z)\times m(\dot r\,\hat r + r\dot\varphi\,\hat\varphi + \dot z\,\hat z)$ only $r\hat r\times mr\dot\varphi\,\hat\varphi = mr^2\dot\varphi\,\hat z$ has a $z$-component. Spherical: with the velocity of [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]] and $\hat r\times\hat\theta = \hat\varphi$, $\hat r\times\hat\varphi = -\hat\theta$,
>
> $$
> \vec L = mr\,\hat r\times\bigl(\dot r\,\hat r + r\dot\theta\,\hat\theta + r\sin\theta\,\dot\varphi\,\hat\varphi\bigr) = mr^2\bigl(\dot\theta\,\hat\varphi - \sin\theta\,\dot\varphi\,\hat\theta\bigr),
> $$
>
> which gives $L^2$; and $\hat z\cdot\hat\varphi = 0$, $\hat z\cdot\hat\theta = -\sin\theta$ give $L_z$.
>
> Consistency: $rF_\varphi = \frac{d}{dt}(mr^2\dot\varphi)$ is the $\varphi$-equation of [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]], and $r\sin\theta\,F_\varphi = \frac{d}{dt}(mr^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi)$ the $\varphi$-equation of [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]].

^der-b1-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Name the point
> "Angular momentum is conserved" means nothing until the point is named. A particle on a spring $\vec F = -k\vec r$ fixed at the origin keeps $\vec L$ about the origin, $m(x\dot y - y\dot x)\,\hat z$, but not about any other point of the plane (H&S Example 1.6). A free particle keeps $\vec L$ about every fixed point, with different values ([[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-2|part 3]]). The point must be fixed in an inertial frame; for systems and for moving points see [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]] and [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]].

## Conservative forces

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.3: Criteria for a Conservative Force
> Let $\vec F(\vec r)$ depend on position only and have continuous first partial derivatives on an open region $D$. Consider the statements:
> 1. the work $\int_A^B \vec F\cdot d\vec r$ is the same along every path in $D$ from $A$ to $B$;
> 2. $\oint_C \vec F\cdot d\vec r = 0$ for every closed path $C$ in $D$;
> 3. $\vec F = -\nabla U$ for some function $U$ on $D$;
> 4. $\nabla\times\vec F = \vec 0$ throughout $D$.
>
> Then $1 \Leftrightarrow 2 \Leftrightarrow 3 \Rightarrow 4$. If $D$ is star-shaped (for instance all of space, a ball or a half-space), also $4 \Rightarrow 3$, so all four are equivalent. The potential energy is then $U(\vec r) = -\int_{\vec r_0}^{\vec r}\vec F\cdot d\vec r'$, unique up to an additive constant.
>
> *Source: H&S §1.8 (the four equivalent tests) · Morin §5.3, Theorem 5.2 and its footnote 8*

^thm-b1-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> - $1 \Leftrightarrow 2$: [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-1|Theorem §A4.5.1]].
> - $1 \Rightarrow 3$: fix $\vec r_0$ and define $U(\vec r) = -\int_{\vec r_0}^{\vec r}\vec F\cdot d\vec r'$ along any path; by 1 the value does not depend on the path. Moving the endpoint by $d\vec r$ along a short straight segment changes $U$ by $dU = -\vec F\cdot d\vec r$, while $dU = \nabla U\cdot d\vec r$; for arbitrary $d\vec r$ this gives $\vec F = -\nabla U$ (Morin eqs. (5.30)–(5.32)).
> - $3 \Rightarrow 1$: along a path $\vec r(s)$ from $A$ to $B$, $\vec F\cdot\frac{d\vec r}{ds} = -\frac{d}{ds}U(\vec r(s))$ by the chain rule, so the work is $U(A) - U(B)$, independent of the path.
> - $3 \Rightarrow 4$: $U$ has continuous second partial derivatives (its first ones are $-F_i$), so the mixed partials commute and $\nabla\times\nabla U = \vec 0$; for instance $(\nabla\times\nabla U)_z = \partial_x\partial_y U - \partial_y\partial_x U = 0$. For a force in the plane, statement 4 is the single condition $\partial F_x/\partial y = \partial F_y/\partial x$ of [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]].
> - $4 \Rightarrow 3$ on a star-shaped $D$: in the language of differential forms, $\nabla\times\vec F = \vec 0$ says that the 1-form $F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy + F_z\,dz$ is closed, and the Poincaré lemma makes a closed form on a star-shaped domain exact, i.e. a differential $-dU$. Morin's argument instead takes a closed curve that bounds a surface in $D$ and applies Stokes' theorem: $\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r = \iint_S(\nabla\times\vec F)\cdot d\vec S = 0$. This is the converse that level A stated without proof for regions "without holes" ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^rem-a4-5-2|Remark: When the test is also sufficient]]); star-shaped is a precise sufficient condition.

^der-b1-2-3

> [!caution] Caution: A curl-free force need not be conservative
> On space with the $z$-axis removed, take $\vec F = \kappa\,\dfrac{-y\,\hat x + x\,\hat y}{x^2 + y^2} = \dfrac{\kappa}{r}\,\hat\varphi$ (cylindrical $r$). Its curl vanishes everywhere it is defined, yet once around the axis on a circle of radius $r$,
>
> $$
> \oint\vec F\cdot d\vec r = \frac{\kappa}{r}\cdot 2\pi r = 2\pi\kappa \neq 0 .
> $$
>
> Locally $\vec F = -\nabla(-\kappa\varphi)$, but the angle $\varphi$ cannot be defined continuously around the axis ([[Angle form on the punctured plane]]). The region has a hole, so it is not star-shaped and Stokes' theorem cannot be used for curves around the axis. This is the reason for the region condition in [[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]] and in Morin's footnote: a force that is infinite somewhere (on the axis here) needs a second look. The magnetic field around a long straight wire has exactly this form ([[§A8.2 Straight Wires and Parallel Currents#^thm-a8-2-2|EM Theorem §A8.2.2]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.4: Central Forces Are Conservative
> For every differentiable $g(r)$ of $r = \lvert\vec r\rvert$,
>
> $$
> \nabla g(r) = g'(r)\,\hat r = \frac{\vec r}{r}\,\frac{dg}{dr} \qquad (r \neq 0) .
> $$
>
> Hence a central force $\vec F = f(r)\,\hat r$ with $f$ continuous for $r > 0$ is conservative on space without its centre, with potential energy
>
> $$
> U(r) = -\int_{r_0}^{r} f(r')\,dr' .
> $$
>
> For power laws $f = -kr^n$: $U = kr^{n+1}/(n+1)$ for $n \neq -1$, and $U = k\ln r$ for $n = -1$; gravity ($n = -2$, $k = GMm$, $r_0 = \infty$) gives $U = -GMm/r$ ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 421, Midterm 1 (Fall 2016), Problem 1 (the gradient formula) · Morin §5.3 (Example: central force) · H&S Example 1.10, Problem 1.36*

^thm-b1-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$, $\partial r/\partial x = x/r$, and likewise for $y$, $z$. By the chain rule $\partial g/\partial x = g'(r)\,x/r$, so $\nabla g = g'(r)\,(x, y, z)/r = g'(r)\,\hat r$. With $U$ as defined, $U'(r) = -f(r)$ by the fundamental theorem of calculus, so $-\nabla U = f(r)\,\hat r = \vec F$: statement 3 of [[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]] holds directly on the whole region $r > 0$, and no curl needs to be computed (the region, space minus a point, is not star-shaped, but statement 3 implies the others). The power laws follow by integrating $kr^n$.

^der-b1-2-4

## Energy

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.5: Energy Theorem for a Particle
> Split the net force on a particle as $\vec F = -\nabla U(\vec r, t) + \vec F'$, where $U$ has continuous partial derivatives in $\vec r$ and $t$ and $\vec F'$ is everything else. Then along the motion the energy $E = \tfrac12 mv^2 + U(\vec r, t)$ changes at the rate
>
> $$
> \frac{dE}{dt} = \vec F'\cdot\vec v + \frac{\partial U}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> Hence $E$ is conserved if and only if, along the motion, the power of the other forces is cancelled by $\partial U/\partial t$; in particular it is conserved when
> 1. the other forces do no work: $\vec F'\cdot\vec v = 0$ at every instant (for example a magnetic force $q\vec v\times\vec B$, the normal force of a fixed frictionless surface, the tension of a string whose support is fixed), and
> 2. $U$ does not depend explicitly on time.
>
> Over a finite time, $\Delta E = W' + \int(\partial U/\partial t)\,dt$, with $W'$ the work of $\vec F'$.
>
> *Source: H&S §1.8 (Energy), eqs. (1.94)–(1.110), Example 1.7 · Landau §6 (energy is conserved for a closed system and in a constant external field) · Morin §5.1, §5.3*

^thm-b1-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The kinetic energy changes at the rate $\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\tfrac12 m\,\vec v\cdot\vec v\bigr) = m\vec v\cdot\dot{\vec v} = \vec F\cdot\vec v$, the power of the net force ([[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]). By the chain rule $\frac{d}{dt}U(\vec r(t), t) = \nabla U\cdot\vec v + \partial U/\partial t$. Adding,
>
> $$
> \frac{dE}{dt} = \bigl(-\nabla U + \vec F'\bigr)\cdot\vec v + \nabla U\cdot\vec v + \frac{\partial U}{\partial t} = \vec F'\cdot\vec v + \frac{\partial U}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> Integrating over time gives the finite form, with $W' = \int\vec F'\cdot\vec v\,dt = \int\vec F'\cdot d\vec r$. For $\vec F = q\vec v\times\vec B$, $\vec v\cdot(\vec v\times\vec B) = 0$; a normal force of a fixed surface, or the tension of a string of fixed length on a fixed support, is perpendicular to the velocity.

^der-b1-2-5

*Uses:* [[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]], [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]], [[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|EM Principle §A7.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What level B adds to energy conservation
> - Level A required "only conservative forces do work" ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]). Forces that do no work at all, of whatever kind, are harmless: they may be velocity-dependent (magnetic) or unknown in advance (normal forces, tensions).
> - A force $-\nabla U(\vec r, t)$ with time-dependent $U$ is path-independent at each instant, yet energy is not conserved: a particle in a potential well whose depth is changed gains or loses energy at the rate $\partial U/\partial t$. Landau's condition is a "constant external field".
> - The energy $E$ is an integral of the motion that contains the velocity only through $v^2$; this is what makes one-dimensional motion solvable by quadrature ([[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method|§B1.3]]).
>
> *Source: H&S §1.8 · Landau §6*

## Conservation laws and symmetries

> [!remark] Remark: Each conservation law reflects a symmetry
> The hypotheses of the three theorems are symmetries of the potential energy:
>
> | conserved | hypothesis (this section) | symmetry |
> |---|---|---|
> | $E$ | $U$ does not depend on $t$ | homogeneity of time |
> | $p_x$ | $U$ does not depend on $x$ | translations along $x$ |
> | $L_z$ | $U$ does not depend on the azimuth about the $z$-axis | rotations about that axis |
>
> Here each was proved separately from Newton's second law. Landau derives all of them from one idea: the Lagrangian of a closed system is unchanged by shifts in time, translations and rotations, and each invariance yields a conserved quantity; a closed system has exactly seven additive integrals (energy, three components of momentum, three of angular momentum). This is Noether's theorem ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]], [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-5|Theorem §B6.4.5]]): there the conservation laws change layer, from consequences of Newton's laws to consequences of symmetries of the action. H&S postpone the connection to their Chapter 6 in the same way.
>
> *Source: Landau §6–9 · H&S §1.8*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.2.1: The Spherical Pendulum
> A bob of mass $m$ on a light string of length $R$ hangs from a fixed point $O$ and moves in all directions. Which quantities are conserved, and why?
>
> Take spherical coordinates centred at $O$ with the polar axis pointing **down**, so $\theta$ is the angle of the string from the downward vertical and gravity is $mg\,\hat z$, $U = -mgz = -mgR\cos\theta$. With $r = R$ fixed, [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]] gives $v^2 = R^2(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2)$.
> - *Energy.* The tension is along the string, perpendicular to the velocity of a bob on a sphere about the fixed $O$, so it does no work; gravity is conservative and time-independent. By [[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac12 mR^2\bigl(\dot\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2\bigr) - mgR\cos\theta = \text{const}.
> $$
>
> - *Angular momentum about the vertical through $O$.* The tension passes through $O$ (no torque about $O$); gravity is parallel to the $z$-axis, so its torque $\vec r\times mg\hat z$ is horizontal. So $\tau_z = 0$ and by [[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]
>
> $$
> L_z = mR^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi = \text{const}.
> $$
>
> The horizontal components of $\vec L$ are not conserved: gravity's torque is horizontal.
>
> Eliminating $\dot\varphi$ leaves one equation in $\theta$ alone,
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac12 mR^2\dot\theta^2 + \frac{L_z^2}{2mR^2\sin^2\theta} - mgR\cos\theta ,
> $$
>
> a one-dimensional energy equation to which [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method|§B1.3]] applies. Motion at constant $\theta_0$ (the conical pendulum, [[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^ex-a3-9-2|Example §A3.9.2]]) needs the $\theta$-dependent part to be stationary: $L_z^2\cos\theta_0/(mR^2\sin^3\theta_0) = mgR\sin\theta_0$, which with $L_z = mR^2\sin^2\theta_0\,\dot\varphi$ gives $\dot\varphi^2 = g/(R\cos\theta_0)$, the level-A result.
>
> *Source: H&S Problem 1.35 (solved here)*

^ex-b1-2-1

> [!example] Example §B1.2.2: Partial Symmetries
> Which components of momentum and angular momentum are conserved for a particle in the gravitational field of (a) an infinite uniform plane, (b) an infinite uniform cylinder, (c) two point masses, (d) an infinite uniform helix of pitch $h$?
>
> Use parts 2 of [[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]] and [[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]: a component is conserved when $U$ does not change under the corresponding translation or rotation.
> - (a) Plane $z = 0$: $U = U(z)$, so $p_x$, $p_y$ and $L_z$ (about any point of the $z$-axis, indeed about any vertical axis) are conserved.
> - (b) Cylinder along $z$: $U = U(r)$ in cylindrical coordinates, so $p_z$ and $L_z$ about the axis.
> - (c) Two points on the $z$-axis: $U$ depends on $r$ and $z$ but not on the azimuth, so only $L_z$ about the line through them.
> - (d) Helix along $z$: $U$ is unchanged by a rotation through $\delta\varphi$ combined with a shift $\delta z = h\,\delta\varphi/2\pi$, so it depends on $\varphi$ and $z$ only through $\chi = \varphi - 2\pi z/h$: $U = U(r, \chi)$. Then $\partial U/\partial\varphi = \partial U/\partial\chi$ and $\partial U/\partial z = -(2\pi/h)\,\partial U/\partial\chi$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{dL_z}{dt} + \frac{h}{2\pi}\frac{dp_z}{dt} = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial\varphi} - \frac{h}{2\pi}\frac{\partial U}{\partial z} = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial\chi} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial\chi} = 0 .
> $$
>
> Neither $L_z$ nor $p_z$ is conserved, but $L_z + hp_z/2\pi$ is. A screw symmetry gives a conserved combination.
>
> *Source: Landau §9, Problem 3 (solved here by the Newtonian route; Landau argues from the Lagrangian)*
