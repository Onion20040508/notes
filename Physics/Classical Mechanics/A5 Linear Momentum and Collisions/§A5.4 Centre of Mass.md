Every real object is made of many particles pushing and pulling on each other: a falling cat twists, a firework shell bursts. Which point of such a system obeys Newton's second law with the net external force? Combining $d\vec P/dt = \vec F_{\text{ext}}$ ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]]) with the right weighted average of the particles' positions answers it: the centre of mass. This is what justifies treating extended bodies as particles.

## Definition

> [!definition] Definition §A5.4.1: Centre of Mass
> For particles of masses $m_j$ at positions $\vec r_j$, with total mass $M = \sum_j m_j$, the **centre of mass** is the mass-weighted average position
>
> $$
> \vec r_{\text{CM}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^N m_j\,\vec r_j, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad x_{\text{CM}} = \frac1M\sum_j m_j x_j \ \text{ and likewise for } y, z .
> $$
>
> For a continuous body the sum becomes an integral over mass elements, $\vec r_{\text{CM}} = \frac1M\int \vec r\,dm$, with $dm$ written through a density, for a thin wire $dm = \lambda\,ds$ ($\lambda$ in kg/m). The velocity of the centre of mass is $\vec v_{\text{CM}} = d\vec r_{\text{CM}}/dt = \frac1M\sum_j m_j\vec v_j$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.6, eqs. (9.29)–(9.35)*

^def-a5-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Practical points
> - Choosing the origin at one of the particles makes its term zero, but its mass still counts in $M$: leaving Earth's mass out of the Earth–Moon calculation would put the centre of mass at the Moon.
> - There need not be any mass at the centre of mass: a ring, a hollow sphere, a doughnut, or a cubic cell of a salt crystal all have their centre of mass in empty space.
> - Symmetry often gives the answer at once: a uniform ring or sphere has it at the geometric centre; a body symmetric about a line has it on that line.

^rem-a5-4-1

## Motion of the centre of mass

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.4.1: Motion of the Centre of Mass
> The total momentum of a system is that of a single particle of mass $M$ moving with the centre of mass, and the centre of mass accelerates as if the net external force acted on such a particle:
>
> $$
> \vec P = M\,\vec v_{\text{CM}}, \qquad M\,\vec a_{\text{CM}} = \vec F_{\text{ext}} .
> $$
>
> In particular, for a closed system the velocity of the centre of mass never changes.
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.6, eqs. (9.26)–(9.28), (9.36)–(9.37)*

^thm-a5-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate $M\vec r_{\text{CM}} = \sum_j m_j\vec r_j$ (fixed masses): $M\vec v_{\text{CM}} = \sum_j m_j\vec v_j = \vec P$. Differentiate again and use [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]]: $M\vec a_{\text{CM}} = d\vec P/dt = \vec F_{\text{ext}}$. If the system is closed, $\vec F_{\text{ext}} = \vec 0$ and $\vec v_{\text{CM}}$ is constant.

^der-a5-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]], [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^def-a3-3-2|Def. §A3.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why this is not a coincidence
> This is more than the first law: it concerns a point where there may be no particle at all, in a system whose parts accelerate every which way under internal forces. It is not magic that the fragments of an explosion "conspire" to keep the centre of mass on course: $M\vec a_{\text{CM}} = \sum_j m_j\vec a_j$ is the sum of all the forces on all the particles, and in that sum the internal forces cancel in third-law pairs. The mass-weighted average is exactly the average that makes this bookkeeping work. When a firework shell bursts, its centre of mass continues on the shell's parabola (until fragments reach the ground), and each fragment traces its own parabola around it. "Gravity acts at the centre of mass" is shorthand for this, and, for torques in a uniform field, for [[§A7.1 Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-a7-1-3|Theorem §A7.1.3]].

^rem-a5-4-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.4.1: Earth and Moon
> With the origin at Earth's centre ($m_E = 5.97\times10^{24}$ kg) and the Moon ($m_M = 7.36\times10^{22}$ kg) at $r_M = 3.82\times10^8$ m,
>
> $$
> R_{\text{CM}} = \frac{m_M r_M}{m_E + m_M} = \frac{(7.36\times10^{22})(3.82\times10^8)}{6.04\times10^{24}}\ \mathrm m = 4.65\times10^6\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> Earth's radius is $6.37\times10^6$ m, so the centre of mass of the pair lies inside Earth, about 1720 km below the surface.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 9.16; PHY 181, TBL 21*

^ex-a5-4-1

> [!example] Example §A5.4.2: Semicircular Wire
> Where is the centre of mass of a uniform thin wire of mass $M$ bent into a semicircle of radius $R$? Put the centre of the circle at the origin and the wire in $y \ge 0$. By symmetry $x_{\text{CM}} = 0$. The element at angle $\theta$ has length $ds = R\,d\theta$ and mass $dm = \lambda R\,d\theta$ with $\lambda = M/(\pi R)$, and height $y = R\sin\theta$:
>
> $$
> y_{\text{CM}} = \frac1M\int_0^\pi R\sin\theta\,\frac{M}{\pi R}\,R\,d\theta = \frac{R}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{2R}{\pi} \approx 0.64\,R .
> $$
>
> The centre of mass lies on the symmetry axis, below the top of the arc and *off* the wire, in the empty region it encloses.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 21; method of UP1 Example 9.18*

^ex-a5-4-2

> [!example] Example §A5.4.3: Pulling Together on Ice
> A 90 kg adult and a 30 kg child stand at rest on frictionless ice, holding the ends of a 20 m rope. The adult pulls himself 3.0 m toward the child. How far does the child move, and how fast is the child moving when the adult moves at 0.50 m/s?
>
> The horizontal external force is zero and the system starts at rest, so the centre of mass stays put (with the adult at $x = 0$ and the child at $x = 20$ m, it is at $x = 5.0$ m). Hence $m_a\,\Delta x_a + m_c\,\Delta x_c = 0$:
>
> $$
> \Delta x_c = -\frac{90}{30}\,(3.0\ \mathrm m) = -9.0\ \mathrm m, \qquad v_c = \frac{90}{30}\,(0.50\ \mathrm{m/s}) = 1.5\ \mathrm{m/s} \ \text{(toward the adult)} .
> $$
>
> On ground with friction, by contrast, the adult can run toward a standing child: then the centre of mass moves, at $(90)(0.50)/120 = 0.375$ m/s, pushed by friction from the ground.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 21–22*
