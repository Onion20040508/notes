Kinetic energy ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem|§A4.2]]) measures the quantity of motion with a scalar and forgets its direction. Momentum keeps it. This section asks what a force acting for some time does to a body's momentum; the answer, the impulse–momentum theorem, is the time counterpart of the work–energy theorem and the tool for brief, violent forces such as impacts.

## Momentum

> [!remark] Remark: Momentum, recalled
> The momentum $\vec p = m\vec v$ of a body was defined with the second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^def-a3-3-2|Def. §A3.3.2]]), which in its general form reads $\vec F_{\text{net}} = d\vec p/dt$ ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|Principle §A3.3.3]]). It is a vector along $\vec v$, which is what makes it more than a rewriting of $K = p^2/2m$. Unlike $K$, it depends linearly on mass and speed alike: an air molecule ($6\times10^{-25}$ kg at 500 m/s) has $p = 3\times10^{-22}$ kg·m/s, a 1400 kg car at 15 m/s has $2.1\times10^4$ kg·m/s. A large momentum is hard to change (a loaded supertanker), a small one easy (a gas molecule hitting a wall).

^rem-a5-1-1

## Impulse

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.1: Impulse; Average Force
> A force $\vec F(t)$ acting during a time $dt$ gives the **impulse** $d\vec J = \vec F(t)\,dt$; over the interval from $t_i$ to $t_f$,
>
> $$
> \vec J = \int_{t_i}^{t_f} \vec F(t)\,dt .
> $$
>
> The **average force** over the interval $\Delta t = t_f - t_i$ is $\vec F_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_{t_i}^{t_f}\vec F(t)\,dt$, so $\vec J = \vec F_{\text{ave}}\,\Delta t$. Impulse is a vector, measured in N·s = kg·m/s; for one component it is the area under the graph of $F(t)$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.2, eqs. (9.2)–(9.5)*

^def-a5-1-1

![[ph-cm-a5-1-1.svg]]
*An impact force $F(t)$ (blue) rises to $F_{\max}$ and dies away. The area under it is the impulse $J$; the average force $F_{\text{ave}}$ is the height of the rectangle (red) with the same area over the same $\Delta t$.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.1: Impulse–Momentum Theorem
> The impulse of the net force on a body equals the change of its momentum:
>
> $$
> \vec J_{\text{net}} = \int_{t_i}^{t_f} \vec F_{\text{net}}\,dt = \vec p_f - \vec p_i = \Delta\vec p,
> \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
> \vec F_{\text{net,ave}} = \frac{\Delta \vec p}{\Delta t} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.2, eqs. (9.6)–(9.8)*

^thm-a5-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|Principle §A3.3.3]], $\vec F_{\text{net}}(t) = d\vec p/dt$. Integrate both sides, component by component, from $t_i$ to $t_f$: the left side is $\vec J_{\text{net}}$, the right side is $\vec p(t_f) - \vec p(t_i)$. Dividing by $\Delta t$ gives the average form.

^der-a5-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|Principle §A3.3.3]], [[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^def-a5-1-1|Def. §A5.1.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|FTC I (Theorem §34.1)]]

> [!remark] Remark: Time versus distance
> The second law integrated over *time* gives $\int \vec F_{\text{net}}\,dt = \Delta\vec p$; integrated over *distance* it gives $\int \vec F_{\text{net}}\cdot d\vec r = \Delta K$ ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]]). The impulse form is the natural one when the duration of a force is known or estimated, as in collisions lasting milliseconds; in practice one usually measures $\Delta\vec p$ and estimates $\Delta t$ to find the average force. Because it starts from $d\vec p/dt$, it also remains valid when the mass changes ([[§A5.5 Rocket Propulsion|§A5.5]]).

^rem-a5-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Impulse is a vector
> - $\Delta\vec p = \vec p_f - \vec p_i$ must be formed with signs. A 0.25 kg ball dropped from 1.50 m hits the floor at $5.4$ m/s. If it stops dead, $\Delta p = 1.4$ kg·m/s upward; if it bounces back to its starting height, its velocity reverses and $\Delta p = 2.7$ kg·m/s upward: bouncing doubles the impulse, and the force, for the same contact time.
> - An impulse does not give a body momentum from nothing; it *changes* the momentum it already has.

^cau-a5-1-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.1.1: Half the Force
> A constant force acts for a time $\Delta t$ on an object initially at rest on a frictionless surface and gives it a certain final speed. How long must a force half as large act to give the same final speed?
>
> The vertical forces cancel, so the horizontal force is the net force and $\Delta p = F\,\Delta t$, starting from $p_i = 0$. The same final speed means the same final momentum, $m v_f$:
>
> $$
> \tfrac12 F\,\Delta t' = F\,\Delta t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta t' = 2\,\Delta t ,
> $$
>
> twice as long: the impulse $F\Delta t$, not the force alone, fixes the momentum. By the same token, the original force acting for the original time on an object of twice the mass gives it the same momentum and so half the speed.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 8*

^ex-a5-1-1

> [!example] Example §A5.1.2: Same Distance, Different Time
> Identical constant forces push two identical objects from a start line to a finish line; $A$ starts at rest, $B$ is already moving forward. Which has the larger change of kinetic energy, and which the larger change of momentum?
> - Same force over the same distance: the same work, so by the work–energy theorem the **same** $\Delta K$.
> - $B$ is faster and crosses the stretch in less time, so it receives a smaller impulse: $A$ has the **larger** $\Delta p$.
>
> Force times distance fixes $\Delta K$; force times time fixes $\Delta p$.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 20*

^ex-a5-1-2

> [!example] Example §A5.1.3: Why Airbags Work
> A car moving at 27 m/s hits a wall. The driver (weight 860 N, mass $860/9.8 = 87.8$ kg) is stopped by seatbelt and airbag in about 2.5 s. Taking $+x$ along the car's motion,
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{ave}} = \frac{m\,(0 - v_i)}{\Delta t}\,\hat\imath = \frac{(87.8\ \mathrm{kg})(-27\ \mathrm{m/s})}{2.5\ \mathrm s}\,\hat\imath = -(948\ \mathrm N)\,\hat\imath ,
> $$
>
> about 1.1 times his weight. Stopped by the steering wheel in 0.20 s instead, he would feel $1.19\times10^4$ N, about 14 times his weight. The change of momentum is the same either way; the restraint only spreads it over a longer time.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 9.2*

^ex-a5-1-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The theorem is the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|FTC I]], Single Variable Analysis) applied to each component of $\vec p(t)$; the average force is the mean value of $\vec F$ over the interval.
