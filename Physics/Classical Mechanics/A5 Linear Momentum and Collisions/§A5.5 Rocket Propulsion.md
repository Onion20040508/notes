A rocket speeds up by throwing mass backwards, so its own mass changes as it goes. Then $\vec F = m\vec a$ cannot simply be applied to the rocket ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^cau-a3-3-2|Caution in §A3.3]]). The way out is to apply momentum conservation ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]) to a system of constant mass, rocket *plus* the gas it ejects in a short time $dt$. This gives the rocket's equation of motion and Tsiolkovsky's rocket equation.

## The model

> [!model] Model §A5.5.1: Ideal Rocket
> The rocket moves along a straight line (the $x$-axis); at time $t$ its mass (body plus unburned fuel) is $m$ and its velocity $v$, measured in an inertial frame. Assumptions:
> - burned fuel is ejected backwards at a constant speed $u$ **relative to the rocket**, so its velocity in the inertial frame is $v - u$;
> - no external force acts (deep space), or, in a second version, only the uniform weight $m\vec g$ near Earth's surface (vertical launch);
> - in a time $dt$ the rocket ejects a positive mass $dm_g$ of gas, so its own mass changes by $dm = -dm_g$.
>
> It fails where these fail: drag in the atmosphere, gravity varying with height on long flights, exhaust speed changing with conditions.
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.7*

^mod-a5-5-1

## The rocket equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.5.2: Rocket Equation
> In the ideal rocket without external force,
>
> $$
> m\,\frac{dv}{dt} = -u\,\frac{dm}{dt} = u\,\Bigl|\frac{dm}{dt}\Bigr| ,
> $$
>
> so the rocket is pushed forward by the **thrust** $T = u\,|dm/dt|$. Burning fuel from mass $m_i$ down to $m_f$ changes its velocity by
>
> $$
> \Delta v = u\,\ln\frac{m_i}{m_f} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.7, eq. (9.38); Example 9.20*

^thm-a5-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> System: the rocket at time $t$, including the gas $dm_g$ it will eject during $dt$. Its mass is constant and no external force acts, so its momentum is conserved ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> m v = (m - dm_g)(v + dv) + dm_g\,(v - u) .
> $$
>
> Expanding, $0 = m\,dv - dm_g\,dv - dm_g\,u$. The product $dm_g\,dv$ of two infinitesimals is negligible, leaving $m\,dv = u\,dm_g = -u\,dm$. Dividing by $dt$ gives the equation of motion. Separating, $dv = -u\,dm/m$, and integrating from $(m_i, v_i)$ to $(m_f, v_f)$,
>
> $$
> v_f - v_i = -u\int_{m_i}^{m_f}\frac{dm}{m} = u\ln\frac{m_i}{m_f} .
> $$

^der-a5-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A5.5 Rocket Propulsion#^mod-a5-5-1|Model §A5.5.1]], [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|FTC I (451 Thm. §34.1)]]

![[ph-cm-a5-5-1.svg]]
*The system in the derivation. At time $t$ (top) the rocket of mass $m$ moves at $v$; at $t + dt$ (bottom) the rocket, now of mass $m - dm_g$, moves at $v + dv$, and the ejected gas $dm_g$ (orange) moves at $v - u$ in the inertial frame. The total momentum is the same in both pictures.*

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the rocket equation
> - The thrust is constant if fuel burns at a constant rate, but the mass being accelerated keeps falling, so the acceleration grows during the burn.
> - $\Delta v$ depends on the exhaust speed and on the **mass ratio** $m_i/m_f$, only logarithmically: doubling the mass ratio adds the fixed amount $u\ln 2$ to $\Delta v$. Without external forces it does not matter how quickly the fuel is burned.

^rem-a5-5-1

> [!caution] Caution: Not $\vec F = d(m\vec v)/dt$ for the rocket alone
> Applying $\vec F = d\vec p/dt$ to the rocket alone, with $m$ varying, ignores the momentum carried away by the exhaust and gives a wrong result. The momentum form of the second law holds for a system of fixed mass; here that system is rocket + exhaust. Also keep the signs straight: $dm < 0$ for the rocket, $dm_g > 0$ for the gas, and $u$ is measured relative to the rocket, not to the ground.

^cau-a5-5-1

## Rocket in uniform gravity

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.5.3: Rocket in Uniform Gravity
> For a rocket launched vertically near Earth's surface, with $g$ treated as constant and $y$ up,
>
> $$
> m\,\frac{dv}{dt} = u\,\Bigl|\frac{dm}{dt}\Bigr| - mg ,
> \qquad
> \Delta v = u\ln\frac{m_i}{m_f} - g\,\Delta t ,
> $$
>
> where $\Delta t$ is the burn time. This presupposes that the rocket actually rises, i.e. that the thrust exceeds the weight.
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.7, eq. (9.39)*

^thm-a5-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Now the weight $-mg\,\hat\jmath$ acts on the system during $dt$ and its impulse $-mg\,dt$ equals the change of momentum ([[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]):
>
> $$
> (m - dm_g)(v + dv) + dm_g(v - u) - mv = -mg\,dt .
> $$
>
> Dropping $dm_g\,dv$ as before, $m\,dv - u\,dm_g = -mg\,dt$, and with $dm_g = -dm$, $dv = -u\,dm/m - g\,dt$. Integrating over the burn gives $\Delta v = u\ln(m_i/m_f) - g\Delta t$.

^der-a5-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A5.5 Rocket Propulsion#^mod-a5-5-1|Model §A5.5.1]], [[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]], [[§A5.5 Rocket Propulsion#^thm-a5-5-2|Theorem §A5.5.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Burn fast
> With gravity the burn time matters: every second of burning costs $g\Delta t$ of velocity. That is why a launch is so violent at lift-off: the fuel must be burned as quickly as possible to get the largest $\Delta v$.

^rem-a5-5-2

## Example

> [!example] Example §A5.5.1: Thrust on a Spacecraft
> A spacecraft of initial mass $2.0\times10^4$ kg (including fuel) in gravity-free space fires its thrusters for 30 s, ejecting $2.0\times10^2$ kg/s of gas at $2.5\times10^2$ m/s relative to the craft.
> - *Thrust:* $T = u\,|dm/dt| = (250\ \mathrm{m/s})(200\ \mathrm{kg/s}) = 5.0\times10^4$ N, constant.
> - *Acceleration:* the mass is $m(t) = 2.0\times10^4\ \mathrm{kg} - (200\ \mathrm{kg/s})\,t$, so $a(t) = T/m(t) = (5.0\times10^4\ \mathrm N)/\bigl[2.0\times10^4\ \mathrm{kg} - (200\ \mathrm{kg/s})\,t\bigr]$: $2.5$ m/s² at $t = 0$, $2.9$ m/s² at 15 s, $3.6$ m/s² at 30 s, and $0$ at 35 s, after the thrusters stop.
> - *Velocity gained:* $\Delta v = (250\ \mathrm{m/s})\ln\bigl(2.0\times10^4/1.4\times10^4\bigr) = 89$ m/s. Integrating $a(t)$ over the 30 s gives the same, since $\int_0^{30\,\mathrm s} a\,dt = (T/|dm/dt|)\ln(m_i/m_f)$ and $T/|dm/dt| = u$.
>
> On Earth's surface this craft could not even lift off: its thrust, $5.0\times10^4$ N, is less than its weight, $2.0\times10^5$ N, so the launch pad would supply an extra external force and Theorem §A5.5.3 would not apply.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 9.20*

^ex-a5-5-1
