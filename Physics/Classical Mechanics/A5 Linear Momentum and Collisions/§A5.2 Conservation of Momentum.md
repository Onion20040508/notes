When two bodies interact, the third law makes their forces on each other equal and opposite, and they act for the same time. By the impulse–momentum theorem ([[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]) the momentum one gains the other loses. Summed over a system, internal forces therefore cannot change the total momentum: only external forces can. If they cancel, the total momentum is conserved, however complicated the interaction.

## Momentum of a system

> [!definition] Definition §A5.2.1: Total Momentum; Closed System
> The **total momentum** of a system of $N$ bodies is the vector sum $\vec P = \sum_{j=1}^N \vec p_j$. A system is **closed** (or **isolated**) if its total mass is constant and the net external force on it is zero.
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.3, eq. (9.17)*

^def-a5-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.1: External Forces Change the Total Momentum
> The rate of change of the total momentum of a system equals the net external force on it:
>
> $$
> \frac{d\vec P}{dt} = \vec F_{\text{ext}} .
> $$
>
> Internal forces, however large, drop out. This generalises [[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]], where all parts of the system share one acceleration.
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.6, eqs. (9.20)–(9.26)*

^thm-a5-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The force on body $j$ is the external force $\vec F^{\text{ext}}_j$ plus the forces $\vec F_{kj}$ exerted on it by the other bodies $k$ of the system. By [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|Principle §A3.3.3]], $d\vec p_j/dt = \vec F^{\text{ext}}_j + \sum_{k\neq j}\vec F_{kj}$. Summing over $j$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d\vec P}{dt} = \sum_j \vec F^{\text{ext}}_j + \sum_j\sum_{k\neq j}\vec F_{kj} .
> $$
>
> In the double sum each pair of bodies $\{j, k\}$ contributes two terms, $\vec F_{kj} + \vec F_{jk}$, which add to zero by the third law ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|Principle §A3.5.1]]). The first sum is $\vec F_{\text{ext}}$.

^der-a5-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|Principle §A3.3.3]], [[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|Principle §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]], [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^def-a5-2-1|Def. §A5.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why you cannot lift yourself
> Standing in a basket and pulling up on its handles, your pull is internal to the system (you + basket) and cannot change its momentum. The internal forces on one *part* of a system need not cancel; only their sum over the whole system does.

^rem-a5-2-1

## Conservation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.2.2: Conservation of Momentum
> The total momentum of a closed system is constant:
>
> $$
> \sum_{j=1}^N \vec p_j = \text{constant}; \qquad\text{for two bodies}\quad \Delta\vec p_1 = -\Delta\vec p_2 .
> $$
>
> The statement holds component by component: if one component of the net external force vanishes, that component of $\vec P$ is conserved even if the others are not.
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.3, eqs. (9.10)–(9.17); §9.5, eq. (9.18)*

^thm-a5-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $\vec F_{\text{ext}} = \vec 0$ in [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]]: $d\vec P/dt = \vec 0$. For one component, $dP_x/dt = F_{\text{ext},x}$, which is zero if $F_{\text{ext},x} = 0$. For two bodies, $\vec p_1 + \vec p_2$ constant means their changes are opposite.

^der-a5-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Status of the law
> Here the conservation of momentum is derived from the third law, so it is a theorem. The text also stresses that it holds far beyond Newtonian mechanics, from galaxy clusters to quarks, and for light, whose momentum is defined differently ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|QM Principle §A1.2.3]]): with conservation of energy ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|Principle §A4.6.3]]) it is one of the foundations of physics.

^rem-a5-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Choose the system first
> - Momentum is conserved for a *system*, and only if it is closed. A ball bouncing on the floor is not a closed system (gravity and the floor act on it), so its momentum changes. Ball + Earth is closed: a 0.25 kg superball dropped from 1.50 m that bounces back to the same height changes its momentum by $+2.7$ kg·m/s ([[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^cau-a5-1-1|Caution in §A5.1]]), so Earth's changes by $-2.7$ kg·m/s, and Earth's velocity by $-2.7/(5.97\times10^{24})$ m/s $\approx -4.5\times10^{-25}$ m/s.
> - External forces may act if they cancel: billiard balls feel weight and normal force, with zero sum.
> - During a brief impact, finite external forces such as road friction give only a small impulse ($F\,\Delta t$ with $\Delta t$ tiny), while the internal impact forces are huge. So momentum is conserved from *just before to just after* a collision even when it is not conserved over longer times.
> - The mass inside the boundary must not change; mass may move between bodies of the system. For a rocket, the exhaust must be part of the system ([[§A5.5 Rocket Propulsion#^thm-a5-5-2|Theorem §A5.5.2]]).

^cau-a5-2-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.2.1: Everyone Jumps at Once
> All the people in the world gather in one spot and jump at the same moment. After they all land, is Earth's momentum larger, smaller or unchanged?
>
> Take people + Earth as the system: it is closed (neglecting the Sun, the Moon and the planets), and every force involved, the push-off, gravity in flight, the landing, is internal. In Earth's initial rest frame $\vec P = \vec 0$ throughout. After landing everyone is again at rest relative to Earth, so people and Earth share one velocity $\vec V$ with $(M_E + m)\vec V = \vec 0$: Earth's momentum is **the same as before**. In between, Earth recoiled while the people rose and was pulled back as they fell.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 16–18*

^ex-a5-2-1

> [!example] Example §A5.2.2: Throwing a Rock on Ice
> A 100 kg person standing on frictionless ice throws a 4.0 kg rock at 10 m/s, $60^\circ$ above the horizontal. Is the momentum of the system person + rock conserved, and how fast does the person recoil?
>
> Only *partly*. The external forces, gravity and the normal force from the ice, are vertical; while throwing, the person pushes down hard and the normal force exceeds the weight, so the vertical momentum changes. Horizontally there is no external force, so $P_x$ stays zero:
>
> $$
> 0 = (4.0\ \mathrm{kg})(10\ \mathrm{m/s})\cos 60^\circ + (100\ \mathrm{kg})\,V_x
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_x = -0.20\ \mathrm{m/s} ,
> $$
>
> 0.20 m/s opposite to the rock's horizontal motion.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 17–18*

^ex-a5-2-2
