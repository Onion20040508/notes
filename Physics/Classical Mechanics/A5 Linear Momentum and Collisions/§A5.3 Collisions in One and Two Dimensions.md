In a collision or explosion the bodies exert large forces on each other for a short time, and external forces hardly matter meanwhile, so the total momentum just after equals that just before ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]). Kinetic energy is a different matter: it may be conserved, lost to deformation and heat, or gained from stored energy. Classifying collisions by what happens to it gives the second equation that, with momentum, fixes the outcome.

## Kinds of collision

> [!definition] Definition §A5.3.1: Elastic, Inelastic, Perfectly Inelastic; Explosion
> For a closed system of colliding bodies, with $\vec P_f = \vec P_i$ always, the interaction is
> - **elastic** if the total kinetic energy is unchanged, $K_f = K_i$ (for two bodies: they separate at the relative speed at which they approached);
> - **inelastic** if $K_f < K_i$;
> - **perfectly inelastic** if the bodies stick together and move as one; this loses as much kinetic energy as momentum conservation allows;
> - an **explosion** if one body breaks into several and $K_f > K_i$, the extra energy coming from stored (chemical, elastic) potential energy.
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.4*

^def-a5-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: How much kinetic energy can be lost?
> After a perfectly inelastic collision the composite of mass $M$ must still carry the total momentum $\vec P$, so it keeps $K_f = P^2/2M$ ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]]). All the kinetic energy can disappear only if $\vec P = \vec 0$, as when two equal bodies meet head on with equal speeds. (The text defines "perfectly inelastic" by $K_f = 0$; that is this special case of sticking together.)

^rem-a5-3-1

## Two standard results

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.3.1: Perfectly Inelastic Collision
> If bodies of masses $m_1$, $m_2$ with velocities $\vec v_1$, $\vec v_2$ stick together, the composite moves with
>
> $$
> \vec v_f = \frac{m_1\vec v_1 + m_2\vec v_2}{m_1 + m_2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.3, Example 9.6; §9.5*

^thm-a5-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]], $m_1\vec v_1 + m_2\vec v_2 = (m_1 + m_2)\,\vec v_f$; divide by $m_1 + m_2$. In two dimensions this is one equation per component.

^der-a5-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.3.2: Elastic Collision in One Dimension
> In a one-dimensional elastic collision of $m_1$ (velocity $v_{1i}$) with $m_2$ ($v_{2i}$), the relative velocity reverses, $v_{2f} - v_{1f} = -(v_{2i} - v_{1i})$, and
>
> $$
> v_{1f} = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)\,v_{1i} + 2m_2\,v_{2i}}{m_1 + m_2}, \qquad
> v_{2f} = \frac{(m_2 - m_1)\,v_{2i} + 2m_1\,v_{1i}}{m_1 + m_2} .
> $$
>
> Velocities are components along the line of motion, with signs.
>
> *Source: UP1 §9.4, Example 9.11*

^thm-a5-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Momentum and kinetic energy are both conserved:
>
> $$
> m_1(v_{1i} - v_{1f}) = m_2(v_{2f} - v_{2i}), \qquad m_1(v_{1i}^2 - v_{1f}^2) = m_2(v_{2f}^2 - v_{2i}^2) .
> $$
>
> If the bodies do collide, $v_{1f} \neq v_{1i}$, and dividing the second equation by the first gives $v_{1i} + v_{1f} = v_{2i} + v_{2f}$, the reversal of the relative velocity. Substitute $v_{2f} = v_{1i} + v_{1f} - v_{2i}$ into the momentum equation and solve: $(m_1 + m_2)\,v_{1f} = (m_1 - m_2)\,v_{1i} + 2m_2 v_{2i}$; then $v_{2f}$ follows. (The other root of the quadratic system, $v_{1f} = v_{1i}$, $v_{2f} = v_{2i}$, describes no collision.)

^der-a5-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]], [[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^def-a5-3-1|Def. §A5.3.1]], [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Special cases and two dimensions
> - **Equal masses**, target at rest: $v_{1f} = 0$, $v_{2f} = v_{1i}$. The bodies exchange velocities; total momentum and kinetic energy are both unchanged (a hockey puck or air-track glider hitting an identical one at rest).
> - **Light body on a heavy one at rest** ($m_2 \gg m_1$): $v_{1f} \approx -v_{1i}$, it bounces back with nearly its speed.
> - **Two dimensions**: write momentum conservation for the $x$- and $y$-components separately ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]) and combine the components into speed and direction at the end. Two component equations fix two unknowns; an elastic collision adds the energy equation.

^rem-a5-3-2

> [!caution] Caution: Momentum yes, kinetic energy not necessarily
> Never assume kinetic energy is conserved in a collision unless it is stated to be elastic. When a collision is followed (or preceded) by motion under conservative forces, treat the stages separately: momentum across the collision, energy before and after it (Example §A5.3.3).

^cau-a5-3-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.3.1: What the Third Law Guarantees
> Two objects $A$ and $B$ collide; by the third law the force $A$ exerts on $B$ is equal and opposite to the force $B$ exerts on $A$. Which must then be equal and opposite: the changes of momentum, the works done, or the changes of kinetic energy?
>
> The two forces are opposite at every instant and act for the same time, so their impulses are opposite and $\Delta\vec p_A = -\Delta\vec p_B$. The works are $\int \vec F_{BA}\cdot d\vec r_A$ and $\int \vec F_{AB}\cdot d\vec r_B = -\int \vec F_{BA}\cdot d\vec r_B$, which add to $\int \vec F_{BA}\cdot d(\vec r_A - \vec r_B)$. This is not zero when the separation of the bodies changes while they push on each other, as it does when they compress and spring back. So only the momentum changes are guaranteed to be opposite; the works and the kinetic-energy changes are not.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 16–18*

^ex-a5-3-1

> [!example] Example §A5.3.2: Elastic Collision of Unequal Pucks
> A 12 g puck moving at 2.5 m/s to the left hits a 15 g puck at rest, elastically. Take $+x$ to the right; body 1 is the 15 g puck ($v_{1i} = 0$), body 2 the 12 g puck ($v_{2i} = -2.5$ m/s). By [[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^thm-a5-3-2|Theorem §A5.3.2]]
>
> $$
> v_{1f} = \frac{2(12)(-2.5)}{27}\ \mathrm{m/s} = -2.22\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad
> v_{2f} = \frac{(12 - 15)(-2.5)}{27}\ \mathrm{m/s} = +0.28\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> The struck puck moves off to the left at 2.22 m/s and the lighter incoming puck bounces back to the right at 0.28 m/s. Check: momentum $-30 = 15(-2.22) + 12(0.28)$ g·m/s, and $\tfrac12(12)(2.5)^2 = 37.5 = \tfrac12(15)(2.22)^2 + \tfrac12(12)(0.28)^2$ g·m²/s².
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 9.11*

^ex-a5-3-2

> [!example] Example §A5.3.3: Bullet, Block and Spring
> A bullet of mass $m$ and speed $V$ embeds itself in a block of mass $M$ at rest on a frictionless surface; the block is attached to a spring of constant $k$ whose other end is fixed. What is the maximum compression?
>
> *Collision* (so brief that the spring has not yet compressed; perfectly inelastic): momentum gives $v = mV/(m + M)$ just afterwards. *Compression* (only the spring does work): mechanical energy is conserved, $\tfrac12(m + M)v^2 = \tfrac12 k x_{\max}^2$, so
>
> $$
> x_{\max} = v\sqrt{\frac{m + M}{k}} = \frac{mV}{\sqrt{k\,(m + M)}} .
> $$
>
> Energy is *not* conserved in the first stage: the collision keeps only the fraction $m/(m + M)$ of the bullet's kinetic energy. Writing $\tfrac12 mV^2 = \tfrac12 kx_{\max}^2$ would be wrong.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 18*

^ex-a5-3-3

> [!example] Example §A5.3.4: Collision at an Intersection
> A 1200 kg car going east at 60 km/h (16.67 m/s) collides with a 3000 kg truck going north at 40 km/h (11.11 m/s), and the two lock together. With $x$ east and $y$ north, momentum is conserved just before to just after the impact (road friction gives negligible impulse during it). By components,
>
> $$
> v_{w,x} = \frac{(1200)(16.67)}{4200}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 4.76\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad
> v_{w,y} = \frac{(3000)(11.11)}{4200}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 7.94\ \mathrm{m/s} ,
> $$
>
> so the wreck moves at $\sqrt{4.76^2 + 7.94^2} = 9.26$ m/s (33 km/h), at $\arctan(7.94/4.76) = 59^\circ$ north of east, i.e. $31^\circ$ east of north. The truck has the larger momentum though the car is faster. The kinetic energy drops from 352 kJ to 180 kJ: about half is lost to deformation and heat.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 9.14*

^ex-a5-3-4
