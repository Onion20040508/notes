The rigid body of [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^mod-a6-1-1|Model §A6.1.1]] does not deform, and [[§A7.1 Conditions for Equilibrium|§A7.1]] found the forces on such bodies at rest. Real bodies stretch, shorten, squeeze and shear under those forces, slightly for a steel rod and a lot for a rubber band. This section measures the load by the stress, the deformation by the strain, and for small loads relates the two by a material constant, the elastic modulus; it ends with what happens when the load is no longer small.

## Stress and strain

> [!definition] Definition §A7.2.1: Stress and Strain
> **Stress** is the deforming force per unit area of the surface it acts on; its SI unit is the pascal, $1\ \mathrm{Pa} = 1\ \mathrm{N/m^2}$. **Strain** is the resulting fractional deformation, a dimensionless number. There are three basic kinds:
> - **tensile** (or **compressive**) stress stretches (or shortens) a body along one direction, with forces perpendicular to a cross-section; the strain is a fractional change of length;
> - **bulk** (volume) stress squeezes a body equally from all sides; the strain is a fractional change of volume;
> - **shear** stress acts tangentially to opposite faces; the strain is a sideways shift of layers.
>
> *Source: UP1 §12.3*

^def-a7-2-1

> [!law] Law §A7.2.1: Linear Stress–Strain Relation
> For small enough stress, strain is proportional to stress:
>
> $$
> \text{stress} = (\text{elastic modulus})\times\text{strain} .
> $$
>
> The **elastic modulus**, in pascals, is a property of the material and of the kind of stress (Young's, bulk or shear modulus below), measured in the laboratory and tabulated. A large modulus means a stiff material, a small one an easily deformed material: rubber's Young's modulus is far smaller than steel's, which is why a rubber band stretches visibly under a load that would not measurably lengthen a steel band of the same size.
>
> *Valid when:* the stress is below the material's proportionality (linearity) limit ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-5|Def. §A7.2.5]]); the moduli also depend on conditions such as temperature.
> *Source: UP1 §12.3, eq. (12.33)*

^law-a7-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A7.2.2: Tensile Stress and Strain; Young's Modulus
> A rod of length $L_0$ and cross-section $A$, pulled (or pushed) by forces $F_\perp$ along its length, changes length by $\Delta L = L - L_0$. Then
>
> $$
> \text{tensile stress} = \frac{F_\perp}{A}, \qquad \text{tensile strain} = \frac{\Delta L}{L_0}, \qquad Y = \frac{F_\perp/A}{\Delta L/L_0} = \frac{F_\perp}{A}\,\frac{L_0}{\Delta L},
> $$
>
> and $Y$ is **Young's modulus**. Compressive stress and strain are defined by the same formulas with absolute values.
>
> *Source: UP1 §12.3, eqs. (12.34)–(12.36)*

^def-a7-2-2

> [!definition] Definition §A7.2.3: Bulk Stress and Strain; Bulk Modulus; Compressibility
> A body of volume $V_0$ squeezed from all sides by an increase $\Delta p$ of the pressure $p = F_\perp/A$ (force per area normal to its surface) changes volume by $\Delta V$. The bulk strain is $\Delta V/V_0$, and
>
> $$
> B = -\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta V/V_0} = -\Delta p\,\frac{V_0}{\Delta V}, \qquad k = \frac1B ,
> $$
>
> are the **bulk modulus** and the **compressibility**. The minus sign makes $B > 0$, since a pressure increase decreases the volume.
>
> *Source: UP1 §12.3, eqs. (12.37)–(12.40)*

^def-a7-2-3

> [!definition] Definition §A7.2.4: Shear Stress and Strain; Shear Modulus
> Equal and opposite forces $F_\parallel$ applied tangentially to two opposite faces of area $A$, a distance $L_0$ apart, shift one face relative to the other by $\Delta x$. Then
>
> $$
> \text{shear stress} = \frac{F_\parallel}{A}, \qquad \text{shear strain} = \frac{\Delta x}{L_0}, \qquad S = \frac{F_\parallel/A}{\Delta x/L_0} = \frac{F_\parallel}{A}\,\frac{L_0}{\Delta x},
> $$
>
> and $S$ is the **shear modulus**. Shear moduli exist only for solids: a fluid at rest does not resist shear.
>
> *Source: UP1 §12.3, eqs. (12.41)–(12.43)*

^def-a7-2-4

> [!remark] Remark: Some values
> Approximate moduli in units of $10^{10}\ \mathrm{Pa}$:
>
> | Material | Young's $Y$ | Bulk $B$ | Shear $S$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | steel | 20 | 16 | 7.5 |
> | aluminium | 7.0 | 7.5 | 2.5 |
> | copper | 11 | 14 | 4.4 |
> | granite | 4.5 | 4.5 | 2.0 |
> | concrete | 2.0 | | |
> | water | | 0.22 | |
> | mercury | | 2.5 | |
>
> Liquids have bulk moduli only. Water's compressibility is $4.6\times10^{-5}$ per atmosphere: $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ ($1.013\times10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$) changes its volume by less than one part in $10^4$.

^rem-a7-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: A rod is a spring
> Solving Young's relation for the force, $F_\perp = (YA/L_0)\,\Delta L$: within the linear range a rod behaves like a spring obeying Hooke's law ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^law-a3-6-3|Law §A3.6.3]]) with constant $k = YA/L_0$. The spring constant is not a property of the material alone: a thicker rod is stiffer, a longer one softer.

^rem-a7-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: Signs, and stress that varies along a body
> - Compressive stress and strain are quoted as positive numbers; in the bulk modulus the minus sign absorbs $\Delta V < 0$.
> - The stress in a member that carries its own weight is not uniform. In a pillar the force on a cross-section grows downward (largest stress at the base); in a hanging rod it grows upward (largest at the top).
> - A bent beam is stretched on its convex side and compressed on its concave side: a shelf sagging under books has its top surface in compression and its bottom in tension. (The caption of UP1 Fig. 12.20, p. 619, states the reverse, against the text on p. 618.) I-beams put most of the material where these stresses are largest.

^cau-a7-2-1

## Beyond the linear range

> [!definition] Definition §A7.2.5: Elastic and Plastic Behaviour
> A body is **elastic** if it returns to its original size and shape when the load is removed. As the load increases from zero:
> - up to the **proportionality (linearity) limit**, stress is proportional to strain ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^law-a7-2-1|Law §A7.2.1]]);
> - up to the **elastic limit**, the behaviour stays elastic but may be nonlinear;
> - beyond the elastic limit the material is **plastic**: it deforms permanently and does not come back when unloaded;
> - at the **breaking (ultimate) stress** it fractures.
>
> These limits are ranges rather than sharp points.
>
> *Source: UP1 §12.4*

^def-a7-2-5

![[ph-cm-a7-2-1.svg]]

Unloading from a point $P$ in the plastic range follows the red line, parallel to the linear part, and leaves a permanent strain.*

> [!remark] Remark: Stiffness and strength are different properties
> Elastic modulus measures how hard a material is to deform; breaking stress measures how much it can take. Two metals with similar moduli can have very different breaking stresses: about $2.2\times10^8\ \mathrm{Pa}$ for aluminium and up to $2.0\times10^9\ \mathrm{Pa}$ for some steels. A rod of $1\ \mathrm{in^2}$ cross-section ($6.45\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2}$) therefore breaks under about $1.4\times10^5\ \mathrm N$ ($3.2\times10^4\ \mathrm{lb}$) in aluminium and roughly nine times that in strong steel. Rubber combines a low modulus with a high elastic limit, which is why it stretches far and still comes back.

^rem-a7-2-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A7.2.1: Stretching a Steel Rod
> A steel rod of length $L_0 = 2.0\ \mathrm m$ and cross-section $A = 0.30\ \mathrm{cm^2} = 3.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ holds a $550\ \mathrm{kg}$ platform (rod's own weight neglected). With $Y = 2.0\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{Pa}$,
>
> $$
> \frac{F_\perp}{A} = \frac{(550\ \mathrm{kg})(9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2})}{3.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2}} = 1.8\times10^8\ \mathrm{Pa}, \qquad \Delta L = \frac{F_\perp}{A}\,\frac{L_0}{Y} = \frac{(1.8\times10^8)(2.0\ \mathrm m)}{2.0\times10^{11}} = 1.8\ \mathrm{mm} .
> $$
>
> A strain of $9\times10^{-4}$ is invisible, yet the stress is already about a tenth of the highest breaking stress quoted for steel ($2.0\times10^9\ \mathrm{Pa}$, remark above), and the stretch is proportional to the load only as long as the steel stays in its linear range.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 12.8*

^ex-a7-2-1

> [!example] Example §A7.2.2: Compressing Oil in a Hydraulic Press
> A press holds $V_0 = 250\ \mathrm L$ of oil of compressibility $k = 2.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{atm^{-1}}$, and the pressure rises by $\Delta p = 2300\ \mathrm{psi} = 2300/14.7\ \mathrm{atm} = 156\ \mathrm{atm}$. By [[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-3|Def. §A7.2.3]], $\Delta V/V_0 = -k\,\Delta p$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\Delta V}{V_0} = -(2.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{atm^{-1}})(156\ \mathrm{atm}) = -3.1\times10^{-3}, \qquad \Delta V = -0.78\ \mathrm L .
> $$
>
> The oil loses well under 1 % of its volume at over 150 atmospheres, which is why liquids transmit pressure in hydraulic machines almost without loss of stroke.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 12.9*

^ex-a7-2-2

> [!example] Example §A7.2.3: Shearing a Bookcase
> A bookcase $L_0 = 180\ \mathrm{cm}$ tall, with a top of $A = (30.0\ \mathrm{cm})(90.0\ \mathrm{cm}) = 0.270\ \mathrm{m^2}$, is pushed sideways at the top with $F_\parallel = 50.0\ \mathrm N$; the top shifts $\Delta x = 15.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ relative to the fixed bottom and springs back when released. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{F_\parallel}{A} = 185\ \mathrm{Pa}, \qquad \frac{\Delta x}{L_0} = 0.0833, \qquad S = \frac{185\ \mathrm{Pa}}{0.0833} = 2.2\times10^3\ \mathrm{Pa} .
> $$
>
> This "shear modulus" describes the bookcase as a structure, with its loose joints, not its wood: it is millions of times smaller than the moduli in the table. The same shearing failure brings down earth dams and slopes.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 12.10*

^ex-a7-2-3
