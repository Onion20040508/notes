Bridges, ladders, shelves and forearms are meant not to move. For a point particle, or a system treated as one, equilibrium meant $\sum\vec F = \vec 0$ ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-3|Def. §A3.2.3]]); for an extended rigid body that is not enough, since balanced forces can still turn it. Combining the centre-of-mass motion ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]) with the rotational law of Chapter A6 gives two conditions, one for forces and one for torques, and with them the unknown forces on a body at rest.

## Equilibrium and its conditions

> [!definition] Definition §A7.1.1: Equilibrium; Static Equilibrium
> A rigid body is in **equilibrium** when the linear acceleration of its centre of mass and its angular acceleration are both zero relative to an inertial frame: it is at rest or moves with constant velocity and constant angular velocity. It is in **static equilibrium** when it is at rest in the chosen inertial frame. This extends [[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-3|Def. §A3.2.3]] (equilibrium of a particle, $\vec F_{\text{net}} = \vec 0$) by the condition on the rotation.
>
> *Source: UP1 §12.1*

^def-a7-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.1.1: Conditions for Equilibrium
> A rigid body in equilibrium satisfies
>
> $$
> \sum_k\vec F_k = \vec 0 \quad\text{(first condition)}, \qquad \sum_k\vec\tau_k = \vec 0 \quad\text{(second condition)},
> $$
>
> where the sums run over the external forces and their torques about one point. For forces in the $xy$-plane these are three scalar equations:
>
> $$
> \sum_k F_{kx} = 0, \qquad \sum_k F_{ky} = 0, \qquad \sum_k\tau_k = 0 \ \ (\text{about an axis along } z).
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §12.1, eqs. (12.2)–(12.9)*

^thm-a7-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The centre of mass moves as a particle carrying the whole mass under the net external force, $\sum\vec F_k = M\vec a_{\text{cm}}$ ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]); in equilibrium $\vec a_{\text{cm}} = \vec 0$. The net external torque equals the rate of change of the angular momentum, $\sum\vec\tau_k = d\vec L/dt$ ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]]); for rotation about an axis, $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ ([[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]]), and in equilibrium $\alpha = 0$. For a body at rest $\vec L = \vec 0$ at all times, so the net torque vanishes about every point. (For a body turning steadily, $\alpha = 0$ gives only the torque component along the axis; the full vector condition then needs $\vec L$ constant, as for a body spinning about a symmetry axis.) For planar forces, the force sums have only $x$- and $y$-components and the torques only a $z$-component.

^der-a7-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A7.1 Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-a7-1-1|Def. §A7.1.1]], [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]], [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.1.2: Free Choice of Pivot
> If the net external force on a body is zero and the net torque about one point is zero, then the net torque about every other point is zero too. So in an equilibrium problem the torques may be taken about any point, the **pivot**, whether or not the body could actually turn about it.
>
> *Source: UP1 §12.1*

^thm-a7-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $\vec R$ be the position of the new point $O'$ relative to the old one $O$. A force $\vec F_k$ applied at $\vec r_k$ (relative to $O$) is at $\vec r_k - \vec R$ relative to $O'$, so
>
> $$
> \sum_k(\vec r_k - \vec R)\times\vec F_k = \sum_k\vec r_k\times\vec F_k - \vec R\times\sum_k\vec F_k = \vec 0 - \vec R\times\vec 0 = \vec 0 .
> $$

^der-a7-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A7.1 Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-a7-1-1|Theorem §A7.1.1]], [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^def-a6-3-1|Def. §A6.3.1]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]] (distributivity of the cross product)

## Where the weight acts

> [!definition] Definition §A7.1.2: Centre of Gravity
> The **centre of gravity** of a body is the point at which its whole weight can be taken to act for the purpose of computing gravitational torques. The torque of the weight about a point is the **gravitational torque**.
>
> *Source: UP1 §12.1*

^def-a7-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.1.3: In a Uniform Field the Weight Acts at the Centre of Mass
> In a uniform gravitational field $\vec g$, the total gravitational torque on a body about any point equals the torque of its total weight $M\vec g$ applied at its centre of mass:
>
> $$
> \sum_i\vec r_i\times m_i\vec g = \vec r_{\text{cm}}\times M\vec g .
> $$
>
> So the centre of gravity coincides with the centre of mass.
>
> *Source: UP1 §12.1*

^thm-a7-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Since $\vec g$ is the same for every mass element it factors out of the sum:
>
> $$
> \sum_i\vec r_i\times m_i\vec g = \Bigl(\sum_i m_i\vec r_i\Bigr)\times\vec g = M\vec r_{\text{cm}}\times\vec g = \vec r_{\text{cm}}\times M\vec g ,
> $$
>
> using $\sum_i m_i\vec r_i = M\vec r_{\text{cm}}$, the definition of the centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]]).

^der-a7-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A7.1 Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-a7-1-2|Def. §A7.1.2]], [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]], [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^def-a6-3-1|Def. §A6.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Uniform field, tipping and stability
> Near Earth's surface $\vec g$ is uniform even over a building or a ship, so the centre of gravity is the centre of mass. Only for bodies so large that $\vec g$ varies across them do the two points differ (tidal forces, [[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^def-a8-4-1|Def. §A8.4.1]]). A body standing on a base tips back when tilted as long as its centre of gravity stays above the base of support, because the gravitational torque about the tipping edge then turns it back; once the centre of gravity passes beyond the edge, the same torque turns it over. This is why a truck with a high centre of mass tips more easily than a low car.

^rem-a7-1-1

## Solving statics problems

> [!remark] Remark: Strategy
> 1. Choose the body (sometimes several) and list every external force on it, with its point of application.
> 2. Draw the free-body diagram of the extended body: forces at their points of application, an $xy$-frame, the pivot and the lever arms. Give unknown forces a working direction; a negative result means the actual direction is the opposite one.
> 3. Write $\sum F_x = 0$, $\sum F_y = 0$ and $\sum\tau = 0$ about the pivot. Put the pivot where unknown forces act: their torques vanish and the torque equation has fewer unknowns.
> 4. Count: three independent equations in a planar problem determine at most three unknowns. If there are more unknowns, the problem needs extra information.
> 5. Check by taking torques about a second point.

^rem-a7-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Hinges, pivots and indeterminate problems
> - A hinge or joint can push in any direction in the plane: give it two unknown components, not a force along the attached beam. (The force at a hinge lies along the beam only for a weightless member loaded at its ends.)
> - A door on two hinges has four unknown hinge components but only three equations. UP1 closes the problem by assuming each hinge carries half the weight; without such an assumption only the horizontal components and the sum of the vertical ones are fixed.
> - The pivot is a bookkeeping choice: the body need not be able to turn about it.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A7.1.1: How Far over the Edge?
> A uniform beam of mass $M$ and length $L$ rests, unattached, on a ledge and sticks out a distance $x$ beyond the edge. A student of mass $m$ wants to walk to its far end. As the student moves out, the support force from the ledge shifts toward the edge; when the beam is about to tip, it acts at the edge alone. Take torques about the edge: the beam's weight acts at its centre, a distance $L/2 - x$ back from the edge; the student's at $x$ in front. Just at tipping,
>
> $$
> mg\,x = Mg\Bigl(\frac L2 - x\Bigr) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_{\max} = \frac{M}{M + m}\,\frac L2 .
> $$
>
> With no student ($m = 0$) the beam can stick out up to half its length; a heavy student ($m \gg M$) can trust only a short overhang.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 26*

^ex-a7-1-1

> [!example] Example §A7.1.2: Ladder against a Smooth Wall
> A uniform ladder of length $L = 5.0\ \mathrm m$ and weight $w = 400\ \mathrm N$ leans against a frictionless wall at $\beta = 53^\circ$ to a rough floor. Forces: the floor's normal force $N$ (up) and static friction $f$ (toward the wall) at the foot, the weight at the midpoint, and the wall's normal force $F$ (horizontal, away from the wall) at the top. Take the pivot at the foot, so that $N$ and $f$ drop out of the torque equation. The lever arms are $\tfrac L2\cos\beta$ for $w$ and $L\sin\beta$ for $F$:
>
> $$
> F\,L\sin\beta - w\,\frac L2\cos\beta = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F = \frac w2\cot\beta = 151\ \mathrm N .
> $$
>
> Then $\sum F_y = 0$ gives $N = w = 400\ \mathrm N$, and $\sum F_x = 0$ gives $f = F = 151\ \mathrm N$. The ladder does not slip as long as $\mu_s \geq f/N = \tfrac12\cot\beta = 0.377$. $L$ cancels: only the angle matters, and a flatter ladder needs more friction.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 12.5*

> [!example] Example §A7.1.3: Holding a Weight in the Hand
> A forearm (weight neglected) holds a weight $w = 222.4\ \mathrm N$ ($50.0\ \mathrm{lb}$) in the hand, $13.0\ \mathrm{in}$ from the elbow; the biceps pulls vertically upward on the forearm $1.5\ \mathrm{in}$ from the elbow, and the forearm is at $60^\circ$ to the upper arm. All three forces (biceps tension $T$, weight, elbow force $F$) are vertical. Torques about the elbow, where $F$ acts:
>
> $$
> T\,(1.5\ \mathrm{in})\sin60^\circ - w\,(13.0\ \mathrm{in})\sin60^\circ = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = \frac{13.0}{1.5}\,w = 1.93\times10^3\ \mathrm N\ \text{(up)} .
> $$
>
> The forces give $F = T - w = 1.71\times10^3\ \mathrm N$, pushing **down** on the forearm at the elbow. The angle and the length unit cancel. The muscle's short lever arm forces it to pull almost nine times the load, and the elbow joint carries nearly eight times the load.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 12.4*
