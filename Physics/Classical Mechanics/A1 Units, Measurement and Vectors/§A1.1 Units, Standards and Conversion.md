Physics describes nature with a small number of quantitative laws, and a law can only be tested if the quantities in it can be measured and compared. This section fixes how quantities are expressed: as a number times a unit, with units built from a few agreed standards, and converted from one system to another by multiplying by 1.

## Scale, models and laws

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.1: Order of Magnitude
> The **order of magnitude** of a positive number is the power of 10 closest to it on a logarithmic scale: round $\log_{10}$ of the number to the nearest integer $n$; the order of magnitude is $10^n$.
>
> Equivalently, write the number as $a \times 10^n$ with $1 \le a < 10$. If $a < \sqrt{10} \approx 3$, the order of magnitude is $10^n$; otherwise it is $10^{n+1}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.1*

^def-a1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why orders of magnitude are useful
> Physics spans enormous ranges of length, mass and time, from subatomic particles to the whole universe. Rounding to powers of 10 turns comparisons into mental arithmetic. For example, $800 = 8 \times 10^2$ has order $10^3$, while $250 = 2.5 \times 10^2$ has order $10^2$. A hydrogen atom is about $10^{-10}\ \mathrm m$ across and the Sun about $10^{9}\ \mathrm m$, so about $10^{9}/10^{-10} = 10^{19}$ atoms span the Sun. The precise values ($1.06 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm m$ and $1.39 \times 10^9\ \mathrm m$) give $1.31 \times 10^{19}$: the rough answer is nearly as informative.

^rem-a1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Models, theories and laws, and how these notes label statements
> - A **model** represents something that is too difficult or impossible to display directly, such as the Bohr picture of an electron orbiting a nucleus. It is accurate only for some aspects of the system.
> - A **theory** is a testable explanation of patterns in nature, supported by evidence and verified repeatedly by independent groups. Within its domain it should describe *every* aspect of the systems it covers. It is not a guess.
> - A **law** is a concise statement, often a single equation, of a general pattern, such as $\vec F_{\text{net}} = m\vec a$. "Principle" is often used for less general statements, but the usage is loose.
>
> No law is known with certainty, because not every experiment can be done. One good, verifiable counterexample forces a law to be modified or abandoned.
>
> These notes sort statements by their logical status instead. A **principle** is a fundamental law that is assumed (Newton's laws, gravitation). A **law** is an empirical rule valid in a stated range. A **theorem** is derived within the theory. A **model** is an idealisation with stated assumptions.

^rem-a1-1-2

## Units and standards

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.2: Physical Quantity; Unit
> A **physical quantity** is defined either by how it is measured (length with a meter stick, time with a clock) or by how it is calculated from other measurements (average speed as distance divided by time). A **unit** is a standardized value in terms of which a measurement is expressed: a measured value is a number times a unit, as in $100\ \mathrm m$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.2*

^def-a1-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.3: Base and Derived Quantities; SI Units
> In a system of units, a few **base quantities** have units defined by a measurement procedure (the **base units**). The base quantities must be independent of one another, every other quantity must be expressible through them, and they should be measurable very precisely. All other quantities are **derived quantities**, with **derived units** formed as products and quotients of base units.
>
> The **SI** (metric system) uses seven base quantities:
>
> | Base quantity | SI base unit |
> | --- | --- |
> | length | meter (m) |
> | mass | kilogram (kg) |
> | time | second (s) |
> | electric current | ampere (A) |
> | thermodynamic temperature | kelvin (K) |
> | amount of substance | mole (mol) |
> | luminous intensity | candela (cd) |
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.2, Table 1.1*

^def-a1-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Derived units in practice
> Area is measured in $\mathrm m^2$, volume in $\mathrm m^3$, speed in $\mathrm{m/s}$, density in $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$, electric charge in $\mathrm{A\cdot s}$. An angle in radians is a ratio of two lengths (arc length over radius). Mechanics needs only the meter, kilogram and second. A few non-SI units stay in common use, always tied to SI by a conversion: degrees Celsius, the liter ($1\ \mathrm L = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm m^3$), the electron-volt.

^rem-a1-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: The three mechanical standards
> - **Second.** First defined as $1/86\,400$ of a mean solar day. Since 1967 it is the duration of $9\,192\,631\,770$ vibrations of cesium atoms, because Earth's rotation is gradually slowing.
> - **Meter.** First defined as $10^{-7}$ of the distance from the equator to the North Pole, then by a platinum–iridium bar (1889) and by a krypton wavelength (1960). Since 1983 it is the distance light travels in vacuum in $1/299\,792\,458$ s, which makes the speed of light exactly $299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
> - **Kilogram.** In the text it is the mass of a platinum–iridium cylinder kept near Paris, with copies at national laboratories; all masses trace back to it by comparison. The text anticipates a redefinition in terms of more fundamental processes (watt balance, silicon sphere). That redefinition took effect in 2019, after the text was written.
>
> Standards are chosen to be reproducible and to rest on phenomena that do not change.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.2; the 2019 date of the redefinition is an outside fact, not from the text.*

^rem-a1-1-4

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.4: Metric Prefixes
> A **metric prefix** multiplies a unit by a power of 10:
>
> | Prefix | Symbol | Factor | Prefix | Symbol | Factor |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | yotta | Y | $10^{24}$ | deci | d | $10^{-1}$ |
> | zetta | Z | $10^{21}$ | centi | c | $10^{-2}$ |
> | exa | E | $10^{18}$ | milli | m | $10^{-3}$ |
> | peta | P | $10^{15}$ | micro | µ | $10^{-6}$ |
> | tera | T | $10^{12}$ | nano | n | $10^{-9}$ |
> | giga | G | $10^{9}$ | pico | p | $10^{-12}$ |
> | mega | M | $10^{6}$ | femto | f | $10^{-15}$ |
> | kilo | k | $10^{3}$ | atto | a | $10^{-18}$ |
> | hecto | h | $10^{2}$ | zepto | z | $10^{-21}$ |
> | deka | da | $10^{1}$ | yocto | y | $10^{-24}$ |
>
> So the same unit covers any range: nanometers in optics, meters in construction, kilometers in air travel.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.2, Table 1.2*

^def-a1-1-4

> [!caution] Caution: One prefix at a time, and mass prefixes go on the gram
> Prefixes are never stacked: $10^{15}\ \mathrm m$ is $1\ \mathrm{Pm}$, not "1 megagigameter". Since the kilogram already carries a prefix, prefixes for mass attach to the gram: $10^3\ \mathrm{kg} = 10^6\ \mathrm g = 1\ \mathrm{Mg}$ (also called a metric ton, t).

^cau-a1-1-1

## Unit conversion

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.5: Conversion Factor
> A **conversion factor** is a ratio of two equal quantities expressed in different units, such as $\dfrac{1\ \mathrm{km}}{1000\ \mathrm m}$, $\dfrac{1609\ \mathrm m}{1\ \mathrm{mi}}$ or $\dfrac{60\ \mathrm s}{1\ \mathrm{min}}$. It equals 1, so multiplying by it changes the units of a quantity but not the quantity. To convert, multiply by conversion factors arranged so that the unwanted units cancel and the wanted ones remain: $80\ \mathrm m \times \dfrac{1\ \mathrm{km}}{1000\ \mathrm m} = 0.080\ \mathrm{km}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.3*

^def-a1-1-5

> [!caution] Caution: Squared and cubed units; units that do not match
> - Units obey the rules of algebra. A unit that appears squared or cubed needs its conversion factor squared or cubed: $1\ \mathrm{cm^3} = (10^{-2}\ \mathrm m)^3 = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm m^3$, not $10^{-2}\ \mathrm m^3$.
> - If a factor is written upside down, the units do not cancel. Always check that the units left at the end are the ones asked for.
> - Skipping a conversion can be costly. NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter (1999) was lost because one program reported thruster impulses in pound-seconds while the software using them expected newton-seconds.

^cau-a1-1-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.1.1: Choosing a Prefix
> Write $m = 1.93 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{kg}$ with a prefix so that the number lies between 1 and 1000. Prefixes go on the gram, so first remove the "kilo":
>
> $$
> 1.93 \times 10^{13}\ \mathrm{kg} = 1.93 \times 10^{13} \times 10^{3}\ \mathrm g = 1.93 \times 10^{16}\ \mathrm g .
> $$
>
> Now $10^{16} = 10^{1} \times 10^{15}$, so $m = 19.3\ \mathrm{Pg}$. (With exa, $m = 1.93 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{Eg}$, which is below 1.) To check, count all powers of 10, including those hidden in prefixes: $13 + 3 = 16$ at the start and $1 + 15 = 16$ at the end.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 1.1*

^ex-a1-1-1

> [!example] Example §A1.1.2: Density of Iron in SI Units
> Convert $\rho = 7.86\ \mathrm{g/cm^3}$ to $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$. Use $1\ \mathrm{kg} = 10^3\ \mathrm g$ once and $1\ \mathrm{cm} = 10^{-2}\ \mathrm m$ three times:
>
> $$
> 7.86\ \frac{\mathrm g}{\mathrm{cm^3}} \times \frac{1\ \mathrm{kg}}{10^3\ \mathrm g} \times \left(\frac{1\ \mathrm{cm}}{10^{-2}\ \mathrm m}\right)^{3} = \frac{7.86}{(10^3)(10^{-6})}\ \frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{m^3}} = 7.86 \times 10^{3}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}.
> $$
>
> A $\mathrm{g/cm^3}$ is a thousand times larger than a $\mathrm{kg/m^3}$: the factor $10^{-3}$ from the mass is outweighed by the factor $10^{6}$ from the volume.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 1.3*

^ex-a1-1-2
