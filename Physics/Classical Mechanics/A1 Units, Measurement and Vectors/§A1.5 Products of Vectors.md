Vectors can be added and scaled ([[§A1.4 Vector Algebra|§A1.4]]), but not divided. They can, however, be multiplied in two ways. The scalar product turns two vectors into a number and measures how much they point the same way; it defines work and energy. The vector product turns two vectors into a third, perpendicular to both; it defines torque, angular momentum and the magnetic force.

## The scalar product

> [!definition] Definition §A1.5.1: Scalar (Dot) Product
> The **scalar product** of $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ is the number
>
> $$
> \vec A \cdot \vec B = AB\cos\varphi ,
> $$
>
> where $\varphi$ is the angle between the two vectors when drawn from a common tail ($0 \le \varphi \le 180^\circ$). It does not matter from which vector the angle is measured, since $\cos\varphi = \cos(-\varphi)$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.4, eq. (2.27)*

^def-a1-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.5.1: Properties of the Scalar Product
> - **Sign.** For nonzero vectors, $\vec A \cdot \vec B > 0$ for $0 \le \varphi < 90^\circ$, and $< 0$ for $90^\circ < \varphi \le 180^\circ$. Parallel vectors give $AB$ and antiparallel ones give $-AB$.
> - **Orthogonality.** For nonzero vectors, $\vec A \cdot \vec B = 0$ exactly when $\vec A \perp \vec B$.
> - **Square.** $\vec A \cdot \vec A = A^2$.
> - **Projection.** $\vec A \cdot \vec B = A\,(B\cos\varphi) = B\,(A\cos\varphi)$: the magnitude of one vector times the projection of the other onto it. In particular $\vec A \cdot \hat\imath = A_x$, $\vec A \cdot \hat\jmath = A_y$, $\vec A \cdot \hat k = A_z$.
> - **Algebra.** $\vec A \cdot \vec B = \vec B \cdot \vec A$ and $\vec A \cdot (\vec B + \vec C) = \vec A \cdot \vec B + \vec A \cdot \vec C$.
> - **Unit vectors.** $\hat\imath \cdot \hat\imath = \hat\jmath \cdot \hat\jmath = \hat k \cdot \hat k = 1$ and $\hat\imath \cdot \hat\jmath = \hat\jmath \cdot \hat k = \hat k \cdot \hat\imath = 0$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.4, eqs. (2.28)–(2.32)*

^thm-a1-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Sign, orthogonality, square and unit vectors follow from $\cos\varphi$: it is positive below $90^\circ$, zero at $90^\circ$, negative above, $\cos 0 = 1$ and $\cos 180^\circ = -1$; the unit vectors have length 1 and are mutually perpendicular. The projection form regroups $AB\cos\varphi$. For $\vec B = \hat\imath$ it gives $A\cos\theta_A = A_x$ ([[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^thm-a1-3-2|Theorem §A1.3.2]]); for $\hat\jmath$ the angle is $90^\circ - \theta_A$ and gives $A\sin\theta_A = A_y$. Symmetry holds because the definition does not distinguish the two vectors. For distributivity, take the $x$ axis along $\vec A$. Then $\vec A \cdot \vec V = A\,V_x$ for any $\vec V$, and $x$-components add ([[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]): $(\vec B + \vec C)_x = B_x + C_x$.

^der-a1-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^def-a1-5-1|Def. §A1.5.1]], [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^thm-a1-3-2|Theorem §A1.3.2]], [[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.5.2: Scalar Product in Components
> $$
> \vec A \cdot \vec B = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z, \qquad \cos\varphi = \frac{\vec A \cdot \vec B}{AB} = \frac{A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z}{AB} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.4, eqs. (2.33)–(2.34)*

^thm-a1-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Expand $(A_x\hat\imath + A_y\hat\jmath + A_z\hat k) \cdot (B_x\hat\imath + B_y\hat\jmath + B_z\hat k)$ by distributivity into nine terms $A_iB_j\,(\hat e_i \cdot \hat e_j)$. The six with different unit vectors vanish and the three with equal ones carry a factor 1. Dividing the definition $\vec A \cdot \vec B = AB\cos\varphi$ by $AB$ gives the angle.

^der-a1-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^def-a1-5-1|Def. §A1.5.1]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-1|Theorem §A1.5.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the scalar product matters
> The angle between two vectors given in components can be found without knowing what the axes mean: rotating or relabelling the axes leaves $\vec A \cdot \vec B$ unchanged. In mechanics the scalar product defines the **work** done by a force over a displacement ([[§A4.1 Work#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]), which for a constant force is $W = \vec F \cdot \vec D$ ([[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]]). Only the component of the force along the displacement does work, and a force perpendicular to the motion does none.

^rem-a1-5-1

> [!example] Example §A1.5.1: Angle between Two Forces
> Two dogs pull on a stick with $\vec F_1 = (10.0\,\hat\imath - 20.4\,\hat\jmath + 2.0\,\hat k)\ \mathrm N$ and $\vec F_2 = (-15.0\,\hat\imath - 6.2\,\hat k)\ \mathrm N$. Then $F_1 = \sqrt{10.0^2 + 20.4^2 + 2.0^2}\ \mathrm N = 22.8\ \mathrm N$ and $F_2 = \sqrt{15.0^2 + 6.2^2}\ \mathrm N = 16.2\ \mathrm N$. By [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]],
>
> $$
> \vec F_1 \cdot \vec F_2 = (10.0)(-15.0) + (-20.4)(0) + (2.0)(-6.2)\ \mathrm{N^2} = -162.4\ \mathrm{N^2},
> $$
>
> $$
> \cos\varphi = \frac{-162.4}{(22.8)(16.2)} = -0.439 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \varphi = 116.0^\circ .
> $$
>
> The negative product already says the angle is obtuse: the two dogs pull partly against each other.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 2.16*

^ex-a1-5-1

## The vector product

> [!definition] Definition §A1.5.2: Vector (Cross) Product
> The **vector product** $\vec A \times \vec B$ is the vector with
> - **magnitude** $\lvert\vec A \times \vec B\rvert = AB\sin\varphi$, where $0 \le \varphi \le 180^\circ$ is the angle from $\vec A$ to $\vec B$;
> - **direction** perpendicular to the plane containing $\vec A$ and $\vec B$, with sense given by the **corkscrew right-hand rule**: turn a corkscrew (or the curled fingers of the right hand) from the first vector toward the second through the angle $\varphi$; the direction in which the screw advances (the thumb points) is the direction of $\vec A \times \vec B$.
>
> Geometrically, $AB\sin\varphi$ is the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ (base $A$, height $B\sin\varphi$).
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.4, eq. (2.35)*

^def-a1-5-2

![[ph-cm-a1-5-1.svg]]
*Left: the scalar product as a projection. The red segment is $B\cos\varphi$, the shadow of $\vec B$ on the line of $\vec A$, and $\vec A \cdot \vec B = A\,(B\cos\varphi)$. Right: the vector product. $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ (blue) span the shaded parallelogram of area $AB\sin\varphi$; $\vec A \times \vec B$ (red) is perpendicular to it, on the side given by turning from $\vec A$ to $\vec B$ (curved arrow).*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.5.3: Properties of the Vector Product
> - **Anticommutativity.** $\vec A \times \vec B = -\,\vec B \times \vec A$.
> - **Distributivity.** $\vec A \times (\vec B + \vec C) = \vec A \times \vec B + \vec A \times \vec C$.
> - **Parallel vectors.** $\vec A \times \vec B = \vec 0$ if $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ are parallel or antiparallel; in particular $\vec A \times \vec A = \vec 0$.
> - **Unit vectors.** $\hat\imath \times \hat\imath = \hat\jmath \times \hat\jmath = \hat k \times \hat k = \vec 0$ and, in cyclic order,
>
> $$
> \hat\imath \times \hat\jmath = \hat k, \qquad \hat\jmath \times \hat k = \hat\imath, \qquad \hat k \times \hat\imath = \hat\jmath ,
> $$
>
> while the reversed order gives a minus sign, for example $\hat\jmath \times \hat\imath = -\hat k$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.4, eqs. (2.36)–(2.39)*

^thm-a1-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Swapping the factors keeps the magnitude $AB\sin\varphi$ but reverses the sense of turning, so the corkscrew advances the other way. For parallel or antiparallel vectors $\sin 0 = \sin 180^\circ = 0$. Two different axis unit vectors are perpendicular, so their product has magnitude $\sin 90^\circ = 1$ and lies along the third axis. Turning from $\hat\imath$ to $\hat\jmath$ advances a right-handed screw along $+\hat k$, which is what "right-handed axes" means ([[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^def-a1-3-6|Def. §A1.3.6]]); the other two follow by cyclic relabelling. *Distributivity* (stated in the text without proof): for fixed $\vec A \ne \vec 0$, $\vec A \times \vec V$ is obtained from $\vec V$ in three steps. Project $\vec V$ onto the plane perpendicular to $\vec A$, which leaves a vector of length $V\sin\varphi$; rotate it by $90^\circ$ about $\vec A$ in the right-hand sense; multiply by $A$. Each step carries a parallelogram to a parallelogram, so it carries the sum $\vec B + \vec C$ to the sum of the images, and $\vec A \times (\vec B + \vec C) = \vec A \times \vec B + \vec A \times \vec C$.

^der-a1-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]], [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^def-a1-3-6|Def. §A1.3.6]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.5.4: Vector Product in Components
> $$
> \vec A \times \vec B = (A_yB_z - A_zB_y)\,\hat\imath + (A_zB_x - A_xB_z)\,\hat\jmath + (A_xB_y - A_yB_x)\,\hat k .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.4, eqs. (2.40)–(2.41)*

^thm-a1-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Expand $(A_x\hat\imath + A_y\hat\jmath + A_z\hat k) \times (B_x\hat\imath + B_y\hat\jmath + B_z\hat k)$ by distributivity into nine terms, keeping the order of the factors. The three terms with equal unit vectors vanish. The other six are $A_xB_y\,\hat k - A_xB_z\,\hat\jmath - A_yB_x\,\hat k + A_yB_z\,\hat\imath + A_zB_x\,\hat\jmath - A_zB_y\,\hat\imath$ by the unit-vector table. Grouping by unit vector gives the formula. Each component has the pattern "next times next-next, minus the reverse" in the cyclic order $x \to y \to z \to x$.

^der-a1-5-4

*Uses:* [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Dot or cross, and the order
> - $\vec A \cdot \vec B$ is a number; $\vec A \times \vec B$ is a vector. The terms "dot product" and "scalar product" are synonyms, as are "cross product" and "vector product".
> - In a cross product the order matters: $\vec B \times \vec A$ points the opposite way. Keep the order through every step of an expansion.
> - The scalar product is largest for parallel vectors, the vector product for perpendicular ones.

^cau-a1-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Vectors in a plane
> If $\vec R$ and $\vec F$ both lie in the $xy$-plane, $\vec R \times \vec F$ lies along $z$, and $\vec R \times \vec F = RF\sin\varphi\;\hat k$ with $\varphi$ measured **counterclockwise** from $\vec R$ (the first factor) to $\vec F$. A clockwise angle counts as negative. The sign of the $z$-component then replaces the corkscrew rule.

^rem-a1-5-2

> [!example] Example §A1.5.2: Torque on a Wrench
> The torque that turns a nut is $\vec\tau = \vec R \times \vec F$ (defined in [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^def-a6-3-1|Def. §A6.3.1]]), with $\vec R$ from the nut to the point where the force acts. Here $R = 0.25\ \mathrm m$ and $F = 20.00\ \mathrm N$, in the $xy$-plane, with the nut at the origin.
> - Force at $\varphi = +40^\circ$ (counterclockwise from $\vec R$): $\vec\tau = (0.25\ \mathrm m)(20.00\ \mathrm N)\sin 40^\circ\ \hat k = +3.2\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}\ \hat k$, out of the page. This loosens the nut.
> - Force at $45^\circ$ clockwise from $\vec R$, so $\varphi = -45^\circ$: $\vec\tau = (5.00\ \mathrm{N\cdot m})\sin(-45^\circ)\ \hat k = -3.5\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}\ \hat k$, into the page. This tightens it.
> - The magnitude $RF\sin\varphi$ is largest at $\varphi = 90^\circ$: $\tau_{\max} = RF = 5.00\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}$. Pushing perpendicular to the handle is most effective.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 2.18*

^ex-a1-5-2

> [!example] Example §A1.5.3: A Cross Product in Components
> The magnetic force on a moving charged particle is $\vec F = \zeta\,\vec u \times \vec B$, with $\zeta > 0$ a constant (Electromagnetism, planned). Take $\vec u = -5.0\,\hat\imath - 2.0\,\hat\jmath + 3.5\,\hat k$ and $\vec B = 7.2\,\hat\imath - 1.0\,\hat\jmath - 2.4\,\hat k$, in suitable units. By [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-4|Theorem §A1.5.4]],
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> (\vec u \times \vec B)_x &= (-2.0)(-2.4) - (3.5)(-1.0) = 8.3,\\
> (\vec u \times \vec B)_y &= (3.5)(7.2) - (-5.0)(-2.4) = 13.2,\\
> (\vec u \times \vec B)_z &= (-5.0)(-1.0) - (-2.0)(7.2) = 19.4,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> so $\vec F = \zeta\,(8.3\,\hat\imath + 13.2\,\hat\jmath + 19.4\,\hat k)$ with $F = 24.9\,\zeta$. Check with [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]: $\vec F \cdot \vec B = \zeta\,(59.8 - 13.2 - 46.6) = 0$ and $\vec F \cdot \vec u = \zeta\,(-41.5 - 26.4 + 67.9) = 0$. The force is perpendicular to both the velocity and the field, as every cross product must be.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 2.19*

^ex-a1-5-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The component formula of [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]] is the dot product of $\mathbb R^3$ ([[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-1|LADR 6.1]]).
> - $\lvert\vec A \cdot \vec B\rvert \le AB$, which is $\lvert\cos\varphi\rvert \le 1$ here, is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality ([[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-14|LADR 6.14]]). In abstract inner-product spaces it is what makes "the angle between two vectors" meaningful.
> - The cross-product formula is often remembered as a $3 \times 3$ determinant with $\hat\imath, \hat\jmath, \hat k$ in the first row ([[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-43|LADR 9.43]]).
> - Bilinear and anticommutative, the cross product is the three-dimensional shortcut for the wedge product of differential forms ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^def-22-1|Def. §22.1]]): its three components are the three $2\times2$ minors that the wedge product produces ([[§22 The Algebra of Differential Forms#^rem-22-3|§22, Remark: The Cross Product Is the Pullback in Disguise]]).
