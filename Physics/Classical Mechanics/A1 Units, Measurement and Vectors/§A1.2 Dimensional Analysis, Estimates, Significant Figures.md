Units ([[§A1.1 Units, Standards and Conversion|§A1.1]]) say how a quantity is measured. This section asks what can be learned before and after a calculation: dimensions test whether an equation can be right at all, estimates say roughly what the answer should be, and uncertainties and significant figures say how far a measured or computed number can be trusted.

## Dimensions

> [!definition] Definition §A1.2.1: Dimension
> The **dimension** of a physical quantity $Q$ expresses how it depends on the base quantities, as a product of powers of their symbols:
>
> $$
> [Q] = \mathrm L^{a}\,\mathrm M^{b}\,\mathrm T^{c}\,\mathrm I^{d}\,\Theta^{e}\,\mathrm N^{f}\,\mathrm J^{g},
> $$
>
> with L length, M mass, T time, I current, $\Theta$ temperature, N amount of substance, J luminous intensity. For example $[v] = \mathrm{LT^{-1}}$ and $[\rho] = \mathrm{ML^{-3}}$. A quantity with all seven exponents zero is **dimensionless** (a **pure number**), as are $\pi$, $2$ and an angle in radians. Dimensions obey the rules of algebra, like units.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.4, Table 1.3*

^def-a1-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.2.2: Dimensional Consistency
> An equation relating physical quantities is **dimensionally consistent** if
> 1. every term in it, on either side of the equals sign, has the same dimension; and
> 2. the argument of every standard function in it (sine, cosine, logarithm, exponential) is dimensionless.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.4*

^def-a1-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: What dimensions can and cannot do
> An equation that is not dimensionally consistent cannot be a correct physical law. Checking dimensions therefore catches typos and algebra slips and helps recall formulas: $\pi r^2$ has dimension $\mathrm L^2$ and can be an area, while $2\pi r$ has dimension $\mathrm L$ and cannot. The check is necessary but not sufficient. It is blind to dimensionless factors ($2\pi r^2$ passes as an area), and correct units do not guarantee a correct number.

^rem-a1-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.2.1: Dimensions of Derivatives and Integrals
> For physical quantities $v$ and $t$,
>
> $$
> \left[\frac{dv}{dt}\right] = \frac{[v]}{[t]}, \qquad \left[\int v\,dt\right] = [v]\,[t].
> $$
>
> The same rules hold for units.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.4*

^thm-a1-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The derivative is the limit of the quotients $\Delta v/\Delta t$. Every such quotient has dimension $[v]/[t]$, and taking a limit does not change dimension. The integral is the limit of sums $\sum_k v(t_k)\,\Delta t_k$. Every term has dimension $[v][t]$, and a sum of terms of one dimension has that dimension.

^der-a1-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 Dimensional Analysis, Estimates, Significant Figures#^def-a1-2-1|Def. §A1.2.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^def-28-1|451 Def. §28.1]], [[§32 The Definition of the Riemann Integral#^def-32-8|451 Def. §32.8]]

> [!example] Example §A1.2.1: Checking Equations
> Let $[s] = \mathrm L$, $[v] = \mathrm{LT^{-1}}$, $[a] = \mathrm{LT^{-2}}$, $[t] = \mathrm T$.
> - $s = vt + 0.5\,at^2$: the terms have dimensions $\mathrm L$, $\mathrm{LT^{-1}\cdot T} = \mathrm L$ and $\mathrm{LT^{-2}\cdot T^2} = \mathrm L$. Consistent.
> - $s = vt^2 + 0.5\,at$: the terms have dimensions $\mathrm L$, $\mathrm{LT}$ and $\mathrm{LT^{-1}}$. All three differ, so the equation is nonsense.
> - $v = \sin(at^2/s)$: the argument has dimension $\mathrm{LT^{-2}T^2/L} = 1$, as it must. But the left side is $\mathrm{LT^{-1}}$ and the right side is dimensionless, so the equation is still inconsistent.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 1.5*

^ex-a1-2-1

## Estimates

> [!remark] Remark: How to estimate
> An **estimate** (order-of-magnitude or Fermi calculation) uses experience and sound physical reasoning to get the rough size of a quantity. It is not a random guess. Some strategies:
> - **Big lengths from small ones**, and the reverse: count floors and multiply by a floor height; divide the thickness of a stack of paper by the number of sheets.
> - **Areas and volumes from lengths**: model the object as a sphere or a box.
> - **Masses from volumes and densities**: air is about $1\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, water $10^3\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, and the densest everyday solids about $10^4\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$.
> - **If all else fails, bound it**: take the geometric mean of a lower and an upper bound. A moose is heavier than a person ($10^2\ \mathrm{kg}$) and lighter than a car ($10^3\ \mathrm{kg}$), so estimate $(10^2 \times 10^3)^{1/2} \approx 3 \times 10^2\ \mathrm{kg}$.
> - **One significant figure is enough.**
> - **Ask whether the result makes sense**: compare with known quantities of the same dimension, then check units, arithmetic and logic.

^rem-a1-2-2

> [!example] Example §A1.2.2: Mass of Earth's Oceans
> Oceans cover most of Earth, so take their area to be Earth's surface, $A = \pi d^2$ with $d \approx 10^7\ \mathrm m$: $A \approx 3 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm m^2$. The depth lies between about $1\ \mathrm{km}$ (common) and $10\ \mathrm{km}$ (the deepest points), so take $D \approx (10^3 \times 10^4)^{1/2}\ \mathrm m \approx 3 \times 10^3\ \mathrm m$. Then
>
> $$
> M = \rho A D \approx (10^3\ \mathrm{kg/m^3})(3 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm m^2)(3 \times 10^3\ \mathrm m) \approx 9 \times 10^{20}\ \mathrm{kg} \sim 10^{21}\ \mathrm{kg}.
> $$
>
> This lies sensibly between the mass of the atmosphere ($\sim 10^{19}\ \mathrm{kg}$) and that of Earth ($\sim 10^{25}\ \mathrm{kg}$), and the quoted value $1.4 \times 10^{21}\ \mathrm{kg}$ has the same order of magnitude.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 1.6*

^ex-a1-2-2

## Accuracy, precision and uncertainty

> [!definition] Definition §A1.2.3: Accuracy and Discrepancy; Precision and Uncertainty
> - **Accuracy** is how close a measurement is to an accepted reference value. It is quantified by the **discrepancy**, the measured value minus the reference value.
> - **Precision** is how closely repeated, independent measurements under the same conditions agree with each other. It is quantified by the **uncertainty** $\delta A$, a measure of their spread (for example, the range or the standard deviation). A result is reported as $A \pm \delta A$.
>
> Accuracy needs a reference value; precision does not.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.6*

^def-a1-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Agreement within uncertainty
> Three measurements of a sheet of paper whose length is quoted as $11.0$ in give $11.1$, $11.2$ and $10.9$ in. The best value is the mean, $11.1$ in. The range, $0.3$ in, gives the uncertainty, so the result is $11.1 \pm 0.3$ in. The discrepancy is $0.1$ in, smaller than the uncertainty, so the measurement agrees with the reference value *within experimental uncertainty*. Uncertainty comes from the limits of the instrument, the skill of the observer, irregularities of the object, and anything else that affects the outcome.

^rem-a1-2-3

> [!definition] Definition §A1.2.4: Percent Uncertainty
> For a measurement $A \pm \delta A$, the **percent uncertainty** is
>
> $$
> \frac{\delta A}{A} \times 100\% .
> $$
>
> It is dimensionless. For instance, $5.1 \pm 0.2$ lb is $5.1$ lb $\pm\, 4\%$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.6, eq. (1.1); Example 1.7*

^def-a1-2-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.2.2: Method of Adding Percents
> If a quantity is calculated by multiplying or dividing measured quantities with small uncertainties (a few percent or less), its percent uncertainty is the sum of their percent uncertainties. For two factors,
>
> $$
> \frac{\delta(AB)}{AB} \approx \frac{\delta(A/B)}{A/B} \approx \frac{\delta A}{A} + \frac{\delta B}{B} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.6*

^thm-a1-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The text states the rule without proof. In the worst case both errors push the same way: $(A + \delta A)(B + \delta B) = AB\bigl(1 + \tfrac{\delta A}{A} + \tfrac{\delta B}{B} + \tfrac{\delta A}{A}\tfrac{\delta B}{B}\bigr)$. The last term is a product of two small numbers and can be dropped. For a quotient, $\dfrac{A + \delta A}{B - \delta B} = \dfrac AB\bigl(1 + \tfrac{\delta A}{A}\bigr)\bigl(1 - \tfrac{\delta B}{B}\bigr)^{-1} \approx \dfrac AB\bigl(1 + \tfrac{\delta A}{A} + \tfrac{\delta B}{B}\bigr)$, using $(1 - \epsilon)^{-1} \approx 1 + \epsilon$ for small $\epsilon$.

^der-a1-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 Dimensional Analysis, Estimates, Significant Figures#^def-a1-2-4|Def. §A1.2.4]]

> [!example] Example §A1.2.3: Area of a Floor
> A floor measures $4.00\ \mathrm m$ by $3.00\ \mathrm m$, with uncertainties of $2\%$ and $1\%$. Its area is $12.0\ \mathrm m^2$ with uncertainty $2\% + 1\% = 3\%$ ([[§A1.2 Dimensional Analysis, Estimates, Significant Figures#^thm-a1-2-2|Theorem §A1.2.2]]), that is $0.03 \times 12.0\ \mathrm m^2 = 0.36\ \mathrm m^2$. The area is given to a tenth of a square meter, so the uncertainty is rounded the same way: $A = 12.0 \pm 0.4\ \mathrm m^2$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.6*

^ex-a1-2-3

## Significant figures

> [!definition] Definition §A1.2.5: Significant Figures
> In a measured value, the last digit written is the first digit that carries some uncertainty. The **significant figures** are the digits from the first nonzero digit on the left through that last digit. Zeros are significant except when they only locate the decimal point: $0.053$ has two significant figures and $10.053$ has five. A number like $1300$ is ambiguous, so it is written $1.3 \times 10^3$, $1.30 \times 10^3$ or $1.300 \times 10^3$ for two, three or four significant figures.
>
> *Source: UP1 §1.6*

^def-a1-2-5

> [!remark] Remark: Significant figures in calculations
> A result can be no more precise than the least precise input.
> - **Multiplication and division**: keep as many significant figures as the input with the fewest. With $r = 1.2\ \mathrm m$, $A = \pi r^2 = 4.5\ \mathrm m^2$, not $4.5238934\ \mathrm m^2$.
> - **Addition and subtraction**: keep no more decimal places than the input with the fewest. $7.56\ \mathrm{kg} - 6.052\ \mathrm{kg} + 13.7\ \mathrm{kg} = 15.208\ \mathrm{kg}$ is reported as $15.2\ \mathrm{kg}$.
> - Exact numbers, such as the 2 in $C = 2\pi r$ and conversion factors like $100\ \mathrm{cm}/1\ \mathrm m$, do not limit the result.
>
> The text, and these notes, normally work with three significant figures.

^rem-a1-2-4

> [!caution] Caution: Round at the end
> Carry at least one or two extra digits through intermediate steps and round only the final answer. Rounding partial results early can make the final answer noticeably wrong.

^cau-a1-2-1

## Solving problems

> [!remark] Remark: Strategy, solution, significance
> The worked examples in these notes follow three stages.
> - **Strategy.** Identify the physical principles involved and draw a sketch, choosing and marking a positive direction. List the knowns, including implied ones ("stopped" means $v = 0$), and the unknowns. Find equations that connect them, as many independent equations as there are unknowns.
> - **Solution.** Do the algebra, then substitute numbers with their units.
> - **Significance.** Check the units. Check that the magnitude and sign are reasonable, by comparing with an estimate. Ask what the result says about the world, and whether the method could answer other questions.
