Drawing vectors to scale ([[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components|§A1.3]]) is only as accurate as a ruler and a protractor, and it becomes unmanageable for many vectors. Once vectors are written in components, every vector operation reduces to arithmetic on numbers, done separately along each axis. This analytical method is the one used throughout mechanics.

## Sums and multiples in components

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.1: Component Rules
> Vectors add, and are multiplied by numbers, component by component:
>
> $$
> \vec A + \vec B = (A_x + B_x)\,\hat\imath + (A_y + B_y)\,\hat\jmath + (A_z + B_z)\,\hat k, \qquad \alpha\vec A = \alpha A_x\,\hat\imath + \alpha A_y\,\hat\jmath + \alpha A_z\,\hat k .
> $$
>
> For the resultant of $N$ vectors $\vec F_k = F_{kx}\hat\imath + F_{ky}\hat\jmath + F_{kz}\hat k$,
>
> $$
> \vec F_R = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \vec F_k, \qquad F_{Rx} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} F_{kx}, \quad F_{Ry} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} F_{ky}, \quad F_{Rz} = \sum_{k=1}^{N} F_{kz} .
> $$
>
> In particular, multiplication by a number distributes over a vector sum, $\alpha(\vec A + \vec B) = \alpha\vec A + \alpha\vec B$, and $-\vec A = -A_x\hat\imath - A_y\hat\jmath - A_z\hat k$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.3, eqs. (2.22)–(2.25)*

^thm-a1-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Sums.* Write $\vec A + \vec B = (A_x\hat\imath + A_y\hat\jmath + A_z\hat k) + (B_x\hat\imath + B_y\hat\jmath + B_z\hat k)$. Commutativity and associativity allow the six terms to be regrouped by unit vector, and distributivity over numbers combines $A_x\hat\imath + B_x\hat\imath = (A_x + B_x)\hat\imath$, and likewise for $y$ and $z$. The same regrouping handles $N$ vectors. *Multiples.* Scaling $\vec A$ by $\alpha$ scales the box whose diagonal is $\vec A$ by the same factor, reversing it if $\alpha < 0$; its edges, the components, become $\alpha A_x, \alpha A_y, \alpha A_z$. *Distributivity over sums.* By the two rules just shown, $\alpha(\vec A + \vec B)$ and $\alpha\vec A + \alpha\vec B$ both have $x$-component $\alpha(A_x + B_x) = \alpha A_x + \alpha B_x$, and similarly for $y$ and $z$. A vector is the sum of its vector components, so the two are the same vector.

^der-a1-4-1

## Equality and the null vector

> [!definition] Definition §A1.4.1: Null Vector
> The **null vector** $\vec 0 = 0\,\hat\imath + 0\,\hat\jmath + 0\,\hat k$ has all components zero. It has zero length and no direction. It is the vector analogue of the number zero: $\vec A + \vec 0 = \vec A$ and $\vec A + (-\vec A) = \vec 0$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.3*

^def-a1-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.2: Equal Vectors Have Equal Components
> $$
> \vec A = \vec B \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A_x = B_x,\quad A_y = B_y,\quad A_z = B_z .
> $$
>
> So one vector equation is equivalent to three scalar equations, one per axis.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.3, eq. (2.24)*

^thm-a1-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> If the components agree, then $\vec A = A_x\hat\imath + A_y\hat\jmath + A_z\hat k = \vec B$. Conversely, $\vec A = \vec B$ means $\vec A - \vec B = \vec 0$. By [[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]], $\vec A - \vec B = (A_x - B_x)\hat\imath + (A_y - B_y)\hat\jmath + (A_z - B_z)\hat k$, whose magnitude $\sqrt{(A_x - B_x)^2 + (A_y - B_y)^2 + (A_z - B_z)^2}$ vanishes only if every component does.

^der-a1-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]], [[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^def-a1-4-1|Def. §A1.4.1]], [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^thm-a1-3-2|Theorem §A1.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The analytical method
> 1. Choose axes, and resolve every vector into components with $A_x = A\cos\theta_A$, $A_y = A\sin\theta_A$ ([[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^thm-a1-3-2|Theorem §A1.3.2]]).
> 2. Add the components along each axis separately ([[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]).
> 3. Recombine: find the magnitude and the direction angle of the result, watching the quadrant.
>
> No figure is needed for the computation, but a sketch is the quickest check that the answer makes sense. The result is exact, not limited by the resolution of a ruler. The same three steps find resultant displacements, velocities and forces, and a balance of forces is the statement that the force components sum to zero along each axis ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A7.1 Conditions for Equilibrium|§A7.1]]).

^rem-a1-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.4.2: Unit Vector of a Direction
> The unit vector in the direction of a nonzero vector $\vec V$ is
>
> $$
> \hat V = \frac{\vec V}{V} .
> $$
>
> Numerator and denominator have the same unit, so $\hat V$ is dimensionless. In components, $\hat V = (V_x/V)\,\hat\imath + (V_y/V)\,\hat\jmath + (V_z/V)\,\hat k$. For a vector along an axis this is simple: $\vec d = -5\ \mathrm m\,\hat\imath$ has $\hat d = -\hat\imath$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.3, eq. (2.26)*

^def-a1-4-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.4.1: Tug-of-War
> Three dogs pull on a toy with forces $A = 160.0\ \mathrm N$ at $55^\circ$ south of east, $B = 200.0\ \mathrm N$ at $60^\circ$ east of north and $C = 140.0\ \mathrm N$ at $55^\circ$ west of north. With which force must a fourth dog pull so that the toy does not move? Take $\hat\imath$ east and $\hat\jmath$ north. The direction angles are $\theta_A = -55^\circ$, $\theta_B = 30^\circ$ and $\theta_C = 145^\circ$, so
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \vec A &= (91.8\,\hat\imath - 131.1\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm N, \qquad \vec B = (173.2\,\hat\imath + 100.0\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm N, \qquad \vec C = (-114.7\,\hat\imath + 80.3\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm N,\\
> \vec R &= \vec A + \vec B + \vec C = (150.3\,\hat\imath + 49.2\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm N .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> Balance requires $\vec D + \vec R = \vec 0$, so $\vec D = -\vec R = (-150.3\,\hat\imath - 49.2\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm N$. Its magnitude is $D = \sqrt{150.3^2 + 49.2^2}\ \mathrm N = 158.1\ \mathrm N$. Its angle is $\tan^{-1}(49.2/150.3) = 18.1^\circ$ from the $x$ axis, and since both components are negative it points $18.1^\circ$ south of west.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 2.10*

> [!example] Example §A1.4.2: Rescue Vector
> A Coast Guard ship is a distance $a$ from a checkpoint, $45^\circ$ north of west. A sailboat in distress is a distance $b$ from the same checkpoint, $30^\circ$ south of east. Where must the ship go? Take $\hat\imath$ east and $\hat\jmath$ north, with the checkpoint at the origin:
>
> $$
> \vec A = \frac{a}{\sqrt 2}\,(-\hat\imath + \hat\jmath), \qquad \vec B = b\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\,\hat\imath - \frac12\,\hat\jmath\Bigr).
> $$
>
> The ship's path $\vec R$ runs from its position to the sailboat, so $\vec A + \vec R = \vec B$, that is
>
> $$
> \vec R = \vec B - \vec A = \Bigl(\frac{\sqrt3\,b}{2} + \frac{a}{\sqrt2}\Bigr)\hat\imath - \Bigl(\frac b2 + \frac{a}{\sqrt2}\Bigr)\hat\jmath ,
> $$
>
> $$
> R^2 = R_x^2 + R_y^2 = a^2 + b^2 + \frac{\sqrt6 + \sqrt2}{2}\,ab = a^2 + b^2 + 2ab\cos 15^\circ .
> $$
>
> The ship heads $\tan^{-1}\!\bigl[(b/2 + a/\sqrt2)/(\sqrt3\,b/2 + a/\sqrt2)\bigr]$ south of east. Check: $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ make an angle of $165^\circ$, and the law of cosines gives the same $R^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos 165^\circ$. Components produce it without any geometry.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 7*

^ex-a1-4-2

> [!example] Example §A1.4.3: Direction of Motion
> A convoy moves with $\vec v = (4.000\,\hat\imath + 3.000\,\hat\jmath + 0.100\,\hat k)\ \mathrm{km/h}$, with $\hat\imath$ east, $\hat\jmath$ north and $\hat k$ up. Its speed is $v = \sqrt{4.000^2 + 3.000^2 + 0.100^2}\ \mathrm{km/h} = 5.001\ \mathrm{km/h}$, so by [[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^def-a1-4-2|Def. §A1.4.2]]
>
> $$
> \hat v = \frac{\vec v}{v} = 0.7998\,\hat\imath + 0.5999\,\hat\jmath + 0.0200\,\hat k .
> $$
>
> On the ground it moves $\tan^{-1}(3/4) \approx 37^\circ$ north of east while climbing $100\ \mathrm{m/h}$. A retreat is antiparallel, along $-\hat v$: $37^\circ$ south of west, descending.
>
> *Source: UP1 Examples 2.8, 2.14*
