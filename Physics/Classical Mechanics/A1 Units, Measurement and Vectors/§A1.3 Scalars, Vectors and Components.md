A number with a unit ([[§A1.1 Units, Standards and Conversion|§A1.1]]) does not fix a displacement, a velocity or a force: "6 km from the tent" does not find the fishing hole unless the direction is given too. This section introduces vectors, first as arrows that add geometrically and then through their components along coordinate axes, which turn vector problems into problems about numbers.

## Scalars and vectors

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.1: Scalar and Vector Quantities
> A **scalar** quantity is specified completely by a single number with a unit: time, mass, distance, volume, temperature, energy. A **vector** quantity needs a magnitude and a direction: displacement, velocity, force, torque. A vector is written with an arrow, $\vec A$. Its **magnitude** $A = \lvert\vec A\rvert \ge 0$ is a scalar. A vector is drawn as an arrow from its **tail** to its **head**, with length proportional to its magnitude.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.1*

^def-a1-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Displacement, the model vector
> The **displacement** from point $A$ to point $B$, $\vec D_{AB}$, is the arrow from $A$ to $B$. It is the same whichever path is taken between the two points, and its magnitude (the straight-line distance) is in general less than the length of the path. The displacement from $B$ back to $A$ has the same magnitude and the opposite direction.

^rem-a1-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.2: Parallel, Antiparallel, Equal and Orthogonal Vectors
> Two vectors are **parallel** if they have the same direction, **antiparallel** if their directions differ by $180^\circ$, and **orthogonal** if their directions differ by $90^\circ$. Two vectors are **equal**, $\vec A = \vec B$, if they are parallel and have equal magnitudes. An arrow may therefore be moved parallel to itself without changing the vector it represents. The antiparallel vector of equal magnitude is written $-\vec A$; for example $\vec D_{BA} = -\vec D_{AB}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.1*

^def-a1-3-2

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.3: Multiplication by a Scalar
> For a number $\alpha$, the vector $\vec B = \alpha\vec A$ has magnitude
>
> $$
> B = \lvert\alpha\rvert\,A ,
> $$
>
> and is parallel to $\vec A$ if $\alpha > 0$ and antiparallel to it if $\alpha < 0$. Dividing by a nonzero number $\alpha$ means multiplying by $1/\alpha$. Division *by a vector* is not defined.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.1, eqs. (2.1)–(2.2)*

^def-a1-3-3

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.4: Vector Sum and Difference
> - **Parallelogram rule.** Translate $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ so that their tails coincide and complete the parallelogram. The **resultant** $\vec R = \vec A + \vec B$ is the diagonal from the common tail to the opposite corner.
> - **Tail-to-head rule.** Place the tail of each vector at the head of the previous one. The resultant of all of them runs from the tail of the first to the head of the last.
> - **Difference.** $\vec A - \vec B = \vec A + (-\vec B)$. In the parallelogram it is the other diagonal, from the head of $\vec B$ to the head of $\vec A$.
>
> Only quantities of the same kind can be added: vectors to vectors, scalars to scalars, never a scalar to a vector.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.1, eqs. (2.3)–(2.5)*

^def-a1-3-4

Green: the difference $\vec A - \vec B$ along the other diagonal, from the head of $\vec B$ to the head of $\vec A$. Neither diagonal has length $A + B$ or $\lvert A - B\rvert$.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.3.1: Laws of Vector Addition
> For vectors $\vec A, \vec B, \vec C$ and numbers $\alpha_1, \alpha_2$:
>
> $$
> \vec A + \vec B = \vec B + \vec A, \qquad (\vec A + \vec B) + \vec C = \vec A + (\vec B + \vec C), \qquad \alpha_1\vec A + \alpha_2\vec A = (\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)\vec A .
> $$
>
> So any number of vectors can be added in any order and grouping.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.1, eqs. (2.7)–(2.9)*

^thm-a1-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The text states these laws; they follow from the constructions. *Commutativity:* in the parallelogram on $\vec A$ and $\vec B$, going along $\vec A$ and then along the opposite side (a copy of $\vec B$), or along $\vec B$ and then along a copy of $\vec A$, reaches the same far corner. *Associativity:* placed tail to head, $\vec A, \vec B, \vec C$ form a chain, and both $(\vec A + \vec B) + \vec C$ and $\vec A + (\vec B + \vec C)$ run from the tail of $\vec A$ to the head of $\vec C$. *Distributivity:* both $\alpha_1\vec A + \alpha_2\vec A$ and $(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)\vec A$ lie along the line of $\vec A$, with signed length $(\alpha_1 + \alpha_2)A$ measured along $\vec A$.

^der-a1-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^def-a1-3-3|Def. §A1.3.3]], [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^def-a1-3-4|Def. §A1.3.4]]

> [!caution] Caution: Magnitudes do not add
> In general $\lvert\vec A + \vec B\rvert \ne A + B$: a diagonal of a parallelogram is not the sum of two sides. Equality holds only for parallel vectors. For antiparallel vectors $\lvert\vec A + \vec B\rvert = \lvert A - B\rvert$, and the sum points along the longer one. In every case $\lvert\vec A + \vec B\rvert \le A + B$.

^cau-a1-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.5: Unit Vector
> A **unit vector** $\hat u$ has magnitude $1$ and no physical unit. Its only job is to specify a direction: a displacement of $6.0\ \mathrm{km}$ to the northeast is $\vec D = (6.0\ \mathrm{km})\,\hat u$ with $\hat u$ pointing northeast, and $-\hat u$ points southwest.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.1*

^def-a1-3-5

> [!example] Example §A1.3.1: A Ladybug on a Meter Stick
> A long measuring stick leans against a wall with its $200$-cm end on the floor. A ladybug lands on the $100$-cm mark and crawls $15$ cm toward the floor, $56$ cm toward the wall, $3$ cm and then $25$ cm toward the floor, and finally $19$ cm toward the wall. Let $\hat u$ point along the stick toward the floor. By [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^thm-a1-3-1|Theorem §A1.3.1]],
>
> $$
> \vec D = (15 - 56 + 3 + 25 - 19)\ \mathrm{cm}\ \hat u = (-32\ \mathrm{cm})\,\hat u .
> $$
>
> The net displacement is $32$ cm toward the wall, so the ladybug ends at the $68$-cm mark, although it crawled $118$ cm in all.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 2.1*

^ex-a1-3-1

## Components

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.6: Vector and Scalar Components
> In a Cartesian $xy$-system, let $\hat\imath$ and $\hat\jmath$ be the unit vectors along $+x$ and $+y$. A vector in the plane is the sum of its **vector components**, its orthogonal projections onto the axes:
>
> $$
> \vec A = \vec A_x + \vec A_y = A_x\,\hat\imath + A_y\,\hat\jmath .
> $$
>
> The numbers $A_x, A_y$ are the **scalar components**. They carry signs, and $\lvert A_x\rvert = \lvert\vec A_x\rvert$. If the vector runs from a point $b(x_b, y_b)$ to a point $e(x_e, y_e)$, then
>
> $$
> A_x = x_e - x_b, \qquad A_y = y_e - y_b .
> $$
>
> In three dimensions, with the **right-handed** order of axes $x, y, z$ (unit vectors $\hat\imath, \hat\jmath, \hat k$), $\vec A = A_x\hat\imath + A_y\hat\jmath + A_z\hat k$ and $A_z = z_e - z_b$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.2, eqs. (2.10)–(2.13), (2.19)–(2.20)*

^def-a1-3-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.3.2: Magnitude and Direction from Components
> The magnitude of a vector is
>
> $$
> A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2} \quad\text{(plane)}, \qquad A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2} \quad\text{(space)}.
> $$
>
> In the plane, the **direction angle** $\theta_A$ is measured counterclockwise from the $+x$ axis to the vector, and
>
> $$
> \tan\theta_A = \frac{A_y}{A_x}, \qquad A_x = A\cos\theta_A, \qquad A_y = A\sin\theta_A .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.2, eqs. (2.15)–(2.17), (2.21)*

^thm-a1-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the plane, $\vec A$ is the diagonal of a rectangle with sides $\lvert A_x\rvert$ and $\lvert A_y\rvert$, so Pythagoras gives $A^2 = A_x^2 + A_y^2$. Squares do not see signs, so this holds in every quadrant. In space, the projection of $\vec A$ onto the $xy$-plane has length $\sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}$ and is perpendicular to $\vec A_z$; Pythagoras once more adds $A_z^2$. The relations $A_x = A\cos\theta_A$ and $A_y = A\sin\theta_A$ are the definitions of cosine and sine for the angle $\theta_A$ measured from $+x$, valid with signs in all four quadrants. Their quotient gives $\tan\theta_A$.

^der-a1-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^def-a1-3-6|Def. §A1.3.6]]

> [!caution] Caution: Which quadrant?
> A calculator's $\tan^{-1}(A_y/A_x)$ always returns an angle $\theta$ between $-90^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, and $\tan\theta$ cannot tell $\vec A$ from $-\vec A$. If $A_x > 0$ (quadrants I and IV), $\theta_A = \theta$; a negative $\theta$ is measured clockwise. If $A_x < 0$ (quadrants II and III), then $\theta_A = \theta + 180^\circ$. Always look at the signs of both components, or a sketch.

^cau-a1-3-2

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.7: Polar Coordinates
> A point $P$ in the plane can be located by its **polar coordinates** $(r, \varphi)$: the **radial coordinate** $r$ is its distance from the origin, and $\varphi$ is the angle (in radians) from the $+x$ axis to the line from the origin to $P$, counterclockwise. Then
>
> $$
> x = r\cos\varphi, \qquad y = r\sin\varphi .
> $$
>
> The unit vector $\hat r$ points radially away from the origin, and $\hat t$, orthogonal to it, points in the direction of increasing $\varphi$. Polar coordinates suit rotation, as Cartesian ones suit straight-line motion.
>
> *Source: UP1 §2.2, eq. (2.18)*

^def-a1-3-7

> [!example] Example §A1.3.2: Displacement of a Mouse Pointer
> A pointer moves on a screen from $b(6.0\ \mathrm{cm}, 1.6\ \mathrm{cm})$ to $e(2.0\ \mathrm{cm}, 4.5\ \mathrm{cm})$, with the origin at the lower left corner. By [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^def-a1-3-6|Def. §A1.3.6]],
>
> $$
> D_x = 2.0 - 6.0 = -4.0\ \mathrm{cm}, \quad D_y = 4.5 - 1.6 = 2.9\ \mathrm{cm}, \quad \vec D = (-4.0\,\hat\imath + 2.9\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm{cm} .
> $$
>
> By [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^thm-a1-3-2|Theorem §A1.3.2]], $D = \sqrt{4.0^2 + 2.9^2}\ \mathrm{cm} = 4.9\ \mathrm{cm}$, and $\tan^{-1}(2.9/(-4.0)) = -35.9^\circ$. Since $D_x < 0$ the vector is in the second quadrant, so $\theta_D = -35.9^\circ + 180^\circ = 144.1^\circ$. The pointer moved $4.0$ cm left and $2.9$ cm up.
>
> *Source: UP1 Examples 2.3–2.4*

^ex-a1-3-2

![[ph-cm-a1-3-2.svg]]
*The displacement of Example §A1.3.2. Red: $\vec D$ from $b$ to $e$. Blue: its vector components $D_x\hat\imath$ (pointing left, since $D_x < 0$) and $D_y\hat\jmath$. The direction angle $\theta_D = 144.1^\circ$ is measured counterclockwise from $+x$. The calculator's $-35.9^\circ$ would describe the opposite vector $-\vec D$.*

> [!example] Example §A1.3.3: Displacement of a Drone
> Take $\hat\imath$ east, $\hat\jmath$ north and $\hat k$ up, with the origin at a control tower. A drone moves from $(300, 200, 100)\ \mathrm m$ to $(1200, 2100, 250)\ \mathrm m$. Then
>
> $$
> \vec D = (900\,\hat\imath + 1900\,\hat\jmath + 150\,\hat k)\ \mathrm m = (0.90\,\hat\imath + 1.90\,\hat\jmath + 0.15\,\hat k)\ \mathrm{km},
> $$
>
> $$
> D = \sqrt{0.90^2 + 1.90^2 + 0.15^2}\ \mathrm{km} = 2.11\ \mathrm{km}.
> $$
>
> The climb of $0.15$ km barely changes the magnitude: it adds in quadrature, not linearly.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 2.7*
