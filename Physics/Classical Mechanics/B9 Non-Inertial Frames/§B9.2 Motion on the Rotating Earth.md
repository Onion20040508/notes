*Sources: Morin, Introduction to Classical Mechanics, §10.2–10.4 · Helliwell & Sahakian, Modern Classical Mechanics, §9.4.*

Every laboratory on Earth is a rotating frame. Level A treated it as inertial, corrected the apparent weight at the equator ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-4|Theorem §A8.1.4]]) and described the Coriolis deflection of winds in words ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^rem-a3-9-3|Remark in §A3.9]]). Here the fictitious forces of [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]] are applied to the turning Earth: the effective gravity at any latitude, the deflection of falling bodies and shells, free horizontal motion and Foucault's pendulum, and the balance that steers large-scale flows. The same equation, in the freely falling frame of Earth's centre, gives the general form of the tidal force of [[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^def-a8-4-1|Def. §A8.4.1]].

## The rotating Earth as a frame

> [!model] Model §B9.2.1: The Rotating Earth
> The Earth-fixed frame rotates with constant angular velocity $\vec\Omega$ along the polar axis, south to north, of magnitude
>
> $$
> \Omega = \frac{2\pi}{\text{sidereal day}} = \frac{2\pi}{86\,164\ \mathrm s} = 7.292\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} .
> $$
>
> (Relative to the stars Earth turns 366.25 times a year, so the sidereal day is about 4 min shorter than the 24 h solar day.) The origin at Earth's centre falls freely around the Sun and with the Moon; the only effect of that acceleration on Earth is tidal (Theorem §B9.2.6), and elsewhere it is ignored. Near a point at latitude $\lambda$ use local axes $\hat x$ east, $\hat y$ north, $\hat z$ up; then
>
> $$
> \vec\Omega = \Omega\,(\cos\lambda\,\hat y + \sin\lambda\,\hat z) .
> $$
>
> *Valid when:* times short compared with the precession of Earth's axis (26 000 years, [[§B10.3 The Heavy Symmetric Top#^rem-b10-3-3|Remark in §B10.3]]); the local form needs distances small compared with $R_E$, so that $\lambda$ and the local vertical are constant.
> *Source: H&S §9.4, eqs. (9.38)–(9.39) · Morin §10.2*

^mod-b9-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Latitude or polar angle
> Morin measures position by the polar angle $\theta = 90^\circ - \lambda$ from the north pole and writes $\omega$ for Earth's rate; H&S use $\lambda$ and $\Omega$, as here. Morin's $\cos\theta$ is $\sin\lambda$: his Foucault rate $\omega\cos\theta$ is the $\Omega\sin\lambda$ below.

^cau-b9-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §B9.2.1: Effective Gravity
> The **effective gravity** at position $\vec r$ (from Earth's centre) is
>
> $$
> \vec g_{\text{eff}} = \vec g - \vec\Omega\times(\vec\Omega\times\vec r),
> $$
>
> the sum of the gravitational field $\vec g$ and the centrifugal force per unit mass. It is what a body at rest in the Earth frame responds to: a plumb line hangs along $\vec g_{\text{eff}}$, a scale under a body at rest reads $mg_{\text{eff}}$ (its apparent weight, [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-2|Def. §A8.1.2]]), and the "$g$" in tables is $g_{\text{eff}}$. The weight of [[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^def-a3-4-1|Def. §A3.4.1]] is the gravitational part $m\vec g$ alone.
>
> *Source: Morin §10.2.2, eq. (10.13) · H&S §9.4*

^def-b9-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.2: Effective Gravity on a Spherical Earth
> If $\vec g$ points to Earth's centre with magnitude $g$, then at latitude $\lambda$ on the surface (radius $R$)
>
> $$
> \vec g_{\text{eff}} = -\bigl(g - R\Omega^2\cos^2\lambda\bigr)\,\hat z - R\Omega^2\sin\lambda\cos\lambda\,\hat y ,
> $$
>
> so $g_{\text{eff}}$ is smaller than $g$ by $R\Omega^2\cos^2\lambda$, and the plumb line is tilted from the radial direction toward the equator by
>
> $$
> \alpha \approx \frac{R\Omega^2}{2g}\,\sin 2\lambda ,
> $$
>
> largest at $\lambda = 45^\circ$, where $\alpha = R\Omega^2/2g = 1.7\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad} \approx 0.1^\circ$. At the equator the first formula is [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-4|Theorem §A8.1.4]]; at the poles $\vec g_{\text{eff}} = \vec g$.
>
> *Source: Morin §10.2.2; Problem 10.3*

^thm-b9-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-6|Theorem §B9.1.6]], the centrifugal acceleration is $\Omega^2\rho$ away from the axis, with $\rho = R\cos\lambda$ the distance from the axis. "Away from the axis" is $\cos\lambda\,\hat z - \sin\lambda\,\hat y$ in local axes (outward from the axis is up at the equator and has a southward horizontal part in the north). So $\vec g_{\text{eff}} = -g\hat z + R\Omega^2\cos\lambda(\cos\lambda\,\hat z - \sin\lambda\,\hat y)$. The angle from the vertical is $\tan\alpha = R\Omega^2\sin\lambda\cos\lambda/(g - R\Omega^2\cos^2\lambda) \approx (R\Omega^2/2g)\sin2\lambda$, since $R\Omega^2 = 0.034\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \ll g$.

^der-b9-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-6|Theorem §B9.1.6]], [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^mod-b9-2-1|Model §B9.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The real Earth
> Earth is not a sphere, and the two corrections feed each other. Its surface is, on average, perpendicular to $\vec g_{\text{eff}}$, so the centrifugal term makes it bulge at the equator; the bulge in turn makes $\vec g$ itself non-radial, by about as much again, which steepens the bulge further (Morin §10.2.2, Remark 1). An equipotential calculation that keeps $\vec g$ radial gives an equatorial bulge of about 11 km; including the bulge's own gravity for a uniform Earth multiplies it by $5/2$, to about 28 km, against the observed 21.5 km, the difference coming from Earth's density increasing toward the centre (Morin Problem 10.12). Together the rotation and the bulge make $g_{\text{eff}}$ about 0.5% smaller at the equator than at the poles, not the 0.3% of the centrifugal term alone.

^rem-b9-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.3: Equation of Motion near Earth's Surface
> In the model, a body of mass $m$ moving near a fixed point of the surface, under real forces $\vec F$ other than gravity, obeys
>
> $$
> m\ddot{\vec r} = \vec F + m\vec g_{\text{eff}} - 2m\,\vec\Omega\times\vec v ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec g_{\text{eff}}$ treated as uniform. In local components,
>
> $$
> -2m\,\vec\Omega\times\vec v = 2m\Omega\bigl[(\sin\lambda\,\dot y - \cos\lambda\,\dot z)\,\hat x - \sin\lambda\,\dot x\,\hat y + \cos\lambda\,\dot x\,\hat z\bigr] .
> $$
>
> For horizontal motion at speed $v$ the horizontal part of the Coriolis force has magnitude $2m\Omega v\,|\sin\lambda|$, whatever the direction of motion, and points to the right of the velocity in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern. It vanishes at the equator.
>
> *Source: H&S §9.4, eqs. (9.40)–(9.42) · Morin §10.2.3*

^thm-b9-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]] with the origin at Earth's centre ($\ddot{\vec R} = \vec 0$ in the model) and $\dot{\vec\Omega} = \vec 0$. The gravitational and centrifugal forces combine into $m\vec g_{\text{eff}}$ (Def. §B9.2.1); over a region small compared with $R_E$ their variation is negligible. The components follow from $\vec\Omega = \Omega(0, \cos\lambda, \sin\lambda)$ and $\vec v = (\dot x, \dot y, \dot z)$: $\vec\Omega\times\vec v = \Omega(\cos\lambda\,\dot z - \sin\lambda\,\dot y,\ \sin\lambda\,\dot x,\ -\cos\lambda\,\dot x)$ ([[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-4|Theorem §A1.5.4]]). For $\dot z = 0$ the horizontal part is $2m\Omega\sin\lambda\,(\dot y, -\dot x)$, which is $\vec v = (\dot x, \dot y)$ turned clockwise by $90^\circ$ and scaled by $2m\Omega\sin\lambda$: to the right if $\sin\lambda > 0$.

^der-b9-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]], [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^mod-b9-2-1|Model §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-4|Theorem §A1.5.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: When the Coriolis force matters
> $2\Omega v$ is $1.5\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ times the speed: about $2\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ for a walker and a few $10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ for a thrown ball, negligible. What makes it visible is time. Over a time $t$ the sideways drift is about $\Omega v t^2$, so it matters for long-range shells, for a pendulum swinging for hours, and above all for air and ocean currents that flow for days. The water in a sink is the opposite case: it crosses the basin in seconds, and the swirl it keeps from filling the basin swamps the Coriolis effect (H&S §9.4; Morin Exercise 10.16).

## Falling bodies and projectiles

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.4: Eastward Deflection of a Falling Body
> A body dropped from rest at height $h$ (no air resistance) lands east of the point vertically below its release by
>
> $$
> d_{\text{east}} = \tfrac13\,\Omega g\cos\lambda\;t^3 = \frac23\,\Omega\cos\lambda\;h\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}
> $$
>
> to first order in $\Omega$, in both hemispheres. From $100\ \mathrm m$ at the equator, $d_{\text{east}} = 2.2\ \mathrm{cm}$.
>
> *Source: Morin §10.2.3 (Example: dropped ball), eq. (10.18) · H&S Problem 9.16*

^thm-b9-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> To zeroth order in $\Omega$ the body falls straight: $\dot x = \dot y = 0$, $\dot z = -gt$ ([[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^mod-a2-3-3|Model §A2.3.3]], with $g = g_{\text{eff}}$). Put this velocity into the Coriolis force of [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]]: the only first-order term is $\ddot x = -2\Omega\cos\lambda\,\dot z = 2\Omega g\cos\lambda\,t$, eastward. Integrating twice from rest, $\dot x = \Omega g\cos\lambda\,t^2$ and $x = \tfrac13\Omega g\cos\lambda\,t^3$; put $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$. The small eastward velocity so acquired produces a second-order Coriolis force, neglected here (Morin, footnote to eq. (10.18)).

^der-b9-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]], [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^mod-a2-3-3|Model §A2.3.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: The same deflection from the inertial frame
> In the inertial frame the top of a tower at the equator moves east at $(R + h)\Omega$, faster than its base at $R\Omega$, and the dropped body keeps that larger speed, less the slowing caused by the slight sideways tilt of gravity as it moves east. Following Morin's inertial-frame calculation, that slowing removes one third of the naive lead $h\Omega t$ and leaves $\tfrac23\Omega h\sqrt{2h/g}$, the same result. In terms of angular momentum: the body's angular momentum about Earth's axis is conserved, so as it falls to a smaller radius it must turn faster than the ground (H&S Problem 9.16). There is also a southward deflection in the northern hemisphere, but it is second order in $\Omega$: the second-order Coriolis term alone gives $\tfrac23(\Omega^2h^2/g)\sin\lambda\cos\lambda$, and the centrifugal force, the decrease of gravity with height and the tilt of the plumb line raise it to $4(\Omega^2h^2/g)\sin\lambda\cos\lambda$ measured from a plumb bob, even for a spherical Earth (Morin Problem 10.13).

^rem-b9-2-3

> [!example] Example §B9.2.1: Shells at the Falklands
> In the 1914 battle off the Falkland Islands ($\lambda = -52^\circ$), British guns fired shells at $v_0 = 823\ \mathrm{m/s}$ at targets up to $15\ \mathrm{km}$ away. Ignoring air resistance, estimate the sideways Coriolis deflection.
>
> *Elevation and flight time.* The range formula ([[§A2.6 Projectile Motion#^thm-a2-6-3|Theorem §A2.6.3]]) gives $\sin2\alpha = gD/v_0^2 = 0.217$, so $\alpha = 6.27^\circ$, and $T = 2v_0\sin\alpha/g = 18.3\ \mathrm s$. The horizontal speed is $u = v_0\cos\alpha = 818\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
>
> *Sideways drift.* By [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]] the horizontal velocity produces a horizontal acceleration $2\Omega|\sin\lambda|\,u$ perpendicular to it, constant to first order in $\Omega$; integrating twice over the flight,
>
> $$
> d = \Omega|\sin\lambda|\,u\,T^2 = \frac{4v_0^3}{g^2}\,\Omega|\sin\lambda|\,\sin^2\alpha\cos\alpha = (7.29\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})(0.788)(818\ \mathrm{m/s})(18.3\ \mathrm s)^2 \approx 16\ \mathrm m ,
> $$
>
> to the left, since $\lambda < 0$. Gunnery tables computed for $52^\circ$ north would correct by $16\ \mathrm m$ to the other side, so the shells would miss by about $32\ \mathrm m$.
>
> *The other terms.* The vertical velocity $\dot z$ gives an east–west acceleration $-2\Omega\cos\lambda\,\dot z$; integrated over the flight it shifts the impact by $\tfrac43\Omega\cos\lambda\,v_0^3\sin^3\alpha/g^2 = 0.45\ \mathrm m$, negligible at this low elevation. For fire due east or west the vertical Coriolis component $2\Omega\cos\lambda\,u = 0.073\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ changes the effective $g$ by $0.75\%$ and so the range by about $110\ \mathrm m$, but along the line of fire.
>
> *Source: H&S Problems 9.10, 9.19*

## Free horizontal motion and Foucault's pendulum

> [!example] Example §B9.2.2: Inertial Circles
> A puck slides at speed $v$ on frictionless ice whose surface is "level", perpendicular to $\vec g_{\text{eff}}$ everywhere. Find its motion in the Earth frame.
>
> The normal force cancels $m\vec g_{\text{eff}}$, and the vertical Coriolis component only changes the normal force. What remains is the horizontal Coriolis force of [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]], of constant magnitude $2m\Omega v\sin\lambda$ and always perpendicular to the velocity. It does no work, so $v$ is constant, and a force of constant size perpendicular to the velocity bends the path into a circle ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^thm-a3-9-1|Theorem §A3.9.1]]):
>
> $$
> 2m\Omega v\sin\lambda = \frac{mv^2}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{v}{2\Omega\sin\lambda}, \qquad \omega' = \frac vr = 2\Omega\sin\lambda ,
> $$
>
> traversed clockwise in the northern hemisphere. At $\lambda = 45^\circ$ and $v = 1\ \mathrm{m/s}$, $r \approx 10\ \mathrm{km}$, so the slightest friction hides it on ice. The motion is the analogue of a charge circling in a uniform magnetic field ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-2|EM Theorem §A7.2.2]]), with $2\Omega\sin\lambda$ in place of the cyclotron frequency $qB/m$.
>
> Near the pole the puck circles at $2\Omega$, not $\Omega$, although a puck at rest in the inertial frame would seem to circle at $\Omega$. Such a puck does not stay put: the "level" surface is not spherical, and the puck would slide toward the pole. To stay at its latitude it must turn at $\Omega$ backward relative to the stars, hence at $2\Omega$ relative to the ground (Morin's remark to Problem 10.7).
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 10.7*

^ex-b9-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.5: Foucault's Pendulum
> A long pendulum on a frictionless pivot at latitude $\lambda$, swinging with small amplitude at angular frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g_{\text{eff}}/\ell} \gg \Omega$, keeps swinging in a vertical plane that turns relative to the ground with angular velocity
>
> $$
> \vec\omega_F = -\,\Omega\sin\lambda\;\hat z ,
> $$
>
> clockwise seen from above in the northern hemisphere and counterclockwise in the southern. The plane makes a full turn in $T_F = (\text{sidereal day})/|\sin\lambda|$: 23.9 h at the poles, $31.8\ \mathrm h$ in Paris ($\lambda = 48.85^\circ$), and never at the equator.
>
> *Source: Morin §10.2.3 (Example: Foucault's pendulum), eq. (10.21) · H&S Example 9.5, eq. (9.55)*

^thm-b9-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $(x, y)$ be the horizontal displacement of the bob. For a long pendulum at small amplitude the bob moves almost horizontally, the tension is $mg_{\text{eff}}$ to zeroth order, and its horizontal component is $-m\omega_0^2(x, y)$ ([[§A1.4 Pendulums#^thm-a1-4-2|WO Theorem §A1.4.2]]). Keep only the horizontal Coriolis force of [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]] (the vertical one changes the tension slightly), and write $\Omega_z = \Omega\sin\lambda$:
>
> $$
> \ddot x - 2\Omega_z\dot y + \omega_0^2x = 0, \qquad \ddot y + 2\Omega_z\dot x + \omega_0^2y = 0 .
> $$
>
> With $\xi = x + iy$ the pair becomes the single equation $\ddot\xi + 2i\Omega_z\dot\xi + \omega_0^2\xi = 0$. Put $\xi = e^{-i\Omega_z t}\eta$: then $\dot\xi = e^{-i\Omega_z t}(\dot\eta - i\Omega_z\eta)$, $\ddot\xi = e^{-i\Omega_z t}(\ddot\eta - 2i\Omega_z\dot\eta - \Omega_z^2\eta)$, and the equation becomes
>
> $$
> \ddot\eta + (\omega_0^2 + \Omega_z^2)\,\eta = 0 .
> $$
>
> This is an ordinary two-dimensional pendulum at $\omega' = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 + \Omega_z^2} \approx \omega_0$, which can swing in a fixed plane. Multiplying by $e^{-i\Omega_z t}$ turns the complex plane through the angle $-\Omega_z t$: the plane of swing turns at $-\Omega_z$, clockwise if $\Omega_z > 0$. In words: only the vertical component $\Omega\sin\lambda$ of Earth's rotation gives a horizontal Coriolis force, so for the pendulum the place is equivalent to the pole of a planet turning at $\Omega\sin\lambda$; at a pole the plane simply stays fixed relative to the stars while the ground turns beneath it (Morin's "Terra Costhetica"; H&S reach the same equations from the rotating-frame Lagrangian of [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^rem-b9-1-2|the Remark in §B9.1]]).

*Theorem §B9.2.5: the exact small-amplitude solution for a bob released from rest at $x = a$, with the rotation exaggerated to $\Omega_z = \omega_0/12$ and shown for three periods, while the plane of swing turns clockwise (northern hemisphere, $\Omega_z > 0$) by $90^\circ$. ## Large-scale flows

> [!remark] Remark: Cyclones and geostrophic balance
> In weather systems the horizontal Coriolis force is comparable to the pressure-gradient force per unit mass $\nabla p/\rho$. Air drawn toward a low is deflected to the right in the northern hemisphere, so it circulates counterclockwise around the low (cyclones, hurricanes), clockwise in the southern hemisphere ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^rem-a3-9-3|Remark in §A3.9]]). In a steady flow the two forces balance, $2\Omega|\sin\lambda|\,v = |\nabla p|/\rho$, and the wind blows along the isobars instead of across them (**geostrophic flow**). The same deflection turns the high-altitude poleward flow from the equator eastward and returns it near $30^\circ$ latitude as the trade winds, from the northeast in the northern hemisphere and from the southeast in the southern (H&S Example 9.4).

^rem-b9-2-4

> [!example] Example §B9.2.3: The Tilt of the Gulf Stream
> The Gulf Stream flows north at somewhat over $1\ \mathrm{m/s}$ through the $80\ \mathrm{km}$ between Florida and the Bahamas (take $\lambda \approx 26^\circ$). Balance the Coriolis force against the pressure difference across the stream.
>
> By [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]] northward flow is pushed east with force $2\rho\Omega\sin\lambda\,v$ per unit volume. A sea surface rising eastward with slope $dh/dx$ produces, at any depth, a hydrostatic pressure gradient $\rho g\,dh/dx$ pushing west. Steady flow needs them to cancel:
>
> $$
> \frac{dh}{dx} = \frac{2\Omega\sin\lambda\,v}{g} = \frac{2(7.29\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})(0.44)(1\ \mathrm{m/s})}{9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}} = 6.5\times10^{-6} ,
> $$
>
> a rise of $0.52\ \mathrm m$ over $80\ \mathrm{km}$, consistent with the measured $0.5\ \mathrm m$ by which the sea at the Bahamas stands higher than off Florida.
>
> *Source: H&S Problem 9.29*

## Tides in the freely falling frame

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.2.6: Tidal Force
> Let a body of mass $M$ be at distance $R$ from Earth's centre, in the direction $-\hat R$ (so $\hat R$ points from $M$ toward Earth). In the non-rotating frame of Earth's centre, which falls freely toward $M$, a mass $m$ at $\vec r$ from the centre ($r \ll R$) feels the gravity of $M$ plus the translational force, which add to
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{tidal}}(\vec r) \approx \frac{GMm}{R^3}\bigl[3\hat R(\hat R\cdot\vec r) - \vec r\bigr], \qquad\text{with $M$ on the $+x$ axis:}\qquad \vec F_{\text{tidal}} = \frac{GMm}{R^3}(2x, -y, -z) ,
> $$
>
> with potential energy $U_{\text{tidal}} = -\dfrac{GMm\,r^2}{2R^3}\bigl(3\cos^2\vartheta - 1\bigr)$, $\vartheta$ the angle between $\vec r$ and the Earth–$M$ line. Along the line it stretches, $2GMmx/R^3$ away from the centre on either side; across it it squeezes, $GMm\,y/R^3$ toward the centre. The corrections are of relative order $r/R$.
>
> *Source: Morin §10.3, eqs. (10.29)–(10.36)*

^thm-b9-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The vector from $M$ to the mass is $\vec R + \vec r$, from $M$ to Earth's centre $\vec R$. By [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]] the gravity of $M$ on $m$ is $-GMm(\vec R + \vec r)/|\vec R + \vec r|^3$. Earth's centre accelerates at $-GM\vec R/R^3$, so the translational force of [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]] is $+GMm\vec R/R^3$. Expand: $|\vec R + \vec r|^2 = R^2(1 + 2\hat R\cdot\vec r/R + r^2/R^2)$, so $|\vec R + \vec r|^{-3} = R^{-3}\bigl(1 - 3\hat R\cdot\vec r/R + O(r^2/R^2)\bigr)$ (binomial expansion). Then
>
> $$
> \frac{\vec F}{GMm} = -\frac{\vec R + \vec r}{R^3}\Bigl(1 - \frac{3\hat R\cdot\vec r}{R}\Bigr) + \frac{\vec R}{R^3} = \frac{3\hat R(\hat R\cdot\vec r) - \vec r}{R^3} + O\Bigl(\frac{r^2}{R^4}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> With $M$ on the $+x$ axis, $\hat R = -\hat x$ and $3\hat R(\hat R\cdot\vec r) = 3x\hat x$. Finally $-\nabla\bigl[-\tfrac{GMm}{2R^3}(3x^2 - r^2)\bigr] = \tfrac{GMm}{R^3}(2x, -y, -z)$, and $3x^2 - r^2 = r^2(3\cos^2\vartheta - 1)$.

^der-b9-2-6

> [!remark] Remark: What level B adds to the tides
> Level A defined the tidal force as the pull at a point minus the pull at the centre ([[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^def-a8-4-1|Def. §A8.4.1]]) and computed it on the Earth–Moon line: the near-side and far-side pulls differ by $4GMmR_E/r^3$ ([[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]], where $r$ is the distance written $R$ here), which is $2GMmR_E/r^3$ outward on each side, as in Theorem §B9.2.6 with $x = \pm R_E$. That subtraction is exactly the translational fictitious force of the freely falling Earth. The vector form adds the inward squeeze across the line, which is half as strong, and the quadrupole potential whose equipotentials give the two bulges.
> - *Rotation does not spoil it.* Earth's motion around the Earth–Moon centre of mass and around the Sun is a translation without rotation: every point of Earth has the same acceleration, so only the translational force enters. Earth's spin adds a centrifugal force that varies over the surface, and it is far larger than the tidal force (it raises a bulge of kilometres, not metres); but it turns with Earth, so it causes no sloshing and no tides (Morin §10.3, Remark 3).
> - *Moon versus Sun.* The tidal strength scales as $M/R^3$; the Sun's is about $0.45$ of the Moon's ([[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^ex-a8-4-1|Example §A8.4.1]]). Writing $M = \tfrac43\pi\rho r_M^3$, $M/R^3 \propto \rho\,(r_M/R)^3$ depends only on the density and the angular size, and Sun and Moon have nearly equal angular sizes. So the ratio $0.45$ says the Moon is about twice as dense as the Sun: a few weeks at the beach measure a ratio of densities (Morin §10.3, Remark 2).
> - The same tidal forces exert a torque on Earth's equatorial bulge, which makes Earth's axis precess: [[§B10.3 The Heavy Symmetric Top#^rem-b10-3-3|Remark in §B10.3]].

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Foucault's pair of equations is a pair of coupled linear oscillators with a velocity (gyroscopic) coupling; the complex combination $x + iy$ that decouples it is the same device as the normal coordinates of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-3|WO Theorem §B2.1.3]], here with complex modes $e^{\pm i\omega' t}e^{-i\Omega_z t}$.
> - The binomial expansion in the tidal derivation is a first-order Taylor expansion with a remainder of relative order $r/R$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).
> - Satellite rendezvous in the rotating frame of an orbiting station (H&S §9.5) is the same machinery with gravity varying across the frame; the linearized equations $\ddot x = 2\omega\dot y$, $\ddot y = 3\omega^2y - 2\omega\dot x$ (H&S eq. (9.63), $y$ radial) make a boost toward the station carry a spacecraft up and then backward, past it.
> - Electromagnetism: the tidal potential of [[§B9.2 Motion on the Rotating Earth#^thm-b9-2-6|Theorem §B9.2.6]], proportional to the second Legendre polynomial of the angle, is the second-order term of the Legendre expansion of the inverse distance that also gives the multipole expansion — [[§B4.1 Multipole Expansion of the Potential#^thm-b4-1-1|EM Theorem §B4.1.1]].
> - Quantum Mechanics level C: Foucault's pendulum (Theorem §B9.2.5) as the classical example of a slowly transported system whose plane comes back turned, the analogue of Berry's phase — [[§C9.4 Time-Dependent Perturbation Theory, Sudden and Adiabatic Limits#^rem-c9-4-4|QM Remark: Adiabatic, classically and thermally]].
