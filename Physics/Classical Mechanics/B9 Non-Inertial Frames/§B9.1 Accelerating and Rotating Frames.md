*Sources: Morin, Introduction to Classical Mechanics, §9.1, §10.1–10.2 · Helliwell & Sahakian, Modern Classical Mechanics, §9.1–9.3.*

Newton's laws hold in inertial frames ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]), and a frame that accelerates relative to one is not inertial ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]). Level A related two frames only when their axes do not rotate ([[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|Theorem §A2.8.1]], [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-2|Theorem §A2.8.2]]) and met the centrifugal and Coriolis forces as named effects ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-3|Def. §A3.6.3]], [[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^def-a3-9-2|Def. §A3.9.2]]). This section relates the time derivatives of a vector in an inertial frame and in a frame that both accelerates and rotates. Newton's second law, rewritten in the moving frame, then produces all four fictitious forces, each with its exact form.

## Frames with moving axes

> [!definition] Definition §B9.1.1: Moving Frame; Derivative Relative to It
> Let $S$ be an inertial frame. A **moving frame** $S'$ is an origin $O'$ at position $\vec R(t)$ in $S$, together with a right-handed orthonormal triad $\hat e_1(t), \hat e_2(t), \hat e_3(t)$ that may turn relative to $S$. Write a vector in the moving basis as $\vec A = \sum_i A_i\,\hat e_i$. The **rate of change of $\vec A$ relative to $S'$** is
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{d\vec A}{dt}\Bigr)_{\text{rot}} = \sum_i \frac{dA_i}{dt}\,\hat e_i ,
> $$
>
> what an observer who paints the axes $\hat e_i$ on her floor and walls measures. The plain derivative $d\vec A/dt = (d\vec A/dt)_{\text{in}}$ is always taken in the inertial frame $S$.
>
> *Source: Morin §10.1 (his $\delta\vec A/\delta t$) · H&S §9.3*

^def-b9-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.1: Angular Velocity of a Frame
> For a moving triad there is exactly one vector $\vec\omega(t)$, the **angular velocity of $S'$ relative to $S$**, with
>
> $$
> \frac{d\hat e_i}{dt} = \vec\omega\times\hat e_i \qquad (i = 1, 2, 3), \qquad \text{namely} \qquad \vec\omega = \tfrac12\sum_i \hat e_i\times\frac{d\hat e_i}{dt} .
> $$
>
> Consequently a point at rest in $S'$, at $\vec r = \sum_i x_i\hat e_i$ from $O'$ with constant $x_i$, moves relative to $O'$ with velocity $\vec\omega\times\vec r$. For a frame turning about a fixed axis $\hat z$ through the angle $\theta(t)$, $\vec\omega = \dot\theta\,\hat z$, the angular velocity vector of [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^def-a6-1-2|Def. §A6.1.2]].
>
> *Source: Morin §9.1 (Theorem 9.2), §10.1 · H&S §9.3, eq. (9.17)*

^thm-b9-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiating $\hat e_i\cdot\hat e_j = \delta_{ij}$ gives $a_{ij} + a_{ji} = 0$ for $a_{ij} = \hat e_i\cdot\dot{\hat e}_j$: the matrix $a$ is antisymmetric. Since the triad is a basis, $\dot{\hat e}_j = \sum_i a_{ij}\hat e_i$. With $\vec\omega$ as defined and $(\vec a\times\vec b)\times\vec c = \vec b(\vec a\cdot\vec c) - \vec a(\vec b\cdot\vec c)$,
>
> $$
> \vec\omega\times\hat e_j = \tfrac12\sum_i\bigl[\dot{\hat e}_i\,\delta_{ij} - \hat e_i\,(\dot{\hat e}_i\cdot\hat e_j)\bigr] = \tfrac12\Bigl[\dot{\hat e}_j - \sum_i a_{ji}\,\hat e_i\Bigr] = \tfrac12\Bigl[\dot{\hat e}_j + \sum_i a_{ij}\,\hat e_i\Bigr] = \dot{\hat e}_j .
> $$
>
> If $\vec\omega'$ also works, $(\vec\omega - \vec\omega')\times\hat e_j = \vec 0$ for all three $j$, so $\vec\omega - \vec\omega'$ is parallel to three independent directions and vanishes. For the fixed point, $\dot{\vec r} = \sum_i x_i\dot{\hat e}_i = \vec\omega\times\sum_i x_i\hat e_i$. For the rotation about $\hat z$, $\hat e_1 = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)$, $\hat e_2 = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)$, $\hat e_3 = \hat z$, and the formula gives $\vec\omega = \tfrac12(\dot\theta\hat z + \dot\theta\hat z + \vec 0) = \dot\theta\hat z$. Morin and H&S reach the same result geometrically, from the circle that the tip of a body-fixed vector describes about the axis; three independent antisymmetric entries $a_{ij}$ are exactly the three components of one vector, which is why a rotation in three dimensions is labelled by a vector (Morin §9.1, Remark 2).

^der-b9-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]] (product rule)

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.2: Time Derivative in a Rotating Frame
> For every vector $\vec A$,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{d\vec A}{dt}\Bigr)_{\text{in}} = \Bigl(\frac{d\vec A}{dt}\Bigr)_{\text{rot}} + \vec\omega\times\vec A, \qquad\text{as operators:}\qquad \Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\Bigr)_{\text{in}} = \Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\Bigr)_{\text{rot}} + \vec\omega\times .
> $$
>
> The total change is the change seen in the moving frame plus the change caused by the turning of its axes. In particular $(d\vec\omega/dt)_{\text{in}} = (d\vec\omega/dt)_{\text{rot}} \equiv \dot{\vec\omega}$, and a vector fixed in $S'$ changes at the rate $\vec\omega\times\vec A$.
>
> *Source: Morin §10.1, eq. (10.5); §9.5, eq. (9.41) · H&S §9.3, eq. (9.20)*

^thm-b9-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the product rule, $\dfrac{d}{dt}\sum_i A_i\hat e_i = \sum_i \dot A_i\,\hat e_i + \sum_i A_i\,\dot{\hat e}_i$. The first sum is $(d\vec A/dt)_{\text{rot}}$ by [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]]. By [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]] the second is $\sum_i A_i\,\vec\omega\times\hat e_i = \vec\omega\times\vec A$. For $\vec A = \vec\omega$ the extra term is $\vec\omega\times\vec\omega = \vec 0$.

^der-b9-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]], [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.3: Angular Velocities Add
> Let frames $S_1, S_2, S_3$ have a common origin, let $S_1$ turn with angular velocity $\vec\omega_{12}$ relative to $S_2$, and $S_2$ with $\vec\omega_{23}$ relative to $S_3$. Then $S_1$ turns relative to $S_3$ with
>
> $$
> \vec\omega_{13} = \vec\omega_{12} + \vec\omega_{23}
> $$
>
> at each instant. ($\vec\omega_{12}$, fixed in $S_2$, generally changes relative to $S_3$; the sum must be formed with the current values.)
>
> *Source: Morin §9.1, Theorem 9.3*

^thm-b9-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]] twice, to the pair $(S_3, S_2)$ and to the pair $(S_2, S_1)$ (the theorem holds with any frame in the role of $S$, since its proof never used that $S$ is inertial): for every $\vec A$, $(d\vec A/dt)_3 = (d\vec A/dt)_2 + \vec\omega_{23}\times\vec A = (d\vec A/dt)_1 + (\vec\omega_{12} + \vec\omega_{23})\times\vec A$. Taking $\vec A = \hat e_i$ fixed in $S_1$ gives $(d\hat e_i/dt)_3 = (\vec\omega_{12} + \vec\omega_{23})\times\hat e_i$, and by the uniqueness in [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]] this vector is $\vec\omega_{13}$.

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]], [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]]

## Velocity and acceleration in a moving frame

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.4: Velocity and Acceleration in a Moving Frame
> Let a particle be at $\vec r_{\text{in}} = \vec R + \vec r$, with $\vec r$ its position relative to $O'$, and let $\vec v = (d\vec r/dt)_{\text{rot}}$ and $\vec a = (d^2\vec r/dt^2)_{\text{rot}}$ be its velocity and acceleration as measured in $S'$. Then
>
> $$
> \vec v_{\text{in}} = \dot{\vec R} + \vec v + \vec\omega\times\vec r, \qquad
> \vec a_{\text{in}} = \ddot{\vec R} + \vec a + \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) + 2\,\vec\omega\times\vec v + \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r .
> $$
>
> For $\vec\omega = \vec 0$ these are the relative-motion rules [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|Theorem §A2.8.1]] and [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-2|Theorem §A2.8.2]]. The extra terms are the **centripetal** acceleration $\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r)$ of a point carried along by the frame, the **Coriolis** acceleration $2\vec\omega\times\vec v$, and the **azimuthal** (Euler) acceleration $\dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r$.
>
> *Source: Morin §10.1, eqs. (10.1), (10.8) · H&S §9.3, eq. (9.21)*

^thm-b9-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate $\vec r_{\text{in}} = \vec R + \vec r$ in $S$ and use [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]] on $\vec r$: $\vec v_{\text{in}} = \dot{\vec R} + \vec v + \vec\omega\times\vec r$. Differentiate again. The term $(d\vec v/dt)_{\text{in}} = \vec a + \vec\omega\times\vec v$, again by the theorem, and by the product rule
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}(\vec\omega\times\vec r) = \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r + \vec\omega\times\Bigl(\frac{d\vec r}{dt}\Bigr)_{\text{in}} = \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r + \vec\omega\times\vec v + \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) .
> $$
>
> Adding, $\vec a_{\text{in}} = \ddot{\vec R} + \vec a + 2\vec\omega\times\vec v + \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) + \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r$. One Coriolis term comes from differentiating $\vec v$, the other from differentiating $\vec\omega\times\vec r$: that is the origin of the factor 2. By [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]], $\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) = -\omega^2\vec r_\perp$, with $\vec r_\perp$ the part of $\vec r$ perpendicular to $\vec\omega$: of magnitude $\omega^2 r_\perp$ and pointing toward the axis, as in [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]].

^der-b9-1-4

## Newton's second law in a non-inertial frame

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.5: Fictitious Forces
> In the moving frame $S'$ a particle of mass $m$, acted on by real forces of resultant $\vec F$, obeys
>
> $$
> m\vec a = \vec F + \vec F_{\text{trans}} + \vec F_{\text{cf}} + \vec F_{\text{cor}} + \vec F_{\text{az}},
> $$
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{trans}} = -m\ddot{\vec R}, \qquad \vec F_{\text{cf}} = -m\,\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r), \qquad \vec F_{\text{cor}} = -2m\,\vec\omega\times\vec v, \qquad \vec F_{\text{az}} = -m\,\dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r .
> $$
>
> These are the **translational**, **centrifugal**, **Coriolis** and **azimuthal** (or **Euler**) forces. $\ddot{\vec R}$ and $\vec\omega$ are properties of the frame, $\vec r$ and $\vec v$ of the particle in that frame. An observer in $S'$ who adds them to the real forces gets the correct $m\vec a$ for her own measurements.
>
> The hypotheses are those of the derivation: $S$ is inertial, the real forces are interactions between bodies and so are the same in both frames ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-3|Def. §A3.6.3]]), and speeds are much less than $c$.
>
> *Source: Morin §10.2, eqs. (10.10)–(10.11) · H&S §9.1, eq. (9.5), §9.3, eq. (9.33)*

^thm-b9-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the inertial frame Newton's second law holds: $m\vec a_{\text{in}} = \vec F$ ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]). Insert $\vec a_{\text{in}}$ from [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-4|Theorem §B9.1.4]] and move every term except $m\vec a$ to the right-hand side.

^der-b9-1-5

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-4|Theorem §B9.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: From named effects to a theorem
> Level A introduced fictitious forces as a definition and a test (no third-law partner, [[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-3|Def. §A3.6.3]]) and described the centrifugal and Coriolis forces in words ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^rem-a3-9-2|Remark in §A3.9]], [[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^def-a3-9-2|Def. §A3.9.2]]). Here they are derived: [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]] is Newton's second law with some pieces of the acceleration moved to the other side of the equation. Nothing new is assumed and no new physics enters; every problem can be redone in the inertial frame, and the choice of frame is a matter of convenience (H&S §9.6). The translational force is the only one at level A that was already exact: it is the $-m\,d\vec u/dt$ of [[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^der-a3-2-2|the derivation of Theorem §A3.2.2]].

^rem-b9-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Signs, frames and the factor 2
> - $\vec\omega$ is the angular velocity of the *frame* relative to an inertial frame, not of the particle. $\vec r$, $\vec v$, $\vec a$ are measured in the moving frame, from its origin $O'$. The equations are vector equations, valid in any basis, but each vector must be the right one.
> - Choose a frame and stay in it. In the inertial frame there is a centripetal acceleration and no centrifugal force; in the rotating frame there is a centrifugal force and, for a body at rest there, no acceleration. Writing both is double counting ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^cau-a3-9-2|Caution in §A3.9]]).
> - The Coriolis term is $2m\vec\omega\times\vec v$, not $m\vec\omega\times\vec v$; and it is not a small correction in general (Example §B9.1.1).
> - Names differ: Morin's "azimuthal force" is H&S's "Euler force"; H&S call all of them "pseudoforces".

^cau-b9-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.1.6: Properties of the Rotational Fictitious Forces
> Let $\vec\omega$ be constant and let $\vec\rho$ be the perpendicular vector from the rotation axis (through $O'$) to the particle, $\rho = |\vec\omega\times\vec r|/\omega$.
> 1. The centrifugal force is $\vec F_{\text{cf}} = m\omega^2\vec\rho$, directly away from the axis. It is conservative, with potential energy $U_{\text{cf}} = -\tfrac12 m\,|\vec\omega\times\vec r|^2 = -\tfrac12 m\omega^2\rho^2$.
> 2. The Coriolis force is perpendicular to both $\vec v$ and $\vec\omega$, has magnitude $2m\omega v\sin\angle(\vec\omega, \vec v)$, and does no work in $S'$. For motion in the plane perpendicular to $\vec\omega$ it always deflects to the same side of the velocity: to the right if the frame turns counterclockwise.
> 3. Hence, if the real forces derive from a time-independent potential $U(\vec r)$, the quantity $\tfrac12 mv^2 + U + U_{\text{cf}}$, built from rotating-frame velocities, is conserved.
>
> *Source: Morin §10.2.2–10.2.3 · H&S §9.3, eqs. (9.34)–(9.36); Problem 9.25 (Coriolis does no work)*

^thm-b9-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) Split $\vec r = r_\parallel\hat\omega + \vec\rho$; then $\vec\omega\times\vec r = \vec\omega\times\vec\rho$ and $\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec\rho) = \vec\omega(\vec\omega\cdot\vec\rho) - \omega^2\vec\rho = -\omega^2\vec\rho$ ([[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]). With $|\vec\omega\times\vec r|^2 = \omega^2r^2 - (\vec\omega\cdot\vec r)^2$, $-\nabla U_{\text{cf}} = \tfrac12 m\nabla\bigl(\omega^2 r^2 - (\vec\omega\cdot\vec r)^2\bigr) = m\bigl(\omega^2\vec r - (\vec\omega\cdot\vec r)\vec\omega\bigr) = m\omega^2\vec\rho$, so $U_{\text{cf}}$ is a potential energy for it ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]]).
> (2) A cross product is perpendicular to both factors, so $\vec F_{\text{cor}}\cdot\vec v = 0$ and its power vanishes. In the plane perpendicular to $\vec\omega = \omega\hat z$ ($\omega > 0$), $-2m\omega\hat z\times\vec v$ is $\vec v$ turned clockwise by $90^\circ$ and scaled by $2m\omega$: to the right of the motion.
> (3) With $\dot{\vec\omega} = \vec 0$ and $\ddot{\vec R} = \vec 0$ (or the translational force included in $U$), dot [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]] with $\vec v$: $\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\tfrac12 mv^2\bigr) = m\vec a\cdot\vec v = (-\nabla U - \nabla U_{\text{cf}})\cdot\vec v + 0 = -\frac{d}{dt}(U + U_{\text{cf}})$.

^der-b9-1-6

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]], [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The Lagrangian in a rotating frame
> H&S obtain the same forces from a Lagrangian, written $\mathcal L$ here because $\vec L$ is angular momentum in this chapter. The Lagrangian is $\mathcal L = \tfrac12 mv_{\text{in}}^2 - U$; substituting $\vec v_{\text{in}} = \vec v + \vec\omega\times\vec r$ (with $\vec R$ fixed) gives, in rotating coordinates,
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = \tfrac12 m v^2 + m\,\vec v\cdot(\vec\omega\times\vec r) + \tfrac12 m\,|\vec\omega\times\vec r|^2 - U(\vec r),
> $$
>
> and Lagrange's equations ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]) return [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]] (H&S eq. (9.33)). Of the four terms, the second is a velocity-dependent potential and the third is $-U_{\text{cf}}$. The conserved quantity of Theorem §B9.1.6 (3) is the energy function of this $\mathcal L$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^def-b6-4-3|Def. §B6.4.3]], conserved by [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]]), not the inertial-frame energy. The term $m\,\vec v\cdot(\vec\omega\times\vec r)$ has the same structure as the magnetic term $q\,\vec v\cdot\vec A$ of a charge in a uniform field: the Coriolis force $-2m\vec\omega\times\vec v$ is the magnetic force $q\vec v\times\vec B$ ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|EM Principle §A7.1.1]]) with $q\vec B$ replaced by $2m\vec\omega$, and like it does no work ([[§A7.2 Charged Particles in a Magnetic Field#^thm-a7-2-1|EM Theorem §A7.2.1]]).

^rem-b9-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: A uniformly accelerating frame imitates gravity
> For $\vec\omega = \vec 0$ and constant $\ddot{\vec R} = \vec A$, the only fictitious force is $-m\vec A$, the same for every body per unit mass, exactly like a uniform gravitational field $\vec g_{\text{eff}} = -\vec A$. A pendulum in a spaceship accelerating at $A$ far from any planet swings with $\omega = \sqrt{A/\ell}$ (H&S Example 9.1), and a thrown ball follows a parabola. That no local experiment distinguishes the two is Einstein's equivalence principle, the starting point of general relativity (Relativity C2, planned).

^rem-b9-1-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B9.1.1: A Body at Rest, Seen from a Turntable
> A turntable turns counterclockwise at constant $\omega$ beneath a mass that is at rest in the lab, at radius $r$ (the turntable is frictionless). Account for its motion in the turntable frame.
>
> In the turntable frame the mass goes round a circle of radius $r$ clockwise, at speed $v = \omega r$: $\vec v = -\omega r\,\hat\varphi$. That needs a net inward force $m v^2/r = m\omega^2 r$. There is no real horizontal force. The fictitious forces of [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]] are
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{cf}} = m\omega^2 r\,\hat r, \qquad \vec F_{\text{cor}} = -2m\,\omega\hat z\times(-\omega r\,\hat\varphi) = 2m\omega^2 r\,(\hat z\times\hat\varphi) = -2m\omega^2 r\,\hat r ,
> $$
>
> and they add to $-m\omega^2 r\,\hat r$: inward, of exactly the required size. The Coriolis force here is twice the centrifugal force; it is negligible only when $v \ll \omega r$. H&S's ball thrown backward along the rim of a rotating space colony at the rim speed is the same situation (H&S Example 9.3).
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 10.5 · H&S Example 9.3*

^ex-b9-1-1

> [!example] Example §B9.1.2: A Mass Released on a Turntable
> A mass bolted at radius $a$ to a frictionless turntable turning counterclockwise at $\omega$ is released at $t = 0$. Find its path in the turntable frame.
>
> In the lab nothing horizontal acts, so the mass continues along the tangent at its speed $a\omega$: with the lab axes along the turntable's axes at $t = 0$, it is at $(a, a\omega t)$, at distance $a\sqrt{1 + \omega^2t^2}$ from the centre and at angle $\arctan(\omega t)$. The turntable axes have meanwhile turned through $\omega t$, so in polar coordinates $(r, \varphi)$ of the turntable frame
>
> $$
> r(t) = a\sqrt{1 + \omega^2 t^2}, \qquad \varphi(t) = -\bigl(\omega t - \arctan\omega t\bigr) .
> $$
>
> For large $t$, $r \approx a\omega t$ and $\varphi \approx -\omega t + \pi/2$: the mass recedes while the turntable turns away beneath it. In the rotating frame the path is an outward spiral that bends clockwise, to the right of its motion, as the Coriolis force of [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-6|Theorem §B9.1.6]] requires. Check at $t = 0$: the mass is at rest in the turntable frame, so only the centrifugal force acts and $\ddot r(0)$ should be $\omega^2 a$; indeed $\ddot r = a\omega^2/(1 + \omega^2t^2)^{3/2}$.
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 10.6*

Right: the same motion in the turntable frame, $r = a\sqrt{1 + \omega^2t^2}$, $\varphi = -(\omega t - \arctan\omega t)$; the curve starts radially outward, pushed by the centrifugal force, and the Coriolis force bends it to the right.*

> [!example] Example §B9.1.3: The Spinning Skater in Her Own Frame
> Model a skater as two hands of total mass $m$ on massless arms, pulled in radially at speed $v$ while she spins at $\omega(t)$. Use the frame of the vertical plane containing her arms, which turns with $\vec\omega = \omega(t)\hat z$.
>
> The hands stay in that plane by construction, so the net tangential force on them in her frame is zero. Radially inward motion, $\vec v = -v\hat r$, gives a Coriolis force $-2m\omega\hat z\times(-v\hat r) = 2m\omega v\,\hat\varphi$, along the spin, and the azimuthal force is $-m\dot\omega\hat z\times r\hat r = -mr\dot\omega\,\hat\varphi$, against it (no real tangential force acts if the arms are pulled in along themselves). Their sum vanishes:
>
> $$
> 2m\omega v = m r\dot\omega .
> $$
>
> In the lab this is conservation of angular momentum ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-4|Theorem §A6.6.4]]): $\frac{d}{dt}(mr^2\omega) = 2mr\dot r\omega + mr^2\dot\omega = 0$ with $\dot r = -v$ gives the same relation. In the rotating frame the speed-up is "caused" by the Coriolis force; in the lab it is the constancy of $mr^2\omega$.
>
> *Source: Morin §10.2.4 (Example: spinning ice skater)*
