From Tycho Brahe's naked-eye observations of the planets, Kepler extracted three empirical rules about the shape of orbits, the speed along them and their periods. Newton showed that all three follow from the inverse-square law ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]]) together with the second law. That was the first great test of his theory. This section states the three laws as consequences of gravitation and extends the circular-orbit results of [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits]] to ellipses.

## Ellipses and conic sections

> [!definition] Definition §A8.3.1: Ellipse, Semi-Major Axis, Eccentricity
> An **ellipse** is the set of points whose distances to two fixed points, the **foci**, add up to a constant. Its longest diameter is the **major axis**, and half of it is the **semi-major axis** $a$. In polar coordinates centred on one focus, with $\theta$ measured from the direction of closest approach,
>
> $$
> r(\theta) = \frac{\alpha}{1 + e\cos\theta}, \qquad 0 \le e < 1,
> $$
>
> where $e$ is the **eccentricity** ($e = 0$ is a circle; $e$ near 1 is long and thin). For an orbit about the Sun the nearest point, at distance $r_p = \alpha/(1+e)$, is the **perihelion**, and the farthest, at $r_a = \alpha/(1-e)$, the **aphelion** (perigee and apogee for orbits about Earth). The semi-major axis is their average:
>
> $$
> a = \tfrac12\,(r_p + r_a).
> $$
>
> The same equation with $e = 1$ is a parabola and with $e > 1$ a hyperbola; together with the circle and ellipse these are the **conic sections**.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.5, eq. (13.10)*

^def-a8-3-1

## Kepler's first law

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.3.1: Kepler's First Law (Orbits Are Conic Sections)
> A mass $m \ll M$ moving under the gravity of $M$ alone follows a conic section with $M$ at a focus. Which conic is fixed by the total energy $E$ (with the angular momentum fixing $\alpha$ and $e$):
>
> | Total energy | Eccentricity | Orbit |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | $E < 0$ | $0 \le e < 1$ | circle or ellipse: bound, closed |
> | $E = 0$ | $e = 1$ | parabola: unbound, just escapes |
> | $E > 0$ | $e > 1$ | hyperbola: unbound, passes once |
>
> In particular, **every planet moves on an ellipse with the Sun at one focus.**
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.5*

^thm-a8-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> University Physics states this without proof; the derivation solves Newton's second law in polar coordinates, using the conservation of energy and of angular momentum, and belongs to Chapter B2 (planned). Two parts can be checked here. A circle ($e = 0$, $r = \alpha$) is a solution, with the speed of [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]]. And the energy classification matches [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-3|Theorem §A8.2.3]]: orbits with $E < 0$ stay within a finite distance, as the closed curves (circle, ellipse) do, while those with $E \ge 0$ go off to infinity, as parabolas and hyperbolas do.

^der-a8-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]], [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-3|Theorem §A8.2.3]], [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]]

Red: the parabola of a body with exactly the escape speed. Green: a hyperbola, for a body faster than escape speed. The faster the body at perihelion, the more open the orbit.*

> [!remark] Remark: Two bodies, one focus
> Strictly, $M$ is not fixed: both bodies orbit their common centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass]]). Each traces a conic of the same shape, with the centre of mass at the shared focus, and the sizes of the two paths are in the inverse ratio of the masses. For a planet and the Sun the Sun's path is tiny, and "the Sun at a focus" is an excellent approximation.

## Kepler's second law

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.3.2: Kepler's Second Law (Equal Areas)
> The line from $M$ to $m$ sweeps out equal areas in equal times. The **areal velocity** is constant:
>
> $$
> \frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2m},
> $$
>
> where $L = \lvert\vec r \times m\vec v\rvert$ is the angular momentum of $m$ about $M$, which is conserved. At perihelion and aphelion the velocity is perpendicular to $\vec r$, so
>
> $$
> r_p\, v_p = r_a\, v_a : \qquad \frac{v_p}{v_a} = \frac{r_a}{r_p}.
> $$
>
> The planet is fastest at perihelion and slowest at aphelion.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.5*

^thm-a8-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take $M$ as origin. The gravitational force on $m$ points along $-\vec r$, so its torque about $M$ is $\vec\tau = \vec r \times \vec F = \vec 0$, and the angular momentum $\vec L = \vec r \times m\vec v$ is constant ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation]]). In a time $dt$ the position vector moves by $\vec v\,dt$ and sweeps a thin triangle of area
>
> $$
> dA = \tfrac12\,\lvert\vec r \times \vec v\,dt\rvert = \frac{\lvert\vec r \times m\vec v\rvert}{2m}\,dt = \frac{L}{2m}\,dt .
> $$
>
> Only the velocity component perpendicular to $\vec r$ contributes, since the cross product kills the radial part. At perihelion and aphelion $r$ is a minimum or maximum, so $dr/dt = 0$: the velocity has no radial component there and $L = m r v$. Equating $L$ at the two points gives $r_p v_p = r_a v_a$.

^der-a8-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation|§A6.6]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors|§A1.5]] (cross product)

![[ph-cm-a8-3-1.svg]]
*An orbit of eccentricity 0.6 with the Sun (orange) at one focus. The two red sectors are swept in equal times and have equal areas: the short, wide one near perihelion and the long, thin one near aphelion. The velocity arrows are in the ratio $v_p/v_a = r_a/r_p = 4$.*

> [!caution] Caution: What the second law needs
> Equal areas follow from the force being **central** (along the line to $M$), not from its $1/r^2$ form: any central force conserves angular momentum. The first and third laws, by contrast, depend on the inverse square.

^cau-a8-3-1

## Energy of an elliptical orbit

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.3.3: Energy of an Elliptical Orbit
> A body on an ellipse of semi-major axis $a$ about $M$ has total energy
>
> $$
> E = -\frac{GMm}{2a},
> $$
>
> the circular-orbit result ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-5|Theorem §A8.2.5]]) with $r$ replaced by $a$. Consequently its speed at distance $r$ is
>
> $$
> v^2 = GM\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right).
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.5 (stated without proof)*

^thm-a8-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> University Physics states the energy without proof; it follows from the two conservation laws applied at the two ends of the major axis. By [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]], $v_a = v_p\, r_p/r_a$. Energy conservation ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-2|Theorem §A8.2.2]]) between perihelion and aphelion reads
>
> $$
> \tfrac12 v_p^2\left(1 - \frac{r_p^2}{r_a^2}\right) = GM\left(\frac{1}{r_p} - \frac{1}{r_a}\right)
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> \tfrac12 v_p^2\,\frac{(r_a - r_p)(r_a + r_p)}{r_a^2} = GM\,\frac{r_a - r_p}{r_p r_a},
> $$
>
> so $v_p^2 = \dfrac{2GM\,r_a}{r_p\,(r_a + r_p)} = \dfrac{GM}{a}\,\dfrac{r_a}{r_p}$, using $r_a + r_p = 2a$. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{E}{m} = \tfrac12 v_p^2 - \frac{GM}{r_p} = \frac{GM}{r_p}\,\frac{r_a - 2a}{2a} = -\frac{GM}{2a},
> $$
>
> since $r_a - 2a = -r_p$. Writing $E = \tfrac12 mv^2 - GMm/r$ at any point and solving for $v^2$ gives the speed formula.

^der-a8-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]], [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-2|Theorem §A8.2.2]], [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^def-a8-3-1|Def. §A8.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Changing orbits (Hohmann transfer)
> To move a spacecraft from a circular orbit of radius $r_1$ to a larger one of radius $r_2$ with the least energy, use a transfer ellipse with perihelion $r_1$ and aphelion $r_2$, so $a = \tfrac12(r_1 + r_2)$. A short forward boost at $r_1$ raises the speed from $\sqrt{GM/r_1}$ to the ellipse's perihelion speed $\sqrt{GM(2/r_1 - 1/a)}$; half an orbit later a second boost at $r_2$ raises the speed from the aphelion speed to $\sqrt{GM/r_2}$. Without it the craft would simply fall back along the ellipse. The trip takes half the period of the transfer ellipse, and the target planet has to arrive at the aphelion at the same moment, so for Earth to Mars launch opportunities come about every two years. The real calculation must also include the gravity of the two planets.

^rem-a8-3-2

## Kepler's third law

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.3.4: Kepler's Third Law
> The square of the period of an orbit is proportional to the cube of its semi-major axis:
>
> $$
> T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\,a^3 .
> $$
>
> The constant depends only on the central mass $M$, so it is the same for all planets of the Sun (and, with a different value, for all satellites of Earth).
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.5, eq. (13.11)*

^thm-a8-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Circular orbit:* square $T = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/GM}$ ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]]) with $a = r$. University Physics stops here and replaces $r$ by $a$.
>
> *Ellipse:* the areal velocity $L/(2m)$ is constant ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]]), so in one period the whole ellipse is swept: $T = \pi a b \big/ \tfrac{L}{2m}$, where $\pi ab$ is the area of an ellipse with semi-minor axis $b$. For an ellipse $b^2 = a^2 - (a - r_p)^2 = r_p(2a - r_p) = r_p r_a$. From the derivation of [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-3|Theorem §A8.3.3]], $L/m = r_p v_p = \sqrt{r_p^2\,\frac{GM}{a}\frac{r_a}{r_p}} = b\sqrt{GM/a}$. Hence
>
> $$
> T = \frac{2\pi a b}{L/m} = \frac{2\pi a b}{b\sqrt{GM/a}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM}} .
> $$

^der-a8-3-4

*Uses:* [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]], [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]], [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-3|Theorem §A8.3.3]], [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^def-a8-3-1|Def. §A8.3.1]]

> [!example] Example §A8.3.1: Halley's Comet
> Halley's comet returns to perihelion every 75.3 years, and its perihelion is 0.586 AU from the Sun ($1\ \mathrm{AU} = 1.50 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm m$, the mean radius of Earth's orbit). Solve [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-4|Theorem §A8.3.4]] for $a$, with $M_{\text{Sun}} = 2.00 \times 10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $T = 75.3 \times 3.156 \times 10^7\ \mathrm s = 2.37 \times 10^9\ \mathrm s$:
>
> $$
> a = \left(\frac{GM_{\text{Sun}}\,T^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} = 2.67 \times 10^{12}\ \mathrm m = 17.8\ \mathrm{AU}.
> $$
>
> Then $r_a = 2a - r_p = 35.6\ \mathrm{AU} - 0.586\ \mathrm{AU} = 35.0\ \mathrm{AU}$. By the second law the comet moves $r_a/r_p \approx 60$ times faster at perihelion than at aphelion, which is why it spends only a short time near the Sun.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 13.13*

^ex-a8-3-1

> [!example] Example §A8.3.2: Weighing Earth with the Moon
> The third law turns any orbit into a scale for the central mass. The Moon's mean orbital radius is $3.84 \times 10^8\ \mathrm m$ and its period $27.3\ \mathrm d = 2.36 \times 10^6\ \mathrm s$:
>
> $$
> M_E = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{G T^2} = \frac{4\pi^2 (3.84 \times 10^8\ \mathrm m)^3}{(6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2})(2.36 \times 10^6\ \mathrm s)^2} = 6.02 \times 10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg},
> $$
>
> within 1% of the value $5.96 \times 10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg}$ found from $g$ in [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^ex-a8-1-2|Example §A8.1.2]]. The residual reflects averaging: the Moon's distance varies by about 10% over its elliptical orbit, and Earth is only 81 times heavier than the Moon, so both circle their common centre of mass. The same method, applied to stars near the centre of the Milky Way, reveals a black hole there ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 13.10*
