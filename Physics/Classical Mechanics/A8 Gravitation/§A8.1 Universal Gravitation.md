Up to now the weight $m\vec g$ was simply one of the forces on a free-body diagram, with $g$ a measured constant. This section names its source: every mass attracts every other mass. One force law explains both an apple falling and the Moon circling, fixes $g$ in terms of Earth's mass and radius, and says how gravity changes with height, with latitude and below the surface.

## The law

> [!principle] Principle §A8.1.1: Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation
> Any two point masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ a distance $r$ apart attract each other. The force on $m_1$ exerted by $m_2$ is
>
> $$
> \vec F_{12} = \frac{G m_1 m_2}{r^2}\,\hat r_{12},
> $$
>
> where $\hat r_{12}$ is the unit vector pointing from $m_1$ toward $m_2$. The force on $m_2$ is $\vec F_{21} = -\vec F_{12}$. The **universal gravitational constant** is
>
> $$
> G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2},
> $$
>
> the same for all materials and everywhere in the Universe.
>
> *Domain: point masses, and spherically symmetric bodies with $r$ the distance between their centres ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|Theorem §A8.1.5]]); other bodies approximately, when their separation is large compared with their size. Gravity acts instantly at a distance and fields are weak; general relativity replaces it where these fail ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton]]).*
> *Source: UP1 §13.1, eq. (13.1)*

^pr-a8-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the law
> - The force is attractive and acts along the line joining the two (centres of) masses. The two forces form a third-law pair ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law]]): equal magnitudes, opposite directions, even when the masses are very different.
> - The $1/r^2$ is geometry: the influence of a point source spreads over spheres whose area grows as $r^2$. The same form returns in Coulomb's law ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|EM Principle §A1.2.2]]).
> - $G$ is tiny. Two 1.0-kg masses 1.0 m apart attract with $6.7 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm N$, about the weight of a grain of pollen. Gravity is the weakest of the fundamental forces; it dominates astronomy only because it is always attractive and the masses there are enormous.
> - Cavendish measured $G$ in 1798 with a torsion balance: two small spheres on a suspended rod are attracted by two large fixed spheres, and the twist of the wire gives the force. Once $G$ was known, Earth's mass followed ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^ex-a8-1-2|Example §A8.1.2]]).

^rem-a8-1-1

> [!example] Example §A8.1.1: Newton's Moon Test
> The Moon orbits at $r_M = 3.84 \times 10^8\ \mathrm m \approx 60\,R_E$ from Earth's centre with period $T = 27.3\ \mathrm d = 2.36 \times 10^6\ \mathrm s$. If one force law governs both, the Moon's acceleration should be $g$ diluted by $(R_E/r_M)^2 \approx 1/60^2$.
>
> Treating the orbit as uniform circular motion ([[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-2|Theorem §A2.7.2]]),
>
> $$
> a_M = \frac{4\pi^2 r_M}{T^2} = \frac{4\pi^2 (3.84 \times 10^8\ \mathrm m)}{(2.36 \times 10^6\ \mathrm s)^2} = 2.72 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m/s^2},
> \qquad
> \frac{g}{60^2} = \frac{9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2}}{3600} = 2.72 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.
> $$
>
> The fall of an apple and the orbit of the Moon are the same phenomenon. This comparison is what led Newton to the inverse-square law.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.1 (Newton's argument); data from UP1 Example 13.10*

^ex-a8-1-1

## Gravity at and above Earth's surface

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.1.2: Free-Fall Acceleration from the Law of Gravitation
> At distance $r \ge R_E$ from Earth's centre, every body falls with acceleration of magnitude
>
> $$
> g = \frac{G M_E}{r^2}, \qquad\text{at the surface}\quad g = \frac{G M_E}{R_E^2}.
> $$
>
> It does not depend on the falling body's mass.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.2, eq. (13.2)*

^thm-a8-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The weight of a body of mass $m$ is the gravitational force of Earth on it ([[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^def-a3-4-1|Def. §A3.4.1]]), and if it is the only force, the second law gives it the acceleration $g$ with $w = mg$ ([[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]). Treat Earth as a sphere of mass $M_E$ ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|Theorem §A8.1.5]]) and set the magnitude from [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]] equal to $mg$: $mg = G m M_E / r^2$. The mass $m$ cancels. (Earth also accelerates toward the body, but with $m/M_E$ times the body's acceleration, which is negligible.)

^der-a8-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]], [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|Theorem §A8.1.5]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^def-a3-4-1|Def. §A3.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why all bodies fall alike
> This is the explanation of the universality of free fall, which kinematics took as an experimental fact ([[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^law-a2-3-2|Law §A2.3.2]]). The cancellation of $m$ uses the same $m$ twice: once as the measure of inertia in $\vec F = m\vec a$ and once as the "gravitational charge" in the force law. Newton's theory takes their equality as an experimental fact and does not explain it. Einstein made it the starting point of general relativity ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton]]).

^rem-a8-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §A8.1.1: Gravitational Field
> The **gravitational field** of a mass $M$ is the force per unit mass it exerts on a test mass $m$ placed at a point, $\vec g = \vec F/m$. For a point mass or a spherically symmetric body, at distance $r$ from its centre,
>
> $$
> \vec g = -\frac{G M}{r^2}\,\hat r ,
> $$
>
> with $\hat r$ the unit vector pointing away from the centre of $M$. A mass $m$ placed in the field feels the force $m\vec g$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.2*

^def-a8-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Field lines
> The field can be drawn as lines pointing radially inward, evenly spread in all directions. The direction of $\vec g$ is along the lines, and its strength is the number of lines per unit area. At distance $r$ that is the fixed total number of lines divided by the area $4\pi r^2$, so the picture builds in the inverse-square law. The field idea (mass $m$ responds to the field of $M$ where it is, instead of to $M$ directly) is used throughout electromagnetism.

^rem-a8-1-3

> [!model] Model §A8.1.3: Uniform Field near the Surface
> Over heights $h \ll R_E$ above Earth's surface and horizontal distances $\ll R_E$, Earth's field ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-1|Def. §A8.1.1]]) is taken to be uniform, with the surface value of [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]]:
>
> $$
> \vec g = -g\,\hat\jmath, \qquad g = \frac{G M_E}{R_E^2} = 9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2},
> $$
>
> with $\hat\jmath$ pointing up, away from Earth's centre. This is the field behind the constant $g$ of the earlier chapters. It explains the universality of free fall ([[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^law-a2-3-2|Law §A2.3.2]]); motion in it is treated by the free-fall model ([[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^mod-a2-3-3|Model §A2.3.3]], the home of near-surface kinematics) and the projectile model ([[§A2.6 Projectile Motion#^mod-a2-6-1|Model §A2.6.1]]). Its potential energy is $mgy$ ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-1|Theorem §A4.4.1]]).
>
> - *Assumes:* $h \ll R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6\ \mathrm m$; a region small enough that "down" has one direction (the directions to Earth's centre from two points a distance $d$ apart differ by $d/R_E$ radians); Earth's rotation ignored ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-4|Theorem §A8.1.4]]).
> - *Fails:* by [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]], at height $h$ the field is $g(h) = GM_E/(R_E + h)^2 = g\,(1 + h/R_E)^{-2} \approx g\,(1 - 2h/R_E)$. The relative drop $2h/R_E$ reaches 1% at $h \approx 32\ \mathrm{km}$; at the height of the International Space Station, 400 km, $g = 8.67\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, 11.5% less ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^ex-a8-1-2|Example §A8.1.2]]). Over such distances use [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]], and for energies [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]].
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.2, Example 13.4*

^mod-a8-1-3

> [!example] Example §A8.1.2: Weighing Earth
> Earth cannot be put on a scale, but $g$ and $R_E$ were measured long ago. Solving [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]] at the surface for the mass,
>
> $$
> M_E = \frac{g R_E^2}{G} = \frac{(9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2})(6.37 \times 10^6\ \mathrm m)^2}{6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m^2/kg^2}} = 5.96 \times 10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg}.
> $$
>
> At the orbit of the International Space Station, $r = R_E + 400\ \mathrm{km} = 6.77 \times 10^6\ \mathrm m$,
>
> $$
> g = \frac{G M_E}{r^2} = \frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.96 \times 10^{24})}{(6.77 \times 10^6)^2}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} = 8.67\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.
> $$
>
> The astronauts keep 88% of their weight. They float because they and the station are in free fall together ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^rem-a8-2-2|the remark on weightlessness]]), not because gravity is absent.
>
> *Source: UP1 Examples 13.3(a), 13.4*

^ex-a8-1-2

## Apparent weight on the rotating Earth

> [!definition] Definition §A8.1.2: Apparent Weight
> The **apparent weight** of a body is the force $F_s$ that a scale (or other support) must exert to hold it; it is what the scale reads. It equals the true weight $mg$ only if the body is not accelerating (the scale in an accelerating elevator: [[§A3.7 Free-Body Diagrams and Problem Solving#^ex-a3-7-2|Example §A3.7.2]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.2*

^def-a8-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.1.4: Apparent Weight at the Equator
> Earth turns once a day, $\omega = 7.27 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. A body at rest on a scale at the equator has apparent weight
>
> $$
> F_s = m\left(g - R_E\,\omega^2\right),
> $$
>
> smaller than $mg$ by $R_E\omega^2 = 0.0337\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, or 0.34% of $g$. At the poles $F_s = mg$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.2, eq. (13.3)*

^thm-a8-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The body moves on a circle of radius $R_E$ at angular speed $\omega$, so it has a centripetal acceleration $R_E\omega^2$ toward Earth's centre ([[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]]). Take the outward direction as positive. The forces are the scale's push $F_s$ outward and the weight $mg$ inward, so the second law gives $F_s - mg = -m R_E \omega^2$. At a pole the body sits on the axis, the circle shrinks to a point and $F_s = mg$. With $\omega = 2\pi/(24 \times 3600\ \mathrm s) = 7.27 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, $R_E\omega^2 = (6.37 \times 10^6\ \mathrm m)(7.27 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})^2 = 0.0337\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.

^der-a8-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]], [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]], [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-2|Def. §A8.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Away from the equator
> At latitude $\lambda$ the body circles the axis at radius $R_E\cos\lambda$, so the centripetal acceleration is smaller and points at the axis, not at Earth's centre. The supporting force then has to tilt slightly off the radial line so that its sum with $m\vec g$ points at the axis. A plumb line hangs along this tilted direction, and buildings are built along it. Earth's equatorial bulge (radius about 30 km larger at the equator, itself a result of rotation) lowers $g$ at the equator by a comparable amount in the same direction.

^rem-a8-1-4

> [!example] Example §A8.1.3: A Day Short Enough to Float
> How fast would Earth have to spin for a person at the equator to read zero on a scale? Set $F_s = 0$ in [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-4|Theorem §A8.1.4]]: gravity alone must supply the centripetal acceleration, $g = v^2/R_E$. Then
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{g R_E} = \sqrt{(9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2})(6.37 \times 10^6\ \mathrm m)} = 7.90 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{m/s},
> \qquad
> T = \frac{2\pi R_E}{v} = 5.07 \times 10^3\ \mathrm s \approx 84\ \mathrm{min}.
> $$
>
> This is exactly the speed and period of a satellite skimming Earth's surface ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]]): a weightless person at the equator would be in orbit.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 13.5*

^ex-a8-1-3

## Gravity below the surface

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.1.5: Shell Theorem
> For a spherically symmetric distribution of mass, the field at distance $r$ from the centre is that of a point mass equal to the mass $M(r)$ inside radius $r$, placed at the centre:
>
> $$
> g(r) = \frac{G\,M(r)}{r^2}.
> $$
>
> Mass outside radius $r$ has no net effect. In particular, outside the body ($r \ge R$) the whole mass acts as if concentrated at the centre.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.1, §13.2 (stated, as a consequence of Gauss's law)*

^thm-a8-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Sketch.* University Physics states this result without proof at this point. The idea uses the field-line picture of [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^rem-a8-1-3|the remark on field lines]]: field lines end only on mass, and the number ending inside a closed surface is proportional to the mass enclosed. This is **Gauss's law** for gravity: the total flux of $\vec g$ out of a closed surface equals $-4\pi G$ times the enclosed mass. For a spherical surface of radius $r$ concentric with a symmetric distribution, $\vec g$ points radially inward with the same magnitude at every point, so the flux is $-g(r)\cdot 4\pi r^2$. Hence $g(r)\,4\pi r^2 = 4\pi G M(r)$. Lines from mass outside the sphere that enter it also leave it, so they contribute nothing. Gauss's law itself is developed for the electric field in Electromagnetism ([[§A2.2 Gauss's Law#^thm-a2-2-2|EM Theorem §A2.2.2]]).

^der-a8-1-5

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^def-a8-1-1|Def. §A8.1.1]], [[§18 Surface Integrals#^thm-18-2|452 Thm. §18.2]] (divergence theorem)

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.1.6: Gravity inside a Uniform Sphere
> Inside a sphere of uniform density $\rho$ and radius $R$, the field strength grows linearly from the centre:
>
> $$
> g(r) = \frac{4}{3}\pi G \rho\, r = g_{\text{surface}}\,\frac{r}{R} \qquad (r \le R).
> $$
>
> At the centre $g = 0$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.2*

^thm-a8-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The mass inside radius $r$ is $M(r) = \rho \cdot \frac43\pi r^3$. By [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|Theorem §A8.1.5]], $g(r) = G M(r)/r^2 = \frac43\pi G\rho\, r$. At $r = R$ this is $g_{\text{surface}}$, so $g(r) = g_{\text{surface}}\, r/R$.

^der-a8-1-6

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|Theorem §A8.1.5]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-2|Def. §A9.1.2]] (density)

![[ph-cm-a8-1-1.svg]]
*Field strength of a uniform-density planet against distance from its centre. Blue: inside, $g$ grows linearly from zero at the centre. Red: outside, $g$ falls off as $1/r^2$. The two meet at the surface. Earth is denser toward its core, so its real profile rises faster inside than the blue line.*

> [!caution] Caution: Which formula where
> $g = GM/r^2$ with the full mass $M$ holds only for $r \ge R$. Below the surface only the enclosed mass counts, and the field decreases toward the centre instead of growing without bound. Inside a hollow spherical shell there is no enclosed mass, so the shell exerts no net force at all on anything inside it.
