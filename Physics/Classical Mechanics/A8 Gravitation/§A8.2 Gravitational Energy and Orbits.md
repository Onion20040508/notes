The potential energy $mgy$ of [[§A4.4 Potential Energy]] assumes a constant $g$, which fails over planetary distances ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^mod-a8-1-3|Model §A8.1.3]]). This section computes the potential energy of the full inverse-square force and uses energy conservation to answer the questions of space travel: how fast must a rocket go to escape, when is a body bound, and what speed, period and energy does a circular orbit have?

## Potential energy of the inverse-square force

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.2.1: Gravitational Potential Energy
> The potential energy of a mass $m$ at distance $r$ from the centre of a spherically symmetric mass $M$, with the zero chosen at infinite separation, is
>
> $$
> U(r) = -\frac{G M m}{r} .
> $$
>
> Between two distances, $\Delta U = U(r_2) - U(r_1) = G M m\left(\dfrac{1}{r_1} - \dfrac{1}{r_2}\right)$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.3, eq. (13.4)*

^thm-a8-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Gravity is conservative: its magnitude and direction depend only on position ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces]]). So $\Delta U = -\int \vec F\cdot d\vec r$ may be computed along any path from $r_1$ to $r_2$. Go radially outward from $r_1$ to $r_2$, then along a circular arc of radius $r_2$ to the final point. On the arc $\vec F \perp d\vec r$, so no work is done. On the radial part, $\vec F = -\dfrac{GMm}{r^2}\hat r$ and $d\vec r = dr\,\hat r$, so
>
> $$
> \Delta U = -\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\left(-\frac{GMm}{r^2}\right)dr = GMm\left[-\frac1r\right]_{r_1}^{r_2} = GMm\left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right).
> $$
>
> Letting $r_2 \to \infty$ (the integral converges) and putting $U(\infty) = 0$ gives $U(r_1) = -GMm/r_1$.

^der-a8-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]], [[§A4.4 Potential Energy|§A4.4]], [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces|§A4.5]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|Theorem §34.1]] (FTC), [[§36 Improper Integrals#^ex-36-2|Example §36.2]] ($\int_1^\infty x^{-p}dx$ converges for $p>1$)

> [!remark] Remark: The old formula as a limit
> Lifting $m$ from the surface ($r_1 = R$) to height $h$ ($r_2 = R + h$) and using $g = GM/R^2$,
>
> $$
> \Delta U = GMm\,\frac{h}{R(R+h)} = \frac{mgh}{1 + h/R} \;\approx\; mgh \qquad (h \ll R).
> $$
>
> So $mgh$ is the near-surface form of the same result, and it overestimates the work by the factor $1 + h/R$.

^rem-a8-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Signs and reference points
> - $U$ is negative everywhere and rises toward zero as the masses separate: pulling them apart takes positive work, so $U$ increases. Masses left alone fall toward lower (more negative) $U$.
> - The zero at infinity is a convenience; only differences of $U$ are physical. Do not mix $-GMm/r$ and $mgy$ in one energy equation, since they use different zeros.

^cau-a8-2-1

> [!example] Example §A8.2.1: Lifting a Payload to the Space Station
> How much energy does it take just to raise a $9000\ \mathrm{kg}$ Soyuz capsule from Earth's surface to the height of the International Space Station, 400 km? With $M_E = 5.96 \times 10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6\ \mathrm m$, [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]] gives
>
> $$
> \Delta U = GM_E m\left(\frac{1}{R_E} - \frac{1}{R_E + 4.00 \times 10^5\ \mathrm m}\right) = 3.32 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm J.
> $$
>
> The near-surface formula would give $mgh = 3.53 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm J$, 6% too high, because $g$ has dropped to $8.67\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ at that height. This is only the lifting; staying up there costs far more ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^ex-a8-2-3|Example §A8.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 13.6*

^ex-a8-2-1

## Conservation of energy, escape and binding

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.2.2: Energy Conservation under Gravity
> A mass $m \ll M$ moving under the gravity of $M$ alone (and no other force doing work) conserves
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac12 m v^2 - \frac{GMm}{r}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \tfrac12 m v_1^2 - \frac{GMm}{r_1} = \tfrac12 m v_2^2 - \frac{GMm}{r_2},
> $$
>
> whatever the direction of the velocity.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.3, eq. (13.5)*

^thm-a8-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Gravity is conservative with potential energy $U(r) = -GMm/r$ ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]]). If it is the only force doing work, mechanical energy $K + U$ is conserved ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy]]). Treating $M$ as fixed requires $m \ll M$; otherwise both kinetic energies must be kept, and momentum conservation relates the two velocities. Energy is a scalar, so the direction of $\vec v$ never enters.

^der-a8-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]], [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy|§A4.6]]

> [!definition] Definition §A8.2.1: Escape Speed
> The **escape speed** from distance $R$ of a body of mass $M$ is the smallest launch speed with which an object reaches infinite distance and never returns (no air resistance). More generally, it is the speed at which the object's total energy is zero.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.3*

^def-a8-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A8.2.2: Gravitationally Bound
> A mass $m$ is **gravitationally bound** to $M$ if it can never get farther than some finite distance from $M$; otherwise it is **unbound**.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.3*

^def-a8-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.2.3: Escape Speed and the Energy Criterion for Binding
> (a) The escape speed from distance $R$ is
>
> $$
> v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} ,
> $$
>
> the same for every escaping mass. (b) An object is bound exactly when its total energy is negative, $E < 0$; it then never gets farther than $r_{\max} = GMm/\lvert E\rvert$. If $E \ge 0$ it escapes.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.3, eq. (13.6)*

^thm-a8-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (b) Since $K = E - U(r) \ge 0$, the object can only be where $U(r) \le E$. If $E < 0$ this means $-GMm/r \le E = -\lvert E\rvert$, i.e. $r \le GMm/\lvert E\rvert$: a finite limit, at which the kinetic energy is zero. If $E \ge 0$, then $U(r) < 0 \le E$ for every $r$, so nothing turns the object back; as $r \to \infty$, $U \to 0$ and the kinetic energy tends to $E \ge 0$.
>
> (a) The marginal case is $E = 0$: the object arrives at infinity with zero speed. Setting $\tfrac12 m v_{\text{esc}}^2 - GMm/R = 0$ gives $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2GM/R}$; the mass $m$ cancels.

^der-a8-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-2|Theorem §A8.2.2]], [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^def-a8-2-1|Def. §A8.2.1]], [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^def-a8-2-2|Def. §A8.2.2]], [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability|§A4.7]]

![[ph-cm-a8-2-1.svg]]
*Energy diagram for $U(r) = -GMm/r$ (blue). With total energy $E < 0$ (red) the object can only be where $U \le E$: the kinetic energy $K$ is the gap between the red line and the blue curve, and it vanishes at the turning point $r_{\max}$. With $E = 0$ (the axis) the object just reaches infinity; with $E > 0$ (green) it gets there with speed to spare.*

> [!caution] Caution: Real bodies have size
> These results treat the masses as points (or spheres that never touch). If $r$ would drop below the sum of the radii, the bodies collide, whatever the sign of $E$. And a bound orbit need not fall in: with a sideways velocity the object can circle $M$ forever ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]]).

^cau-a8-2-2

> [!example] Example §A8.2.2: Escaping Earth, and Escaping the Sun
> From Earth's surface ($M_E = 5.96 \times 10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6\ \mathrm m$),
>
> $$
> v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.96 \times 10^{24})}{6.37 \times 10^6}}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 1.12 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 11\ \mathrm{km/s}.
> $$
>
> To leave the solar system from Earth's orbit ($M_{\text{Sun}} = 1.99 \times 10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$, $R = 1.50 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm m$) the same formula gives $4.21 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 42\ \mathrm{km/s}$, nearly four times more. But a probe launched from Earth already shares Earth's orbital speed of about 30 km/s, so launching along Earth's motion leaves only about 12 km/s to supply. Earth's rotation (about 0.46 km/s at the equator) helps a little in the same way, which is why launch sites are placed near the equator; gravity-assist flybys of planets add more.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 13.7*

^ex-a8-2-2

## Circular orbits

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.2.4: Speed and Period of a Circular Orbit
> A satellite of mass $m \ll M$ in a circular orbit of radius $r$ about the centre of $M$ moves with
>
> $$
> v_{\text{orbit}} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}} .
> $$
>
> Neither depends on $m$. At every radius $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt2\, v_{\text{orbit}}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.4, eqs. (13.7)–(13.8)*

^thm-a8-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Gravity is the only force, and it points to the centre of the circle, so it supplies the centripetal acceleration $v^2/r$ ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion]]): $\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{m v^2}{r}$, which gives $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$. In one period the satellite covers the circumference, $v = 2\pi r/T$; substituting and solving gives $T = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/GM}$. Comparing with [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-3|Theorem §A8.2.3]] at the same $r$ gives the factor $\sqrt 2$.

^der-a8-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion|§A2.7]], [[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion|§A3.9]]

> [!remark] Remark: Weightless, not gravity-free
> An orbiting astronaut is in free fall: gravity is the only force, and astronaut and station fall together, so nothing presses on the astronaut and a scale would read zero. Picture a cannon on a mountain firing horizontally ever faster. Slow shots curve down and hit the ground; at $v_{\text{orbit}}$ the ground curves away exactly as fast as the shot falls, and it never lands. A surface orbit for Earth would have $v = 7.9\ \mathrm{km/s}$ and $T \approx 84\ \mathrm{min}$, the numbers of [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^ex-a8-1-3|Example §A8.1.3]]; air drag rules it out, but a few hundred kilometres up it works.

^rem-a8-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.2.5: Energy of a Circular Orbit
> For a circular orbit of radius $r$,
>
> $$
> K = \frac{GMm}{2r}, \qquad U = -\frac{GMm}{r}, \qquad E = K + U = -\frac{GMm}{2r} = -K = \tfrac12 U .
> $$
>
> The total energy is negative, as a bound orbit must have, and higher orbits have higher (less negative) energy but *less* kinetic energy.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.4, eq. (13.9)*

^thm-a8-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Multiply the orbit condition $GMm/r^2 = mv^2/r$ by $r$: $mv^2 = GMm/r$, so $K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = GMm/(2r)$. Add $U = -GMm/r$ from [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]].

^der-a8-2-5

*Uses:* [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]], [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-1|Theorem §A8.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Same energy, different direction
> Give an object the circular-orbit speed $v = \sqrt{GM/r_1}$ at radius $r_1$, but point it straight away from $M$. Its energy is still $E = -GMm/(2r_1)$, so by [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-3|Theorem §A8.2.3]] it climbs to $r_{\max} = GMm/\lvert E\rvert = 2r_1$ and falls back. For Earth's orbital speed of 30 km/s at $1.50 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm m$ from the Sun, that is $3.0 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm m$, past the orbit of Mars. The energy fixes the size of the orbit, not its shape: both orbits have the same semi-major axis ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-3|Theorem §A8.3.3]]).

^rem-a8-2-3

> [!example] Example §A8.2.3: Putting a Capsule into the Space Station's Orbit
> The ISS orbits at $r = R_E + 400\ \mathrm{km} = 6.77 \times 10^6\ \mathrm m$. By [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]],
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{\frac{GM_E}{r}} = 7.66 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = 5.55 \times 10^3\ \mathrm s \approx 92\ \mathrm{min}.
> $$
>
> What energy must be given to a 9000-kg Soyuz to bring it from rest on the ground into this orbit? In orbit, [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-5|Theorem §A8.2.5]] gives $E_{\text{orbit}} = -GM_E m/(2r) = -2.64 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm J$. On the ground the capsule is not in orbit, so its energy is only potential: $E_{\text{ground}} = -GM_E m/R_E = -5.62 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm J$ (ignoring Earth's rotation). Hence
>
> $$
> \Delta E = E_{\text{orbit}} - E_{\text{ground}} = 2.97 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm J, \qquad K_{\text{orbit}} = \lvert E_{\text{orbit}}\rvert = 2.64 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> About 89% of the energy goes into speed and only $\Delta U = 3.32 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm J$ into height ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^ex-a8-2-1|Example §A8.2.1]]): reaching orbit is mostly about going sideways fast. The rocket must in addition accelerate its own fuel, so the real cost is many times $\Delta E$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Examples 13.9, 13.12*

^ex-a8-2-3

> [!caution] Caution: Comparable masses orbit their centre of mass
> The formulas above assume $m \ll M$, so that $M$ stays put at the centre. Two comparable masses both orbit their common centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass]]): the distance $r$ between them sets the force, but each moves on a circle of a different, smaller radius (half of $r$ for equal masses). Then apply $F = m v^2/r_{\text{orbit}}$ to each body separately. For two galaxies of $1.6 \times 10^{42}\ \mathrm{kg}$ each, $2.4 \times 10^{22}\ \mathrm m$ apart, this gives 47 km/s and a period of about 50 billion years.

^cau-a8-2-3
