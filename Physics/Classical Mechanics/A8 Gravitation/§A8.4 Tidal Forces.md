So far every body has been a point, or a sphere acting as one. But a body of finite size feels a slightly different pull on its near and far sides, and that difference stretches it. This section explains why the oceans rise twice a day, why the Moon matters more for tides than the far stronger pull of the Sun, and what such differential forces do elsewhere in astronomy.

## The tidal force

> [!definition] Definition §A8.4.1: Tidal Force
> The **tidal force** on a part of an extended body, due to a distant mass $M$, is the difference between the gravitational force of $M$ on that part and the force it would feel at the body's centre. It stretches the body along the line toward $M$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.6*

^def-a8-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why the centre is the reference
> Earth as a whole falls freely toward the Moon: both orbit their common centre of mass, which lies about 1700 km below Earth's surface on the Earth–Moon line ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^ex-a5-4-1|Example §A5.4.1]]). Only what differs from that common fall can be seen on Earth. The near side is pulled a little more than the centre and the far side a little less, so relative to the centre both are pulled *outward*. That is why there are two tidal bulges, one facing the Moon and one facing away.

^rem-a8-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A8.4.1: Size of the Tidal Force
> Let a body of radius $R$ have its centre at distance $r$ from a mass $M$. On a small mass $m$, the difference between the pulls of $M$ at the near point ($r_1 = r - R$) and the far point ($r_2 = r + R$) is
>
> $$
> F_{\text{near}} - F_{\text{far}} = GMm\,\frac{(r_2 - r_1)(r_2 + r_1)}{r_1^2\, r_2^2} \;\approx\; \frac{4\,GMm\,R}{r^3} \qquad (R \ll r).
> $$
>
> The tidal effect falls off as $1/r^3$, one power faster than the pull itself.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.6, Example 13.14*

^thm-a8-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]], $F_{\text{near}} - F_{\text{far}} = GMm\left(\dfrac{1}{r_1^2} - \dfrac{1}{r_2^2}\right) = GMm\,\dfrac{r_2^2 - r_1^2}{r_1^2 r_2^2}$, and $r_2^2 - r_1^2 = (r_2 - r_1)(r_2 + r_1)$. Now $r_2 - r_1 = 2R$ exactly, $r_2 + r_1 = 2r$, and $r_1 r_2 = r^2 - R^2 \approx r^2$ when $R \ll r$. Hence the difference is $\approx GMm\,(2R)(2r)/r^4 = 4GMmR/r^3$.

^der-a8-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]], [[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^def-a8-4-1|Def. §A8.4.1]]

> [!example] Example §A8.4.1: Moon versus Sun
> Compare the Moon's pull on 1.0 kg at Earth's near and far sides, then do the same for the Sun. Data: $M_{\text{Moon}} = 7.35 \times 10^{22}\ \mathrm{kg}$ at $3.84 \times 10^8\ \mathrm m$; $M_{\text{Sun}} = 1.99 \times 10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$ at $1.50 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm m$; $R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6\ \mathrm m$.
>
> *Moon.* $F = GMm/(r \mp R_E)^2$ gives $F_{\text{near}} = 3.44 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm N$ and $F_{\text{far}} = 3.22 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm N$, about 7% apart, so the difference is $2.2 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm N$.
>
> *Sun.* The pull is much larger, $5.90 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm N$ (about 180 times the Moon's), but the near and far values differ only in the fourth significant figure: $5.89975 \times 10^{-3}$ and $5.89874 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm N$, a difference of $1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm N$ (0.017%).
>
> [[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^thm-a8-4-1|Theorem §A8.4.1]] gives both differences directly, without subtracting nearly equal numbers: $4GM_{\text{Moon}}R_E/r^3 = 2.2 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm N$ and $4GM_{\text{Sun}}R_E/r^3 = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm N$. The Moon's tidal effect is about twice the Sun's, although its pull is 180 times weaker. Earth's diameter is a far larger fraction of the distance to the Moon than of the distance to the Sun.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 13.14*

^ex-a8-4-1

> [!caution] Caution: Pull versus tide
> A bigger gravitational pull does not mean a bigger tide. Tides come from the *variation* of the pull across the body, which scales as $M/r^3$, not $M/r^2$. And the far-side bulge is not water "pushed away" from the Moon: the far-side water is simply pulled less than the rest of Earth.

^cau-a8-4-1

## Tides on Earth

> [!remark] Remark: Two tides a day, spring and neap
> - The bulges stay roughly on the Earth–Moon line while Earth turns beneath them, so a coast passes through two high and two low tides per rotation. The Moon advances along its orbit in the same direction (one circuit in about 28 days), so successive high tides come about every 12.5 hours, not 12.
> - The Sun's tidal bulges, about half as large, add to the Moon's at new and full moon, when Sun, Moon and Earth are aligned: these are the large **spring tides**. At half moon, when the directions to Sun and Moon are at right angles, they partly cancel: the small **neap tides**.
> - Both orbits are elliptical, so a spring tide is largest when the Moon is at perigee and Earth at perihelion ($1/r^3$ again). The height of the tide at a given place is governed mostly by the shape of the coast and the depth profile of the sea floor; in the Bay of Fundy it reaches 16.3 m.

^rem-a8-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Tidal effects elsewhere
> - *Tidal friction.* Flexing a body dissipates energy as heat. Earth's rotation is slowly being braked this way. The Moon has already been **tidally locked**: its rotation period equals its orbital period, so it always shows Earth the same face. Most moons in the solar system are locked.
> - *Tidal heating.* Jupiter's tidal forces knead its moon Io, keeping its interior hot enough for active volcanoes.
> - *Tidal disruption.* In a close binary star the tidal force of a compact companion (a neutron star or black hole) can exceed the self-gravity holding the other star together and pull matter off it. The matter forms a superheated accretion disc that radiates X-rays.

^rem-a8-4-3
