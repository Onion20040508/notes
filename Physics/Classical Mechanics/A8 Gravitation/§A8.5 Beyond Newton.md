Newton's law of gravitation is enough to navigate every spacecraft ever launched and to track asteroids and planets. Yet it rests on two assumptions it cannot justify, and a few observations disagree with it. This short section says where the Newtonian picture ends, sketches the idea of Einstein's general relativity (1916) that replaces it, and points ahead to the [[Relativity]] subject, where the theory itself is developed.

## What Newton's law takes for granted

> [!remark] Remark: Two unexplained assumptions
> - *Instant action at a distance.* In [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^pr-a8-1-1|Principle §A8.1.1]] the force depends on where the other mass is *now*: moving one mass would be felt everywhere instantly. Special relativity (1905) makes the speed of light $c$ a limit for every signal ([[§A1.5 Spacetime Diagrams and the Interval#^rem-a1-5-4|REL Remark in §A1.5]]), so gravity cannot work this way exactly.
> - *Two masses that happen to agree.* The mass in the law of gravitation is set equal to the inertial mass of $\vec F = m\vec a$. That is why all bodies fall alike ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^rem-a8-1-2|the remark on falling bodies]]). Experiment confirms the equality to high precision, but Newton's theory gives no reason for it.
>
> General relativity removes both: gravity is no longer a force transmitted between bodies but the geometry of space and time, and the equality of the two masses becomes its founding principle.

^rem-a8-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: The principle of equivalence
> Einstein asked why a person in free fall does not feel their own weight. His answer, the **principle of equivalence**, has two faces:
> - free fall in a gravitational field cannot be told apart from floating weightless far from all masses (orbiting astronauts, [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^rem-a8-2-2|the remark on weightlessness]]);
> - equally, a laboratory at rest in a uniform field $\vec g$ cannot be told apart from one accelerating at $\vec a = -\vec g$ in deep space: every experiment gives the same result in both.
>
> A perfectly uniform field does not exist, but over a laboratory near Earth the field is uniform to excellent approximation ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^mod-a8-1-3|Model §A8.1.3]]). What does distinguish the two cases over larger regions is the tidal force ([[§A8.4 Tidal Forces#^def-a8-4-1|Def. §A8.4.1]]). The principle is developed in Relativity, Chapter B4 (planned).

^rem-a8-5-2

## Gravity as geometry

> [!remark] Remark: Curved space-time
> In general relativity, mass and energy curve space and time around them, and bodies (and light) simply move along the straightest paths this curved space-time allows. Euclidean geometry, with straight lines and triangles summing to $180^\circ$, holds only in empty space. The curvature is not seen directly; it shows up in careful measurements of how bodies and light move. Near a large mass, space is stretched and clocks run slow.
>
> For weak fields the predictions agree with Newton's law, which is why the earlier sections work. The differences show up in precise or extreme situations, and each test so far has favoured general relativity:
> - Mercury's perihelion slowly advances. Most of this is due to the other planets, but a small remainder that Newton's law could not explain is exactly predicted by general relativity.
> - Light from distant stars is deflected when it passes the Sun.
> - Gravitational waves from the merger of two black holes were detected in 2015.

^rem-a8-5-3

## Black holes

> [!definition] Definition §A8.5.1: Schwarzschild Radius and Event Horizon
> A mass $M$ compressed inside the **Schwarzschild radius**
>
> $$
> R_S = \frac{2GM}{c^2}
> $$
>
> collapses to a **black hole**: nothing that passes inside $R_S$, not even light, can come back out. The sphere of radius $R_S$ is the **event horizon**. To a distant observer, an object falling toward the horizon seems to slow down and never quite reach it, because time near the horizon is stretched without limit.
>
> *Source: UP1 §13.7, eq. (13.12)*

^def-a8-5-1

> [!caution] Caution: A Newtonian coincidence
> Setting the escape speed $\sqrt{2GM/R}$ ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-3|Theorem §A8.2.3]]) equal to $c$ gives exactly $R = 2GM/c^2$. Michell and Laplace made this argument in the late 1700s, imagining "dark stars" whose light falls back. The agreement with Schwarzschild's 1916 result is an accident: the Newtonian argument uses, among other wrong assumptions, the classical kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mv^2$ for light. $R_S$ belongs to general relativity.

^cau-a8-5-1

> [!example] Example §A8.5.1: How Small Is a Black Hole?
> For the Sun ($M = 1.99 \times 10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg}$) and Earth ($M = 5.97 \times 10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg}$), with $c = 3.0 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$:
>
> $$
> R_S^{\text{Sun}} = \frac{2(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(1.99 \times 10^{30})}{(3.0 \times 10^8)^2}\ \mathrm m = 2.95\ \mathrm{km}, \qquad R_S^{\text{Earth}} = 8.85\ \mathrm{mm}.
> $$
>
> Squeezing Earth into a sphere of radius 8.85 mm would take an average density
>
> $$
> \rho = \frac{M}{\frac43\pi R_S^3} = \frac{5.97 \times 10^{24}\ \mathrm{kg}}{\frac43\pi(8.85 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm m)^3} = 2.06 \times 10^{30}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3},
> $$
>
> about $10^{13}$ times the density of an atomic nucleus or a neutron star ($2.3 \times 10^{17}\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$), the densest objects known short of black holes. No known process could do this. The Sun would need only about 80 times nuclear density. So black holes are expected to form from very massive objects, not small ones.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 13.15*

^ex-a8-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Seeing the unseen with Kepler's laws
> - *The black hole in the Milky Way.* Stars near the centre of our galaxy move on ellipses around an invisible point. Their periods and orbit sizes, through Kepler's third law ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-4|Theorem §A8.3.4]]), give a central mass of about 4 million solar masses, packed into a region that would fit inside the orbit of Mercury and emitting no visible light. The only viable interpretation is a black hole, and similar ones appear to sit at the centres of most galaxies.
> - *Dark matter.* For a star circling far from the centre, the shell theorem ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|Theorem §A8.1.5]]) and the orbit condition ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]]) give $v = \sqrt{G M(r)/r}$, with $M(r)$ the mass inside the orbit. Measured speeds of stars far out in galaxies are much higher than the visible mass allows, and they do not fall off with distance as expected. About five times more unseen matter than visible matter is needed. What it consists of is an open question.

^rem-a8-5-4
