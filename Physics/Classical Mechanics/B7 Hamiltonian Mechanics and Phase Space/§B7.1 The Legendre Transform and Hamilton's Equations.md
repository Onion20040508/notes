*Sources: Morin, Introduction to Classical Mechanics, §15.2–15.4 · Helliwell & Sahakian, Modern Classical Mechanics, §11.1–11.3 · Series Part II (the user's notes), §6–7.*

Lagrangian mechanics describes a state by positions and velocities $(q, \dot q)$ and gives $n$ second-order equations. Hamiltonian mechanics trades each velocity for its generalized momentum $p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot q_i$ ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]) and gives $2n$ first-order equations, in which $q$ and $p$ appear almost symmetrically. The function that generates them is the energy function $h$ of [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^def-b6-4-3|Def. §B6.4.3]], rewritten in the new variables; for most systems it is the mechanical energy of level A, kinetic plus potential ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^def-a4-6-1|Def. §A4.6.1]]), now expressed through momenta. The change of variable is a Legendre transform, and its properties are what make the equations come out symmetric.

*Notation.* $T$ is the kinetic energy (level A writes $K$) and $U$ the potential energy, as at level A (Morin and the series write $V$).

## The Legendre transform

> [!definition] Definition §B7.1.1: Legendre Transform
> Let $F(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ be of class $C^2$ with positive definite Hessian $\partial^2F/\partial x_i\partial x_j$ (strictly convex). Its slopes $s_i = \partial F/\partial x_i$ determine $x$ uniquely, $x = x(s)$, and the **Legendre transform** of $F$ is
>
> $$
> G(s) = \sum_i s_i\,x_i(s) - F\bigl(x(s)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> If $F$ also depends on further **passive** variables $y$, they are carried along unchanged: $G(s, y) = \sum_i s_i x_i - F(x, y)$, with $x = x(s, y)$. In one variable, $G(s)$ is minus the intercept on the vertical axis of the tangent line of slope $s$ to the graph of $F$. Examples: $F = x^2$ gives $s = 2x$ and $G = s^2/4$; $F = \tfrac12 mv^2$ gives $s = mv$ and $G = s^2/2m$.
>
> *Source: Morin §15.3.1–15.3.2 · H&S §11.1 · Series II §6.1*

^def-b7-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.1: Properties of the Legendre Transform
> With $F$, $G$ as in [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-1|Def. §B7.1.1]]:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial G}{\partial s_i} = x_i, \qquad \frac{\partial G}{\partial y_k} = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_k},
> $$
>
> and the transform is its own inverse: the Legendre transform of $G$ in $s$ is $F$. So $s = \nabla_x F$ and $x = \nabla_s G$ carry the same information, with the roles of variable and slope exchanged.
>
> *Source: Morin §15.3.1, eqs. (15.48)–(15.49) · H&S §11.1, eqs. (11.16)–(11.19) · Series II §6.2, Remark 6.3*

^thm-b7-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Invertibility.* The Jacobian matrix of $x \mapsto s = \nabla F(x)$ is the Hessian, which is invertible, so by the inverse function theorem ([[§13 The Inverse Function Theorem#^thm-13-2|452 Thm. §13.2]]) the map has a $C^1$ inverse near each point. It is also one-to-one on a convex domain: for $x \ne x'$, the function $t \mapsto (x - x')\cdot\nabla F(x' + t(x - x'))$ has positive derivative, so $(\nabla F(x) - \nabla F(x'))\cdot(x - x') > 0$.
>
> *Derivatives.* Take the differential of $G = \sum_i s_ix_i - F$:
>
> $$
> dG = \sum_i\bigl(s_i\,dx_i + x_i\,ds_i\bigr) - \sum_i\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i}\,dx_i - \sum_k\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_k}\,dy_k = \sum_i x_i\,ds_i - \sum_k\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_k}\,dy_k ,
> $$
>
> since $\partial F/\partial x_i = s_i$: the $dx_i$ terms cancel. This cancellation is the whole design of the transform; it leaves a function of $(s, y)$ whose partial derivatives can be read off.
>
> *Involution.* The slope of $G$ is $\nabla_s G = x$, so the transform of $G$ is $\sum_i x_is_i - G = \sum_i x_is_i - (\sum_i s_ix_i - F) = F$, as a function of $x$.

^der-b7-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-1|Def. §B7.1.1]], [[§13 The Inverse Function Theorem#^thm-13-2|452 Thm. §13.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why not simply substitute?
> Expressing $F$ in terms of its slope by substitution, $B(s) = F(x(s))$, loses information. For $A(x, y) = x^2 + (y - a)^2$ with slope $z = \partial A/\partial y = 2(y - a)$, the substitution gives $x^2 + z^2/4$, the same for every $a$: a function is not determined by its slopes alone, since shifting it sideways keeps them. The Legendre transform records the slopes *and* the intercepts of the tangent lines, and $F$ is recovered as their envelope. For $A$ it gives $B(x, z) = \tfrac14(z + 2a)^2 - x^2 - a^2$, which remembers $a$.
>
> *Source: H&S §11.1, Example 11.1 · Morin §15.3.2*

## The Hamiltonian

> [!definition] Definition §B7.1.2: Canonical Momenta; Hamiltonian
> Let $L(q, \dot q, t)$ be $C^2$ with positive definite Hessian $\partial^2L/\partial\dot q_i\partial\dot q_j$; for a mechanical Lagrangian this Hessian is the mass matrix $\mathsf M(q, t)$ of the kinetic energy, positive definite ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]). The **canonical momenta**
>
> $$
> p_i = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}(q, \dot q, t)
> $$
>
> can then be solved for the velocities, $\dot q = \dot q(q, p, t)$, and the **Hamiltonian** is the Legendre transform of $L$ in the velocities, with $q$ and $t$ passive:
>
> $$
> H(q, p, t) = \sum_i p_i\,\dot q_i(q, p, t) - L\bigl(q, \dot q(q, p, t), t\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The $q_i$ and $p_i$ are the **canonical coordinates and momenta**; the $2n$-dimensional space of $(q, p)$ is phase space ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^def-b7-2-1|Def. §B7.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Series II §6.1, Definition 6.1 · Morin §15.2.1, eq. (15.21); §15.3.3 · H&S §11.2, eqs. (11.34)–(11.35)*

^def-b7-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: h and H, the same number but not the same function
> At corresponding states, $H(q, p, t) = h(q, \dot q, t)$: the Hamiltonian takes the value of the energy function. But $h$ is a function of $(q, \dot q)$ and $H$ of $(q, p)$, and partial derivatives depend on what is held fixed. At fixed $\dot q$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial h}{\partial q_i} = \sum_j\dot q_j\,\frac{\partial^2L}{\partial q_i\,\partial\dot q_j} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}, \qquad\text{while at fixed } p, \qquad \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} .
> $$
>
> They differ whenever the mass matrix depends on $q$ (the double pendulum). Hamilton's equations hold for $H$, not for $h$. The same formula $p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot q_i$ that in [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^def-b6-4-1|Def. §B6.4.1]] named a quantity computed along a motion is here read as a change of variables that promotes $p$ to an independent coordinate.
>
> *Source: Series II §6.2, Remark 6.2*

^rem-b7-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Eliminate the velocities first; say what is held fixed
> - $H$ must be written as a function of $(q, p, t)$, with every $\dot q$ eliminated, *before* Hamilton's equations are applied. Differentiating $\sum p\dot q - L$ with velocities still in it gives wrong equations of motion.
> - "$\partial L/\partial q$" is ambiguous inside $H$: $\partial L(q, \dot q(q, p))/\partial q$ at fixed $p$ is not $\partial L(q, \dot q)/\partial q$ at fixed $\dot q$. The relation $\partial H/\partial q = -\partial L/\partial q$ holds with the second meaning. A tempting short derivation ("$p$ and $\dot q$ do not depend on $q$, so $\partial(p\dot q)/\partial q = 0$") is wrong, because $\dot q(q, p)$ does depend on $q$; the error happens to cancel against the confusion of the two meanings.
>
> *Source: H&S §11.2 · Morin §15.2.2, Remark 1; §15.2.3, Remark*

## Hamilton's equations

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.2: Hamilton's Equations
> With $H$ as in [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]], the identities
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} = \dot q_i, \qquad \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}, \qquad \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}
> $$
>
> hold at every state, and a curve $q(t)$ solves Lagrange's equations if and only if $(q(t), p(t))$, with $p$ the canonical momentum, solves **Hamilton's equations**
>
> $$
> \dot q_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n .
> $$
>
> *Source: Series II §6.2, eqs. (94)–(95) · Morin §15.2.2, eq. (15.37); §15.3.3 · H&S §11.2, eqs. (11.32), (11.36)*

^thm-b7-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The three identities are [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]] with active variable $x = \dot q$, slope $s = p$ and passive variables $y = (q, t)$; no dynamics is used. Explicitly,
>
> $$
> dH = \sum_i\bigl(p_i\,d\dot q_i + \dot q_i\,dp_i\bigr) - \sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}\,dq_i - \sum_i\underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}}_{= p_i}d\dot q_i - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}\,dt = \sum_i\dot q_i\,dp_i - \sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}\,dq_i - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}\,dt ,
> $$
>
> and the coefficients of $dp_i$, $dq_i$, $dt$ are the partial derivatives of $H(q, p, t)$.
>
> *Lagrange ⟹ Hamilton.* Along a solution, Lagrange's equation $\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i} = \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}$ ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]) reads $\dot p_i = \partial L/\partial q_i = -\partial H/\partial q_i$, and the first identity says $\dot q_i = \partial H/\partial p_i$.
>
> *Hamilton ⟹ Lagrange.* If $(q(t), p(t))$ solves Hamilton's equations, then $\dot q = \partial H/\partial p(q, p, t) = \dot q(q, p, t)$, so $p(t)$ is the canonical momentum of the curve's own velocity, $p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot q_i$; and $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q_i = \partial L/\partial q_i$ is Lagrange's equation.

^der-b7-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]], [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where the physics sits
> The first Hamilton equation, $\dot q = \partial H/\partial p$, only repeats the definition of $p$; in every example it gives back $p = m\dot q$ or its analogue. The dynamics is in the second, $\dot p = -\partial H/\partial q$, which is Lagrange's equation in disguise. In practice one usually eliminates $p$ again and arrives at the Lagrange equations, so for a few particles nothing is gained in speed. The gains are structural: first-order equations whose solutions are curves in phase space, determined by a single point ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]]); a form well suited to numerical integration; transformations that mix $q$ and $p$ ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^def-b8-2-1|Def. §B8.2.1]]), of which the time evolution itself is one ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-4|Theorem §B8.2.4]]); and the form that quantum mechanics takes over.
>
> *Source: Morin §15.2.2, Remark 2; §15.2.4 · H&S §11.2 · Series II §6*

^rem-b7-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.3: Hamilton's Equations from a Phase-Space Action
> Let $q(t)$ and $p(t)$ be varied independently on $[t_0, t_1]$, with $q$ fixed at both ends and $p$ free. The phase-space action
>
> $$
> S[q, p] = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\Bigl(\sum_i p_i\,\dot q_i - H(q, p, t)\Bigr)dt
> $$
>
> is stationary exactly at the solutions of Hamilton's equations. On such a solution the integrand equals $L$, and $S$ is the action of Hamilton's principle ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|Principle §B6.2.1]]). The same functional tests canonical transformations ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]]), and evaluated along true paths as a function of the end point it becomes the generating function of the time evolution ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-4|Theorem §B8.3.4]]).
>
> *Source: Series II §6.2, eqs. (96)–(98) · Morin §15.4.1*

^thm-b7-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Varying $p$*, $p \to p + \varepsilon\,\delta p$: no derivative of $p$ appears, so no integration by parts is needed and
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}S\Big|_0 = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\sum_i\Bigl(\dot q_i - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\Bigr)\delta p_i\,dt .
> $$
>
> *Varying $q$*, $q \to q + \varepsilon\,\delta q$ with $\delta q(t_0) = \delta q(t_1) = 0$: integrate $\int p_i\,\delta\dot q_i\,dt$ by parts; the boundary term $[p_i\,\delta q_i]$ vanishes, and
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}S\Big|_0 = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\sum_i\Bigl(-\dot p_i - \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\Bigr)\delta q_i\,dt .
> $$
>
> Both vanish for every admissible $\delta p$, $\delta q$ exactly when the brackets vanish identically, by the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|Lemma §B5.1.2]]): these are Hamilton's equations. On a solution $\dot q = \partial H/\partial p$, so $\sum p\dot q - H = L$ by the involution property of [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-1|Theorem §B7.1.1]].

## The Hamiltonian as energy

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.1.4: Conservation and Form of the Hamiltonian
> 1. Along every solution of Hamilton's equations, $\dfrac{dH}{dt} = \dfrac{\partial H}{\partial t} = -\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial t}$; if $H$ has no explicit time dependence, it is conserved.
> 2. $H$ takes the value of the energy function $h$, so $H = T_2 - T_0 + U$ in general and $H = T + U$ when the coordinates are related to Cartesian ones without explicit $t$ and $U$ is velocity-independent ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]]).
> 3. For $L = \tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M(q)\,\dot q - U(q, t)$ with $\mathsf M(q)$ symmetric positive definite,
>
> $$
> p = \mathsf M(q)\,\dot q, \qquad \dot q = \mathsf M(q)^{-1}p, \qquad H(q, p, t) = \tfrac12\,p^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M(q)^{-1}p + U(q, t) .
> $$
>
> 4. A coordinate is cyclic in $H$ exactly when it is cyclic in $L$, and then its momentum is conserved.
>
> *Source: Series II §6.2, eq. (99); §6.3, Proposition 6.4; §7.2, Proposition 7.1 · Morin §15.1; §15.2.3 · H&S §11.2*

^thm-b7-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) By the chain rule and Hamilton's equations,
>
> $$
> \frac{dH}{dt} = \sum_i\Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i\Bigr) + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = \sum_i\Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\Bigr) + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial t},
> $$
>
> and $\partial H/\partial t = -\partial L/\partial t$ by [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]. This is [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]] again, in the new variables.
>
> (2) Both are $\sum_i p_i\dot q_i - L$ at corresponding states.
>
> (3) $p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot q_i = \sum_j M_{ij}\dot q_j$; $\mathsf M$ is invertible because it is positive definite. Then, using the symmetry of $\mathsf M^{-1}$,
>
> $$
> H = p^{\mathsf T}\dot q - L = p^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}p - \tfrac12(\mathsf M^{-1}p)^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M(\mathsf M^{-1}p) + U = \tfrac12\,p^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M^{-1}p + U ,
> $$
>
> which is $T + U$ with $T$ written in the momenta.
>
> (4) $\partial H/\partial q_k = -\partial L/\partial q_k$ by [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]], so one vanishes if and only if the other does, and then $\dot p_k = -\partial H/\partial q_k = 0$.

^der-b7-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]], [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]], [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: A position-dependent mass matrix
> When $\mathsf M$ depends on $q$, the kinetic term contributes to $\dot p = -\partial H/\partial q$, through $\partial_q\mathsf M^{-1} = -\mathsf M^{-1}(\partial_q\mathsf M)\,\mathsf M^{-1}$ (differentiate $\mathsf M\mathsf M^{-1} = \mathbb 1$). For a bead on a frictionless parabolic wire $y = x^2/2a$ in uniform gravity, $L = \tfrac12 m(1 + x^2/a^2)\dot x^2 - mgx^2/2a$, so $p = m(1 + x^2/a^2)\dot x$,
>
> $$
> H = \frac{p^2}{2m(1 + x^2/a^2)} + \frac{mg\,x^2}{2a}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{mg\,x}{a} + \frac{x\,p^2}{m a^2(1 + x^2/a^2)^2} :
> $$
>
> besides gravity there is a term from the varying effective mass, which a careless $\dot p = -\partial U/\partial x$ would miss.
>
> *Source: H&S §11.3, Example 11.3 (with $a = 1$ there) · Series II §7.2, eq. (121)*

^rem-b7-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: The charged particle
> For $L = \tfrac12 m|\vec v|^2 - q\phi + q\,\vec v\cdot\vec A$ (SI units), the canonical momentum is $\vec p = m\vec v + q\vec A$, not $m\vec v$, and
>
> $$
> H = \frac{|\vec p - q\vec A|^2}{2m} + q\phi ,
> $$
>
> which is still the energy $\tfrac12 m|\vec v|^2 + q\phi$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^rem-b6-4-4|Remark: Velocity-dependent potentials]]). The magnetic field enters only through the shift $\vec p \to \vec p - q\vec A$; its force is the magnetic part of the Lorentz force ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|EM Principle §A7.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: H&S §11.3, Example 11.4 (Gaussian units there)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B7.1.1: Central Force
> A particle moves in a plane in the potential $U(r)$. In polar coordinates $L = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2) - U(r)$, so $p_r = m\dot r$, $p_\varphi = mr^2\dot\varphi$ and, by [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]] with $\mathsf M = \operatorname{diag}(m, mr^2)$,
>
> $$
> H = \frac{p_r^2}{2m} + \frac{p_\varphi^2}{2mr^2} + U(r) .
> $$
>
> Hamilton's equations:
>
> $$
> \dot r = \frac{p_r}{m}, \qquad \dot p_r = \frac{p_\varphi^2}{mr^3} - U'(r), \qquad \dot\varphi = \frac{p_\varphi}{mr^2}, \qquad \dot p_\varphi = 0 .
> $$
>
> The first and third repeat the definitions of the momenta; the fourth says $\varphi$ is cyclic and the angular momentum $p_\varphi$ is conserved; with $p_r = m\dot r$ the second is the radial equation $m\ddot r = p_\varphi^2/mr^3 - U'(r)$. Because $p_\varphi$ is now an independent variable with a constant value, it may simply be held at that value in $H$: $H = p_r^2/2m + U_{\text{eff}}(r)$ with $U_{\text{eff}} = U + p_\varphi^2/2mr^2$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]], which writes the angular momentum as $L$; here $L$ is the Lagrangian), a one-dimensional Hamiltonian whose $\dot p_r = -\partial H/\partial r$ has the correct sign of the centrifugal term. The same substitution in $L$ gives the wrong sign ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^cau-b6-4-1|Caution: Cyclic depends on the coordinates]]).
>
> *Source: Morin §15.2.1–15.2.2, central-force examples, eqs. (15.25), (15.39)*

^ex-b7-1-1

> [!example] Example §B7.1.2: The Pendulum, Solved Completely
> A bob of mass $m$ on a rigid massless rod of length $\ell$, with $\theta$ the angle from the downward vertical: $L = \tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2 + mg\ell\cos\theta$, so $p = m\ell^2\dot\theta$ (the angular momentum about the pivot) and, the constraint being fixed, $H = T + U$:
>
> $$
> H(\theta, p) = \frac{p^2}{2m\ell^2} - mg\ell\cos\theta, \qquad \dot\theta = \frac{p}{m\ell^2}, \qquad \dot p = -mg\ell\sin\theta .
> $$
>
> Eliminating $p$ gives $\ddot\theta = -(g/\ell)\sin\theta$ ([[§A1.4 Pendulums#^thm-a1-4-2|WO Theorem §A1.4.2]]).
>
> *Small angles.* With $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/\ell}$, $H_2 = p^2/2m\ell^2 + \tfrac12 mg\ell\,\theta^2$ (constant dropped), and the solution with initial data $(\theta_0, p_0)$ is
>
> $$
> \theta(t) = \theta_0\cos\omega_0t + \frac{p_0}{m\ell^2\omega_0}\sin\omega_0t, \qquad p(t) = p_0\cos\omega_0t - m\ell^2\omega_0\,\theta_0\sin\omega_0t .
> $$
>
> Since $H_2 = E$ is conserved, the phase point runs once per period around the ellipse $\theta^2/(2E/mg\ell) + p^2/(2m\ell^2E) = 1$, whose area is $\pi\sqrt{2E/mg\ell}\,\sqrt{2m\ell^2E} = 2\pi E/\omega_0$. The ratio $E/\omega_0$, the enclosed area over $2\pi$, reappears as the action variable ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^ex-b8-2-3|Example §B8.2.3]], [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^rem-b8-3-5|Remark: How far the method reaches]]).
>
> *Any amplitude.* Conservation of $H = E$ gives $p(\theta) = \pm\sqrt{2m\ell^2(E + mg\ell\cos\theta)}$ on the energy curve, and the first Hamilton equation then separates:
>
> $$
> t = \int\frac{m\ell^2\,d\theta}{p(\theta)} = \int\frac{m\ell^2\,d\theta}{\sqrt{2m\ell^2\bigl(E + mg\ell\cos\theta\bigr)}} .
> $$
>
> The motion is reduced to one integral (a quadrature). For an oscillation with turning angle $\theta_{\max}$, $E = -mg\ell\cos\theta_{\max}$, and the integral over a full swing gives the period $4\sqrt{\ell/g}\,K\bigl(\sin\tfrac12\theta_{\max}\bigr)$, with $K$ the complete elliptic integral of the first kind ([[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^ex-b1-3-1|Example §B1.3.1]]), which grows with the amplitude and diverges as $\theta_{\max} \to \pi$: the period becomes infinite at the separatrix of the phase portrait ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^ex-b7-2-1|Example §B7.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Series II §4, §7.1, eqs. (105)–(115)*

> [!example] Example §B7.1.3: The Double Pendulum in Hamiltonian Form
> For the double pendulum of [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^ex-b6-5-1|Example §B6.5.1]], with $\Delta = \theta_1 - \theta_2$, the mass matrix is
>
> $$
> \mathsf M(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} (m_1 + m_2)\ell_1^2 & m_2\ell_1\ell_2\cos\Delta \\ m_2\ell_1\ell_2\cos\Delta & m_2\ell_2^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det\mathsf M = m_2\ell_1^2\ell_2^2\bigl(m_1 + m_2\sin^2\Delta\bigr) > 0 ,
> $$
>
> positive at every configuration, so the Legendre transform is legitimate everywhere, and [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]] gives the exact Hamiltonian
>
> $$
> H = \frac{m_2\ell_2^2\,p_1^2 + (m_1 + m_2)\ell_1^2\,p_2^2 - 2m_2\ell_1\ell_2\cos\Delta\,p_1p_2}{2m_2\ell_1^2\ell_2^2\bigl(m_1 + m_2\sin^2\Delta\bigr)} - (m_1 + m_2)g\ell_1\cos\theta_1 - m_2g\ell_2\cos\theta_2 .
> $$
>
> Its four first-order equations are what a numerical integrator solves.
>
> *Small angles.* $p$ is already first order, so $\mathsf M(\theta)^{-1}$ may be taken at equilibrium, $\mathsf M_0 = \mathsf M(0)$, and $H_2 = \tfrac12 p^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M_0^{-1}p + \tfrac12\theta^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K\theta$ with $\mathsf K$ as in Example §B6.5.1. Take the $\mathsf M_0$-orthonormal mode shapes $v_r$ of [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^thm-b6-5-3|Theorem §B6.5.3]] and define mode coordinates and mode momenta
>
> $$
> \xi_r = v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M_0\,\theta, \qquad \pi_r = v_r^{\mathsf T}p, \qquad\text{so that}\qquad \theta = \sum_r\xi_r v_r, \qquad p = \sum_r\pi_r\,\mathsf M_0 v_r .
> $$
>
> Substituting, and using $v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M_0v_s = \delta_{rs}$ and $v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K v_s = \omega_s^2\delta_{rs}$,
>
> $$
> H_2 = \sum_r\Bigl(\tfrac12\pi_r^2 + \tfrac12\omega_r^2\xi_r^2\Bigr), \qquad \dot\xi_r = v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M_0\mathsf M_0^{-1}p = \pi_r, \qquad \dot\pi_r = -v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K\theta = -\omega_r^2\xi_r .
> $$
>
> The Lagrangian treatment decoupled the equations; the Hamiltonian one decouples the energy itself into independent unit-mass oscillators, each with its own conserved energy ([[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-2|WO Theorem §B2.2.2]]). The equations for $(\xi, \pi)$ have Hamilton's form with the Hamiltonian $H_2$ written in the new variables: $(\theta, p) \to (\xi, \pi)$ is a canonical transformation ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^def-b8-2-1|Def. §B8.2.1]]), and the condition that certifies it, $\{\xi_r, \pi_s\} = \delta_{rs}$, is the $\mathsf M_0$-orthonormality of the modes ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^rem-b8-2-1|Remark: Normal coordinates are canonical]]).
>
> *Source: Series II §7.2, eqs. (118)–(129); §8.1, Example 8.2*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The Legendre transform needs the inverse function theorem ([[§13 The Inverse Function Theorem#^thm-13-2|452 Thm. §13.2]]); the same transform links the thermodynamic potentials ([[§B1.2 Partial-Derivative Identities and Legendre Transforms#^def-b1-2-2|TH Def. §B1.2.2]], [[§B3.1 Potentials and Their Natural Variables#^rem-b3-1-3|TH Remark: Each potential is a Legendre transform]]). Thermodynamics uses the opposite overall sign: where Def. §B7.1.1 writes $G = sx - F$, it writes $g = f - ux$ with $u = \partial f/\partial x$, that is $-G$, so that potentials such as the free energy $U - TS$ keep the sign of an energy.
> - Geometrically, $(q, \dot q)$ is a point of the tangent bundle of configuration space ([[§32 The Tangent Bundle#^def-32-1|591 Def. §32.1]]) and $(q, p)$ a point of the cotangent bundle ([[§32 The Tangent Bundle#^def-32-5|591 Def. §32.5]]): $p$ is a covector, paired with velocities in $\sum_i p_i\dot q_i$ (H&S §11.3, footnote). The Legendre transform maps the one bundle to the other, fibre by fibre.
