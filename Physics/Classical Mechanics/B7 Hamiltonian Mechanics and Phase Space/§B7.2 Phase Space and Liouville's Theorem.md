Level A read one-dimensional motion off an energy diagram: where the particle may go, where it turns, which equilibria are stable ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|Theorem §A4.7.1]], [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|Theorem §A4.7.2]]). Hamilton's equations ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]) turn that picture into a complete one: a state is a point $(q, p)$, the equations define a flow on the space of states, and for one degree of freedom the trajectories are the level curves of $H$, so the whole qualitative behaviour is one contour plot. The new result is Liouville's theorem: the flow moves regions of phase space without changing their volume. It has no counterpart in the Lagrangian variables $(q, \dot q)$, and it is what statistical mechanics is built on.

*Notation.* Potential energy is $U$, as at level A (Morin and the series write $V$); $T(E)$ in this section is a period, not a kinetic energy.

## Phase space and the Hamiltonian flow

> [!definition] Definition §B7.2.1: Phase Space; Hamiltonian Flow
> For a system with $n$ generalized coordinates, **phase space** is the $2n$-dimensional space of the canonical variables $z = (q_1, \dots, q_n, p_1, \dots, p_n)$; a point of it, a **phase point**, is a complete state of the system. Hamilton's equations say that the phase point moves with the **Hamiltonian vector field**
>
> $$
> X_H = \Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_n},\ -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_1}, \dots, -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_n}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> and its path is a **phase trajectory**. The **phase flow** $\varphi_t$ maps each initial state $z_0$ to the state $\varphi_t(z_0)$ at time $t$. A drawing of representative trajectories is a **phase portrait**.
>
> *Source: Morin §15.5.1 · H&S §11.3, eq. (11.37) · Series II §9.1, §9.2, eq. (143)*

^def-b7-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.1: Phase Trajectories Do Not Cross
> Let $H(q, p)$ be of class $C^2$ and independent of $t$. Through each point of phase space passes exactly one phase trajectory: two trajectories that share a point coincide, a trajectory cannot branch, and an equilibrium point ($X_H = 0$) is never reached in finite time from any other point.
>
> *Source: Morin §15.5.1 · Series II §6 (existence and uniqueness, stated)*

^thm-b7-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $X_H$ is $C^1$ because $H$ is $C^2$, so the initial-value problem $\dot z = X_H(z)$, $z(t_0) = z_0$ has exactly one solution (existence and uniqueness for ordinary differential equations with continuously differentiable right-hand side; stated, Mathematical Methods, planned). Since $X_H$ does not depend on $t$, a time shift of a solution is again a solution. If two trajectories pass through the same point $z_*$, at times $t_1$ and $t_2$, shift the second by $t_1 - t_2$; then both solve the same initial-value problem at $t_1$ and coincide. An equilibrium $z_e$ carries the constant solution; a trajectory reaching $z_e$ at a finite time would share that point with it and so would be the constant solution throughout.
>
> Morin's version: crossing trajectories would give two velocity vectors $(\dot q, \dot p)$ at one point, while Hamilton's equations fix exactly one. That excludes crossing at an angle; excluding branching with a common tangent needs the uniqueness theorem (Morin, Problem 15.17).

^der-b7-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^def-b7-2-1|Def. §B7.2.1]], [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Time-dependent H, and why p and not the velocity
> If $H$ depends on $t$, trajectories in $(q, p)$ can cross at different times; they do not cross in the extended space $(q, p, t)$. Phase space uses $p$ rather than $\dot q$ because Hamilton's equations, which treat $q$ and $p$ almost symmetrically, are what make Liouville's theorem below true; in $(q, \dot q)$ it can fail ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^ex-b7-2-3|Example §B7.2.3]]). Geometrically, $(q, p)$ is a point of the cotangent bundle of configuration space ([[§35 The Tangent Bundle#^def-35-5|591 Def. §35.5]]); for the pendulum, whose $\theta$ lives on a circle, phase space is a cylinder.
>
> *Source: Morin §15.5.1 · H&S §11.3, footnote to Example 11.3*

## Phase portraits in one degree of freedom

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.2: Phase Curves of One Degree of Freedom
> Let $H(q, p) = \dfrac{p^2}{2\mu} + U(q)$ with constant $\mu > 0$ and smooth $U$. Then:
> 1. every trajectory lies on a level curve $H = E$, which consists of the arcs $p = \pm\sqrt{2\mu\,(E - U(q))}$ over the allowed region $U(q) \le E$; it is symmetric under $p \to -p$, is traversed toward increasing $q$ where $p > 0$ and decreasing $q$ where $p < 0$, and meets the $q$ axis at the turning points;
> 2. the equilibria are the points $(q_0, 0)$ with $U'(q_0) = 0$. Near a minimum with $U''(q_0) > 0$ the level curves are small ellipses around it (a **centre**); near a maximum with $U''(q_0) < 0$ they are hyperbolas around the two lines $p = \pm\sqrt{\mu\,|U''(q_0)|}\,(q - q_0)$ that cross at it (a **saddle**);
> 3. a closed level curve between simple turning points $q_- < q_+$ (where $U' \ne 0$) is a periodic orbit, and its period ([[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]], with $m$ replaced by $\mu$) is the rate at which the enclosed phase-space area $A(E) = \oint p\,dq$ grows with the energy:
>
> $$
> T(E) = 2\int_{q_-}^{q_+}\frac{\mu\,dq}{\sqrt{2\mu\,(E - U(q))}} = \frac{dA}{dE} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Series II §9.1; §7.1, eqs. (110)–(113) · Morin §15.5.1 · H&S §11.3. The relation $T = dA/dE$ is derived here; the series computes $A = 2\pi E/\omega_0$ for the oscillator, consistent with $T = 2\pi/\omega_0$.*

^thm-b7-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $H$ has no explicit $t$, so it is conserved ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]]); solving $p^2/2\mu + U = E$ for $p$ gives the two arcs, real exactly where $U \le E$ (the allowed region of [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|Theorem §A4.7.1]]). The direction comes from $\dot q = \partial H/\partial p = p/\mu$.
>
> (2) $X_H = (p/\mu, -U'(q))$ vanishes exactly when $p = 0$ and $U'(q) = 0$. By Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), near $q_0$, $H \approx U(q_0) + p^2/2\mu + \tfrac12 U''(q_0)(q - q_0)^2$. If $U''(q_0) > 0$, the levels slightly above $U(q_0)$ are ellipses. If $U''(q_0) < 0$, the level $H = U(q_0)$ is the pair of lines $p^2 = \mu|U''(q_0)|(q - q_0)^2$, and nearby levels are hyperbolas.
>
> (3) Along the orbit $dt = dq/\dot q = \mu\,dq/p$; it runs from $q_-$ to $q_+$ on the upper arc and back on the lower one, which gives the integral for $T$ (the period of [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]], with $\mu$ for $m$). The enclosed area is $A(E) = 2\int_{q_-}^{q_+}\sqrt{2\mu(E - U)}\,dq$. Differentiate in $E$: the endpoints $q_\pm(E)$ move, but the integrand vanishes there, so they contribute nothing, and $\partial_E\sqrt{2\mu(E - U)} = \mu/\sqrt{2\mu(E - U)}$. Hence $dA/dE = T$. (At a simple turning point the integrand of $T$ blows up like $|q - q_\pm|^{-1/2}$, which is integrable, so the differentiation under the integral sign is legitimate.)

^der-b7-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^def-b7-2-1|Def. §B7.2.1]], [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]], [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]], [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|Theorem §A4.7.1]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Simple phase portraits
> - Free particle, $H = p^2/2m$: horizontal lines $p = \text{const}$, traversed to the right above the axis and to the left below it, faster the larger $|p|$.
> - Ball thrown vertically, with $q$ measured downward, $H = p^2/2m - mgq$: the rightward-opening parabolas $q = q_0 + (p^2 - p_0^2)/2m^2g$, all with the same curvature.
> - Harmonic oscillator, $H = p^2/2m + \tfrac12 kq^2$: ellipses around the origin, each traversed in the same time $2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$; the larger ellipses have the larger speeds. Equal periods mean $dA/dE$ is the same for all, so $A \propto E$: indeed $A = 2\pi E\sqrt{m/k}$.
>
> No differential equation was solved: a phase portrait is a contour plot of $H$.
>
> *Source: Morin §15.5.1, Examples 1–3 · H&S §11.3, Example 11.2 · Series II §9.1, Figure 3*

## Liouville's theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.3: Liouville's Theorem
> Let $H(q, p, t)$ be of class $C^2$. The Hamiltonian flow preserves phase-space volume: for every bounded region $\Omega$ of phase space and every $t$,
>
> $$
> \operatorname{vol}\bigl(\varphi_t(\Omega)\bigr) = \operatorname{vol}(\Omega), \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \det D\varphi_t = 1 .
> $$
>
> The shape of the region may be sheared, stretched and folded beyond recognition; its $2n$-dimensional volume does not change. This holds whether or not $H$ depends on time.
>
> *Source: Series II §9.2, Theorem 9.1 · Morin §15.5.2, Theorem 15.2 · H&S §11.7*

^thm-b7-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The divergence vanishes.* Write $\dot z = X(z, t)$ with $X = X_H$. Its divergence in the $2n$ variables is
>
> $$
> \nabla\cdot X = \sum_{i=1}^n\Bigl[\frac{\partial}{\partial q_i}\Bigl(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\Bigr) + \frac{\partial}{\partial p_i}\Bigl(-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\Bigr)\Bigr] = \sum_{i=1}^n\Bigl(\frac{\partial^2H}{\partial q_i\,\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial^2H}{\partial p_i\,\partial q_i}\Bigr) = 0
> $$
>
> by equality of mixed partials ([[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]). The minus sign in Hamilton's equations pairs each expansion rate $\partial\dot q_i/\partial q_i$ with an equal contraction rate $\partial\dot p_i/\partial p_i$.
>
> *The Jacobian stays 1.* Fix $z_0$ and let $\Phi(t) = D\varphi_t(z_0)$, the $2n \times 2n$ Jacobian matrix of the flow map. Differentiating $\frac{d}{dt}\varphi_t(z_0) = X(\varphi_t(z_0), t)$ with respect to $z_0$ (the flow depends smoothly on initial data; stated) gives
>
> $$
> \dot\Phi = DX\bigl(\varphi_t(z_0), t\bigr)\,\Phi, \qquad \Phi(0) = \mathbb 1 .
> $$
>
> By Jacobi's formula for the derivative of a determinant ([[§11 The Classical Groups Are Topological Manifolds#^prop-11-1|591 Prop. §11.1]]), as long as $\Phi$ is invertible,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\det\Phi = \det\Phi\,\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\Phi^{-1}\dot\Phi\bigr) = \det\Phi\,\operatorname{tr}DX = (\nabla\cdot X)\det\Phi = 0 ,
> $$
>
> using $\operatorname{tr}(\Phi^{-1}DX\,\Phi) = \operatorname{tr}DX$. Since $\det\Phi(0) = 1$, $\det\Phi(t) = 1$ for all $t$ (and $\Phi$ stays invertible).
>
> *Volume.* By the change-of-variables formula ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-17|452 Thm. §15.17]]), $\operatorname{vol}(\varphi_t(\Omega)) = \int_\Omega|\det D\varphi_t|\,dV = \operatorname{vol}(\Omega)$.
>
> *The flux route* (series, Morin): a region carried by the flow gains volume where the boundary moves outward and loses it where it moves inward, $\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{vol}(\Omega_t) = \oint_{\partial\Omega_t}X\cdot\hat n\,dA$, which by the divergence theorem ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-1|452 Thm. §17.1]]) is $\int_{\Omega_t}\nabla\cdot X\,dV = 0$. The Jacobian route proves the transport identity this uses, and gives in general $\det\Phi(t) = \exp\int_0^t\nabla\cdot X\,dt'$.

> [!remark] Remark: Two exact checks
> For the free particle, a rectangle $[q_1, q_2] \times [p_1, p_2]$ becomes a parallelogram: each horizontal edge slides rigidly at speed $p/m$, the top faster than the bottom, and the height does not change, since $\dot p = 0$. For a falling ball ($\dot p = mg$ for every point) the same happens while the whole figure also moves up in $p$. In both cases base and height, and so the area, stay $(q_2 - q_1)(p_2 - p_1)$. Liouville's theorem says this is no accident of linear flows.
>
> *Source: Morin §15.5.2, Examples*

^rem-b7-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.4: Liouville's Equation
> Let a large number of copies of the system, with different initial states, be described by a density $\rho(q, p, t)$ in phase space (copies per unit phase volume). Then $\rho$ is constant along each phase trajectory, and
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \sum_{i=1}^n\Bigl(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\Bigr) = 0, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \{\rho, H\} = 0 ,
> $$
>
> with the Poisson bracket of [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]] (convention $\{f, g\} = \sum_i(\partial_{q_i}f\,\partial_{p_i}g - \partial_{p_i}f\,\partial_{q_i}g)$).
>
> *Source: H&S §11.7, eq. (11.154) · Morin §15.5.2, Remark 3*

^thm-b7-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Copies are neither created nor destroyed, so $\rho$ obeys the continuity equation for the velocity field $X$ ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-4|452 Thm. §17.4]]): $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot(\rho X) = 0$. Expanding, $\nabla\cdot(\rho X) = X\cdot\nabla\rho + \rho\,\nabla\cdot X$, and $\nabla\cdot X = 0$ (derivation of [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]]). What remains, $\partial_t\rho + X\cdot\nabla\rho = 0$, is the displayed equation, since $X = (\partial H/\partial p, -\partial H/\partial q)$, and it says that the derivative of $\rho$ along a trajectory, $\frac{d}{dt}\rho(\varphi_t(z), t)$, is zero. Directly: a small region around a moving phase point keeps its number of copies and, by Liouville's theorem, its volume, hence its density.

^der-b7-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]], [[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-4|452 Thm. §17.4]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Constant along the flow, not at a fixed point
> "The density in phase space is constant" means constant for an observer riding with the flow. At a fixed point of phase space the density changes as the cloud of copies streams past: a rectangle of free particles moves off to the right, and the density where it used to be drops to zero. A density that depends on $(q, p)$ only through $H$, $\rho = f(H)$, is stationary for a time-independent $H$, since then $X\cdot\nabla f(H) = f'(H)\,X\cdot\nabla H = 0$; this is where the ensembles of statistical mechanics start ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-1|TH Theorem §B5.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Morin §15.5.2, Remark 3 · Series II §9.2, Remark 9.2*

^cau-b7-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: What Liouville forbids, and dissipation
> Volume preservation rules out attractors: in a Hamiltonian system no open set of initial conditions can be funnelled into a point or onto a small cycle, since its volume would have to shrink. Near the pendulum's stable equilibrium, trajectories circulate on closed curves instead of spiralling in. Friction breaks this. For the damped oscillator in $(x, p = m\dot x)$, $\dot x = p/m$ and $\dot p = -kx - \gamma p$ with $\gamma = b/m$ ([[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-1|WO Theorem §B1.2.1]]), so $\nabla\cdot X = -\gamma$, and every region of the $(x, p)$ plane shrinks as $e^{-\gamma t}$ onto the rest point. Dissipation is, in this sense, the failure of Liouville's theorem in the mechanical variables. It is not the failure of every Hamiltonian description: the same damped motion has a time-dependent canonical description in which areas are conserved ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^ex-b7-2-3|Example §B7.2.3]]), but its momentum is not $m\dot x$.
>
> *Source: Series II §9.2, Remark 9.2*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B7.2.1: Phase Portrait of the Pendulum
> For the pendulum, $H = p^2/2m\ell^2 - mg\ell\cos\theta$ ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^ex-b7-1-2|Example §B7.1.2]]). With $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/\ell}$ and $P = p/(m\ell^2\omega_0)$, $H/mg\ell = \tfrac12 P^2 - \cos\theta$. [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-2|Theorem §B7.2.2]] (with $\mu = m\ell^2$, $U = -mg\ell\cos\theta$) gives the whole portrait:
> - **Centres** at $(\theta, p) = (2\pi k, 0)$, the hanging pendulum. Low-energy curves are the small-angle ellipses of Example §B7.1.2.
> - **Saddles** at $(\pi + 2\pi k, 0)$, the inverted pendulum, an unstable equilibrium.
> - **Libration**, $-mg\ell < E < mg\ell$: closed curves around the centres, the pendulum swinging back and forth between $\pm\theta_{\max}$.
> - **Rotation**, $E > mg\ell$: open wavy curves with $p$ of one sign, the pendulum going over the top; $\theta$ increases (or decreases) without bound, and the motion is periodic in $\theta$ modulo $2\pi$.
> - The **separatrix** $E = mg\ell$, the level curve through the saddles, divides the two kinds of motion:
>
> $$
> p = \pm 2m\ell^2\omega_0\cos\frac\theta2 .
> $$
>
> On it $\dot\theta = \pm 2\omega_0\cos(\theta/2)$, which integrates to $\theta(t) = 2\arcsin\bigl(\tanh\omega_0t\bigr)$ (upper branch, $\theta(0) = 0$): the pendulum approaches the inverted position as $t \to \pm\infty$ and never arrives, as [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]] requires (the exponential approach to a maximum of the potential is [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]], part 2). Correspondingly, the libration period $T(E) = dA/dE$ diverges as $E \to mg\ell$: the area enclosed by the separatrix is finite, $16\,m\ell^2\omega_0$, but grows infinitely fast as the energy reaches it; in the elliptic-integral form of [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^ex-b1-3-1|Example §B1.3.1]], $K(\sin\tfrac12\theta_{\max}) \to \infty$ as $\theta_{\max} \to \pi$.
>
> *Source: Series II §9.1, Figure 3; §7.1 (the explicit separatrix solution and the area are worked out here)*

> [!example] Example §B7.2.2: A Cloud of Pendulums
> Start many identical pendulums with initial states filling the disc of radius $0.4$ about $(\theta, P) = (0, 1.5)$ in the variables of Example §B7.2.1, and follow them with $\dot\theta = \omega_0P$, $\dot P = -\omega_0\sin\theta$. All of them librate, with energies from $E/mg\ell \approx -0.4$ to $0.8$. The higher their energy, the longer their period ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-2|Theorem §B7.2.2]]; Example §B7.1.2), so the outer part of the disc falls behind the inner part on every round: the disc is sheared into a crescent and then into a long, thin filament that winds around the centre.
>
> Integrating the boundary numerically (4800 points) and computing the enclosed area at $\omega_0t = 0, 1.5, 3, 6$ gives $\pi(0.4)^2 = 0.502655$ every time, to within one unit in the sixth digit (the residue is the error of the polygon approximating the ever thinner filament): [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]] at work. In the original variables, since $d\theta\,dp = m\ell^2\omega_0\,d\theta\,dP$, the conserved area is $0.503\,m\ell^2\omega_0$, an angular momentum (angle times angular momentum).
>
> *Source: computed here, illustrating Series II §9.2 and Morin §15.5.2*

> [!example] Example §B7.2.3: Liouville and the Damped Oscillator
> The Lagrangian $L = \tfrac12 e^{\alpha t}(\dot x^2 - \omega^2x^2)$ describes the damped oscillator $\ddot x + \alpha\dot x + \omega^2x = 0$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^ex-b6-4-2|Example §B6.4.2]]). Its canonical momentum and Hamiltonian are
>
> $$
> p = e^{\alpha t}\dot x, \qquad H(x, p, t) = \tfrac12 e^{-\alpha t}p^2 + \tfrac12 e^{\alpha t}\omega^2x^2, \qquad \dot x = e^{-\alpha t}p, \qquad \dot p = -e^{\alpha t}\omega^2x .
> $$
>
> This is a Hamiltonian system (with explicit time dependence, so $H$ is not conserved), and Liouville's theorem applies: every region of the $(x, p)$ plane keeps its area; indeed $\nabla\cdot X = \partial_x(e^{-\alpha t}p) + \partial_p(-e^{\alpha t}\omega^2x) = 0$. Yet the oscillator is damped. The two statements are about different planes. The map $(x, p) \mapsto (x, v)$ with $v = \dot x = e^{-\alpha t}p$ has Jacobian determinant $e^{-\alpha t}$, so in the $(x, v)$ plane, the plane of the mechanical state, every region shrinks as $e^{-\alpha t}$, in agreement with $\nabla_{(x,v)}\cdot(v, -\alpha v - \omega^2x) = -\alpha$ ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^rem-b7-2-4|Remark: What Liouville forbids, and dissipation]]). Liouville's theorem is a statement about canonical variables; here $p$ is not $m\dot x$, and the Lagrangian is not kinetic minus potential energy.
>
> *Source: PHY 421, Midterm 2 (Fall 2021), Problem 3 (the Lagrangian); the Hamiltonian analysis is worked out here*

> - The proof of Liouville's theorem is linear algebra and calculus: the derivative of a determinant ([[§11 The Classical Groups Are Topological Manifolds#^prop-11-1|591 Prop. §11.1]]), the change-of-variables formula ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-17|452 Thm. §15.17]]), or the divergence theorem ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-1|452 Thm. §17.1]]); the density version is the continuity equation of an incompressible flow ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-4|452 Thm. §17.4]]). Phase space as the cotangent bundle: [[§35 The Tangent Bundle#^def-35-5|591 Def. §35.5]].
> - Liouville's theorem is the starting point of statistical mechanics: ensembles and the stationarity of $\rho = f(H)$ ([[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^def-b5-4-1|TH Def. §B5.4.1]], [[§B5.4★ Liouville's Theorem, the Boltzmann Equation and the H-Theorem#^thm-b5-4-1|TH Theorem §B5.4.1]]), the microcanonical ensemble ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^def-b6-1-3|TH Def. §B6.1.3]]) and the canonical distribution ([[§B7.1 The Boltzmann Factor and the Partition Function#^thm-b7-1-1|TH Theorem §B7.1.1]]).
> - What Liouville's theorem does and does not justify in statistical mechanics: equal probabilities on the energy shell, and the limits of ergodicity ([[§B6.1 Microstates, Multiplicity and Equilibrium#^rem-b6-1-2|TH Remark: Why equal probabilities? Ergodicity and its limits]]); the fine-grained entropy is conserved, so entropy grows only after coarse-graining ([[§B6.3 Entropy, Information and the Gibbs Paradox#^rem-b6-3-3|TH Remark: Fine-grained entropy does not grow; coarse-grained entropy does]]); conserved phase-space volume makes counting states in cells of volume $h^3$ consistent ([[§B8.1 The Partition Function of the Ideal Gas#^rem-b8-1-2|TH Remark: Why h³, and why it does not matter]]).
> - Canonical transformations also preserve phase-space area ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^rem-b8-2-4|Remark: Canonical maps preserve phase-space area]]), and the time evolution is one of them ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-4|Theorem §B8.2.4]]); for one degree of freedom this gives a second, independent route to the area statement of Liouville's theorem. The bracket form $\partial_t\rho = -\{\rho, H\}$ is the classical analogue of the quantum evolution of a density operator (Quantum Mechanics level C — [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^thm-c11-2-1|QM Theorem §C11.2.1]], [[§C11.2 Density-Operator Dynamics and Quantum Statistical Mechanics#^rem-c11-2-1|QM Remark: The quantum Liouville equation]]).
> - The phase-space area $\oint p\,dq$ of a periodic orbit, over $2\pi$, is the action variable ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^ex-b8-2-3|Example §B8.2.3]]); [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-2|Theorem §B7.2.2]] says its energy derivative is the period. The old quantum theory quantized exactly this area ([[§A3.1 De Broglie's Matter Waves#^thm-a3-1-3|QM Theorem §A3.1.3]] for the circular orbit).
> - Quantum side: Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization puts one state in each cell of area $h$ between the phase curves of Theorem §B7.2.2 — [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-4|QM Theorem §B9.3.4]], [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^rem-b9-3-2|QM Remark: One state per cell of phase space, and the correspondence principle]]; a coherent state of the oscillator travels around the classical phase ellipse — [[§B4.3 Coherent States#^rem-b4-3-2|QM Remark: Why coherent states are called quasi-classical]].
