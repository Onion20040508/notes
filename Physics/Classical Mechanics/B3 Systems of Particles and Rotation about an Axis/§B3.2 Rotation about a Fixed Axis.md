*Sources: Morin, Introduction to Classical Mechanics, §8.1–8.4 · Landau & Lifshitz, Mechanics, §9.*

Level A described a rigid body turning about a fixed axis ([[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^mod-a6-1-1|Model §A6.1.1]]), defined its moment of inertia ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]]), proved $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ ([[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]]) and, for a body symmetric about the axis, $\vec L = I\vec\omega$ ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-3|Theorem §A6.6.3]]). This section drops the symmetry, lets the axis move parallel to itself, and applies the decomposition of [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles|§B3.1]]: the result is a complete set of equations for **plane motion** of a rigid body, a body all of whose points move parallel to one plane. It also derives the moments of inertia that level A quoted, with the perpendicular-axis theorem and a sum rule. Rotation about an axis whose direction changes needs the inertia tensor ([[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^def-b10-1-1|Def. §B10.1.1]]) and Euler's equations ([[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]]).

## Plane motion and the axial angular momentum

> [!model] Model §B3.2.1: Rigid Body in Plane Motion
> A rigid body (all mutual distances fixed) moves so that every point's velocity is parallel to the $xy$-plane of an inertial frame. Then
> - its configuration is fixed by three numbers: the position $(X, Y)$ of its centre of mass and an angle $\theta$ of rotation about the $z$ direction;
> - relative to any of its points, the body rotates with the same angular velocity $\vec\omega = \dot\theta\,\hat z$, about an axis parallel to $z$;
> - rotation about a fixed axis ([[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^mod-a6-1-1|Model §A6.1.1]]) is the special case of a point on the axis held fixed, and rolling on a line ([[§A6.5 Rolling|§A6.5]]) is another.
>
> The body need not be flat. A flat body in the $xy$-plane (a **lamina**, Morin's "pancake") is the case in which $\vec L$ is automatically along $\hat z$.
>
> *Fails when:* the axis of rotation tilts (tops, gyroscopes, a thrown book): then $\vec\omega$ changes direction, [[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]].
> *Source: Morin §8.1–8.2*

^mod-b3-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §B3.2.1: Moments of Inertia about the Coordinate Axes
> For a body with mass element $dm$ at $(x, y, z)$, the moments of inertia about the three coordinate axes are
>
> $$
> I_x = \int (y^2 + z^2)\,dm, \qquad I_y = \int (z^2 + x^2)\,dm, \qquad I_z = \int (x^2 + y^2)\,dm .
> $$
>
> Each is the moment of inertia of [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]] about that axis: $y^2 + z^2$ is the squared distance from the $x$-axis. They are the diagonal entries of the inertia tensor ([[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^def-b10-1-1|Def. §B10.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Morin §8.1.4, eq. (8.19)*

^def-b3-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.2: Axial Angular Momentum; Rotation about a Fixed Axis
> 1. For any system of particles, in cylindrical coordinates $(\rho_i, \varphi_i, z_i)$ about the $z$-axis through $O$,
>
> $$
> L_z = \sum_i m_i\bigl(x_i\dot y_i - y_i\dot x_i\bigr) = \sum_i m_i\,\rho_i^2\,\dot\varphi_i .
> $$
>
> 2. For a rigid body of **any** shape rotating with angular velocity $\omega$ about the fixed $z$-axis,
>
> $$
> L_z = I_z\,\omega, \qquad T = \tfrac12 I_z\,\omega^2, \qquad L_x = -\omega\int xz\,dm, \qquad L_y = -\omega\int yz\,dm .
> $$
>
> The transverse components $L_x$, $L_y$ vanish for a lamina in the $xy$-plane and for a body symmetric about the axis, but not in general.
>
> *Source: Landau §9, eq. (9.8) · Morin §8.1.1, §8.2, eqs. (8.5), (8.8), (8.20)*

^thm-b3-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Part 1.* $L_z = \sum_i m_i(\vec r_i\times\vec v_i)_z = \sum_i m_i(x_i\dot y_i - y_i\dot x_i)$ by the component formula for the cross product. With $x = \rho\cos\varphi$, $y = \rho\sin\varphi$,
>
> $$
> x\dot y - y\dot x = \rho\cos\varphi\,(\dot\rho\sin\varphi + \rho\dot\varphi\cos\varphi) - \rho\sin\varphi\,(\dot\rho\cos\varphi - \rho\dot\varphi\sin\varphi) = \rho^2\dot\varphi .
> $$
>
> *Part 2.* In a rigid rotation about the $z$-axis every point keeps its $\rho_i$ and $z_i$ and has $\dot\varphi_i = \omega$, so $L_z = \bigl(\sum_i m_i\rho_i^2\bigr)\omega = I_z\omega$ with no condition on the shape. Its velocity is $\vec v = \omega\hat z\times\vec r = \omega(-y, x, 0)$, so $v^2 = \omega^2\rho^2$ gives $T = \tfrac12 I_z\omega^2$, and
>
> $$
> L_x = \sum_i m_i(y_iv_{z,i} - z_iv_{y,i}) = -\omega\sum_i m_ix_iz_i, \qquad L_y = \sum_i m_i(z_iv_{x,i} - x_iv_{z,i}) = -\omega\sum_i m_iy_iz_i .
> $$
>
> For a lamina $z \equiv 0$. For a body symmetric about the $z$-axis the point $(-x, -y, z)$ carries the same mass as $(x, y, z)$, so both sums cancel in pairs. Sums become integrals for a continuous body.

^der-b3-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-4|Theorem §A1.5.4]] (cross product in components), [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^mod-a6-1-1|Model §A6.1.1]], [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^def-b3-2-1|Def. §B3.2.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]] (product rule)

> [!caution] Caution: $\vec L$ need not lie along the axis
> Level A proved $\vec L = I\vec\omega$ only for a body symmetric about the axis. A rod mounted on an axle through its centre but tilted to it has $L_x, L_y \neq 0$; as the rod turns, $(L_x, L_y)$ turns with it, so $\vec L$ changes even at constant $\omega$ and the bearings must supply a torque ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]). Morin calls the nonzero transverse components "strange but true". The fixed-axis results of this chapter use only $L_z = I_z\omega$; the full $\vec L$ is $\mathsf I\vec\omega$ ([[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]]), with the inertia tensor of [[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^def-b10-1-1|Def. §B10.1.1]], whose off-diagonal entries are the integrals $-\int xz\,dm$ and $-\int yz\,dm$ above.

^cau-b3-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.3: Angular Momentum and Kinetic Energy in Plane Motion
> A rigid body in plane motion ([[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^mod-b3-2-1|Model §B3.2.1]]), with centre of mass at $\vec R = (X, Y)$ moving with $\vec V$ and angular velocity $\omega\hat z$, has, about a fixed origin $O$,
>
> $$
> L_z = M\,(XV_y - YV_x) + I_{\text{cm}}\,\omega, \qquad T = \tfrac12 MV^2 + \tfrac12 I_{\text{cm}}\,\omega^2 ,
> $$
>
> where $I_{\text{cm}}$ is the moment of inertia about the axis through the centre of mass parallel to $z$. In particular (**parallel-axis theorem**), for rotation about a fixed axis at perpendicular distance $D$ from the centre of mass, parallel to an axis through it,
>
> $$
> I = I_{\text{cm}} + MD^2 ,
> $$
>
> for any body, flat or not, and only with the centre-of-mass axis as reference.
>
> *Source: Morin §8.1.2–8.1.3, §8.2, Theorems 8.1–8.2, eqs. (8.9)–(8.14)*

^thm-b3-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]], $L_z = (\vec R\times M\vec V)_z + L_{\text{cm},z}$ and $T = \tfrac12 MV^2 + T_{\text{cm}}$. Relative to the centre of mass the body rotates rigidly with $\omega$ about the axis through the centre of mass parallel to $z$, so [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]], applied to the relative coordinates at each instant, gives $L_{\text{cm},z} = I_{\text{cm}}\omega$ and $T_{\text{cm}} = \tfrac12 I_{\text{cm}}\omega^2$.
>
> For rotation about a fixed axis through $O$ the centre of mass moves on a circle of radius $D$ with $\vec V = \omega\hat z\times\vec R$, so $(\vec R\times M\vec V)_z = MD^2\omega$ and $L_z = (I_{\text{cm}} + MD^2)\,\omega$. Comparing with $L_z = I\omega$ of [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]] gives the parallel-axis theorem; the energy gives the same, $T = \tfrac12(MD^2 + I_{\text{cm}})\omega^2$. The cross terms vanished in [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] only because the reference point was the centre of mass.

^der-b3-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]], [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]], [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^mod-b3-2-1|Model §B3.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two proofs of the parallel-axis theorem
> Level A proved the parallel-axis theorem by expanding $|\vec d + \vec\rho_j|^2$ ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-3|Theorem §A6.2.3]]); here it is the special case of the angular-momentum split in which the centre of mass circles the axis at the body's own rate (Morin pictures a stick glued across the body, pivoted at its far end). The same cross term vanishes in both proofs, $\sum_j m_j\vec\rho_j = \vec 0$, which is why both need the centre-of-mass axis. Between two axes neither of which passes through the centre of mass, go through $I_{\text{cm}}$: for a stick, $I$ about a point $\ell/6$ from an end differs from $I_{\text{end}}$ by $m\bigl[(\ell/2)^2 - (\ell/3)^2\bigr] = \tfrac{5}{36}m\ell^2$, not by $m(\ell/6)^2$. The tensor form shifts all three axes at once and adds the products of inertia $-MD_jD_k$: [[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]], of which the scalar theorem is the diagonal.

## Computing moments of inertia

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.4: Perpendicular-Axis Theorem; Sum Rule
> 1. For a lamina in the $xy$-plane,
>
> $$
> I_z = I_x + I_y .
> $$
>
> 2. For any body, with $r^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ the squared distance from the origin,
>
> $$
> I_x + I_y + I_z = 2\int r^2\,dm .
> $$
>
> Part 1 fails for bodies of finite thickness; the parallel-axis theorem does not need flatness.
>
> *Source: Morin §8.1.4, eqs. (8.18)–(8.19); §8.3.1, eq. (8.31)*

^thm-b3-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Add the integrands of [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^def-b3-2-1|Def. §B3.2.1]]: $(y^2 + z^2) + (z^2 + x^2) + (x^2 + y^2) = 2r^2$, and integrals add. For a lamina $z \equiv 0$, so $I_x = \int y^2\,dm$, $I_y = \int x^2\,dm$ and $I_z = \int(x^2 + y^2)\,dm = I_x + I_y$.

^der-b3-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^def-b3-2-1|Def. §B3.2.1]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-3|452 Thm. §15.3]] (additivity in the integrand)

![[ph-cm-b3-2-1.svg]]
*Perpendicular-axis theorem. A mass element $dm$ of a lamina in the $xy$-plane lies at distance $|y|$ from the $x$-axis (green), $|x|$ from the $y$-axis (orange) and $\rho$ from the $z$-axis (red); $\rho^2 = x^2 + y^2$ is the whole proof. For a body of finite thickness the distances to the $x$- and $y$-axes also contain $z^2$, and the theorem fails.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.5: Moments of Inertia of Standard Bodies
> For uniform bodies of mass $M$:
>
> | Body | Axis | $I$ |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | ring, radius $R$ | a diameter | $\tfrac12 MR^2$ |
> | disc, radius $R$ | a diameter | $\tfrac14 MR^2$ |
> | rectangular lamina, sides $a$, $b$ | normal, through the centre | $\tfrac1{12}M(a^2 + b^2)$ |
> | regular $N$-gon lamina, circumradius $R$ | normal, through the centre | $\tfrac12 MR^2\bigl(1 - \tfrac23\sin^2\tfrac{\pi}{N}\bigr)$ |
> | thin spherical shell, radius $R$ | a diameter | $\tfrac23 MR^2$ |
> | solid sphere, radius $R$ | a diameter | $\tfrac25 MR^2$ |
>
> The $N$-gon values $\tfrac14, \tfrac13, \tfrac5{12}$ for $N = 3, 4, 6$ approach the disc's $\tfrac12$ as $N \to \infty$. Rods, hoops and discs about their symmetry axes are in [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^rem-a6-2-2|the level-A table]].
>
> *Source: Morin §8.3.1, Examples 2, 4, 7, 8, 10–12, eqs. (8.25)–(8.37)*

^thm-b3-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Ring and disc about a diameter.* By symmetry $I_x = I_y$, so the perpendicular-axis theorem gives $I_x = \tfrac12 I_z$: $\tfrac12 MR^2$ from the ring's $I_z = MR^2$ and $\tfrac14 MR^2$ from the disc's $I_z = \tfrac12 MR^2$ ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]]). Directly for the ring: an element at angle $\theta$ has $dm = (M/2\pi)\,d\theta$ at distance $R|\sin\theta|$ from the diameter, and $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi$.
>
> *Rectangle.* A rectangle is a row of rods across each axis in its plane, so $I_x = \tfrac1{12}Mb^2$ and $I_y = \tfrac1{12}Ma^2$ (the extent along an axis does not affect the moment about it); add them.
>
> *$N$-gon.* Cut it into $N$ isosceles triangles with apex at the centre, apex angle $2\beta = 2\pi/N$ and altitude $h = R\cos\beta$. With surface density $\sigma$, slice a triangle into strips parallel to its base: the strip at distance $x$ from the apex has length $\ell = 2x\tan\beta$ and mass $dm = 2\sigma x\tan\beta\,dx$, and by the rod formula and the parallel-axis theorem it contributes $dm\,(\ell^2/12 + x^2)$ about the apex:
>
> $$
> I_\triangle = \int_0^h 2\sigma\tan\beta\Bigl(1 + \frac{\tan^2\beta}{3}\Bigr)x^3\,dx = \frac{M_\triangle h^2}{2}\Bigl(1 + \frac{\tan^2\beta}{3}\Bigr) = \frac{M_\triangle R^2}{2}\Bigl(1 - \frac23\sin^2\beta\Bigr),
> $$
>
> using $M_\triangle = \sigma h^2\tan\beta$ and $h = R\cos\beta$. All $N$ triangles share the axis, so their moments add, with $\sum M_\triangle = M$.
>
> *Spherical shell.* Every mass element is at distance $R$ from the centre, so the sum rule gives $I_x + I_y + I_z = 2MR^2$, and by symmetry each is $\tfrac23 MR^2$. (Directly: rings at polar angle $\theta$ have radius $R\sin\theta$ and mass $\tfrac12 M\sin\theta\,d\theta$, and $\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta = \tfrac43$.)
>
> *Solid sphere.* Nest shells of radius $r$ and mass $dm = 3Mr^2\,dr/R^3$:
>
> $$
> I = \int_0^R \tfrac23 r^2\,\frac{3Mr^2}{R^3}\,dr = \frac{2M}{R^3}\,\frac{R^5}{5} = \tfrac25 MR^2 .
> $$

^der-b3-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]], [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]] (parallel axes), [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-20|452 Thm. §15.20]] (change of variables; slicing into shells and rings), [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-4|452 Thm. §15.4]] (additivity over domains)

> [!remark] Remark: Moments of inertia from scaling
> Some moments of inertia need no integral. For a uniform stick of length $\ell$, $I = \int x^2\rho\,dx$ has three powers of length, so a stick of length $2\ell$ (same density) has $8$ times the moment about its centre. But it is also two sticks of length $\ell$ joined at the axis, each turning about its end: $8I_{\text{cm}}(\ell) = 2I_{\text{end}}(\ell)$, so $I_{\text{end}} = 4I_{\text{cm}}$. The parallel-axis theorem supplies the one dimensionful input, $I_{\text{end}} = I_{\text{cm}} + M(\ell/2)^2$; together, $3I_{\text{cm}} = M\ell^2/4$ and $I_{\text{cm}} = \tfrac1{12}M\ell^2$. Scaling alone gives only homogeneous linear relations among the $I$'s; the parallel-axis theorem fixes the scale. The same trick handles self-similar (fractal) bodies.
>
> *Source: Morin §8.3.2; Problem 8.8*

## Equations of plane motion

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.6: Equations of Plane Motion of a Rigid Body
> A rigid body in plane motion, with internal forces obeying the strong third law, obeys
>
> $$
> M\ddot X = F_x^{\text{ext}}, \qquad M\ddot Y = F_y^{\text{ext}}, \qquad I_{\text{cm}}\,\ddot\theta = \tau_{\text{cm},z}^{\text{ext}},
> $$
>
> with the torque taken about the centre of mass, even though it accelerates. If a point $O$ of the body is held fixed (a pivot), the single equation $I_O\ddot\theta = \tau_{O,z}^{\text{ext}}$ about the pivot, which does not contain the pivot force, can replace the torque equation about the centre of mass. About any other fixed point, $dL_z/dt = \tau_z^{\text{ext}}$ holds with $L_z$ from [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]], not with $I\omega$.
>
> *Source: Morin §8.4.3, eqs. (8.46)–(8.47)*

^thm-b3-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The first two equations are [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]. For the third, take the $z$-component of $d\vec L_{\text{cm}}/dt = \vec\tau_{\text{cm}}^{\text{ext}}$, condition 2 of [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], and use $L_{\text{cm},z} = I_{\text{cm}}\omega$ with $I_{\text{cm}}$ constant (the body is rigid and the axis keeps its direction), from [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]]. About a fixed pivot $O$ condition 1 applies, and $L_{O,z} = I_O\omega$ by [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]. About a fixed point that is not a pivot the body does not rotate about it, so $L_z$ has the orbital term $M(XV_y - YV_x)$ as well.

^der-b3-2-6

*Uses:* [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]], [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]], [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: What this adds to τ = Iα
> [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]] covered a fixed axis. Theorem §B3.2.6 gives three equations for the three coordinates $X, Y, \theta$ of any plane motion; with constraint equations (rolling, strings) and constraint forces as further unknowns they determine the motion, as in [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^ex-b3-1-1|Example §B3.1.1]]. Morin's advice: compute torques and angular momenta about the centre of mass or a fixed point, nothing else. The two choices give equations that differ by a combination of the force equations, so one may pick the point that eliminates an unknown force. A third route is energy: [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]] gives $T$, and if no force does net work (rolling without slipping), $T + U$ is conserved; Morin finds this the quickest route in the more complicated rolling problems.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.2.1: Removing a Support
> A rod of mass $m$ and length $2r$, with $I_{\text{cm}} = \beta mr^2$, rests horizontally on two supports, each at distance $d$ from its centre. The right support is suddenly removed. What force $F$ does the left support exert immediately afterwards?
>
> At that instant the rod is at rest, so the centre of mass, at distance $d$ from the left support, has only a tangential acceleration $a = d\alpha$, downward. Three equations (centre of mass, torque about the support, torque about the centre of mass) contain the two unknowns $F$ and $\alpha$:
>
> $$
> mg - F = ma, \qquad mgd = (\beta mr^2 + md^2)\,\alpha, \qquad Fd = \beta mr^2\,\alpha .
> $$
>
> Dividing the last two gives at once
>
> $$
> F = \frac{mg}{1 + d^2/(\beta r^2)} .
> $$
>
> For a uniform rod on supports at its ends ($\beta = \tfrac13$, $d = r$), $F = mg/4$ and $a = \tfrac34 g$; the free end falls with $2a = \tfrac32 g > g$. Limits check: a dumbbell with masses at the ends ($\beta = 1$, $d = r$) gives $mg/2$; a support under the centre ($d \to 0$) gives $mg$; a support far out on a massless extension ($d \gg \sqrt\beta\,r$) gives $F \to 0$.
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 8.10*

^ex-b3-2-1

> [!example] Example §B3.2.2: The Falling Stick, Two Ways
> A massless rod of length $b$ is pivoted at one end; its other end is glued to the middle of a uniform stick (mass $m$, length $\ell$), perpendicular to it. The pivot lets the rod swing about a horizontal axis perpendicular to the rod. The system is released from rest with the rod horizontal, in two orientations.
>
> - *Stick horizontal, parallel to the pivot axis.* Every element of the stick is at distance $b$ from the axis: $I = mb^2$, whatever $\ell$ is. The torque about the pivot is $mgb$, so $\alpha = g/b$ and the centre of mass starts down with $b\alpha = g$. The pivot exerts no force at first: it "doesn't know right away that the stick is moving."
> - *Stick vertical, in the plane of the swing.* Now $I = mb^2 + \tfrac1{12}m\ell^2$ (parallel axes), and
>
> $$
> a_{\text{cm}} = b\alpha = \frac{g}{1 + \ell^2/(12b^2)} ,
> $$
>
> which tends to $g$ for $\ell \ll b$ and to $0$ for $\ell \gg b$, when the ends of the stick move far for a small motion of the centre of mass.
>
> Same body, same pivot, same torque, different $I$: what counts is the distance of each element from the axis, and the stick's extent *along* the axis (first case) adds nothing ([[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^def-b3-2-1|Def. §B3.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 8.11*
