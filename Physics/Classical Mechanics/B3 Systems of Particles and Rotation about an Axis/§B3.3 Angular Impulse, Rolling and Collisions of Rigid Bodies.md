Level A met impulse as the time integral of a force ([[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]), rolling only without slipping ([[§A6.5 Rolling#^def-a6-5-1|Def. §A6.5.1]]), and collisions with rotating bodies only for a body on a fixed axle ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^ex-a6-6-3|Example §A6.6.3]]). This section adds the rotational counterpart of impulse, which relates the changes of $\vec L$ and $\vec p$ even when the force is unknown, and uses it, together with the equations of plane motion of [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis|§B3.2]], for three things: a free body struck by a blow (with the centre of percussion), the transition from sliding to rolling, and collisions between particles and free rigid bodies.

## Angular impulse

> [!definition] Definition §B3.3.1: Angular Impulse
> The **angular impulse** about a point $A$ of the external torques acting from $t_1$ to $t_2$ is
>
> $$
> \vec J_\theta = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\vec\tau_A^{\text{ext}}(t)\,dt .
> $$
>
> It is the rotational counterpart of the impulse $\vec J = \int\vec F\,dt$ ([[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^def-a5-1-1|Def. §A5.1.1]]); its unit is $\mathrm{N\cdot m\cdot s} = \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s}$. (Morin writes $I$ and $I_\theta$ for the two impulses.)
>
> *Source: Morin §8.6, eq. (8.59)*

^def-b3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.1: Angular Impulse–Momentum Theorem
> About a fixed point or the centre of mass (any point allowed by [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]), the angular impulse equals the change of angular momentum:
>
> $$
> \vec J_\theta = \Delta\vec L_A .
> $$
>
> If a single force acts, always at the same position $\vec\rho$ relative to $A$, then
>
> $$
> \Delta\vec L_A = \vec\rho\times\Delta\vec p ,
> $$
>
> with $\Delta\vec p$ the momentum it imparts, and $|\Delta\vec L_A| = \rho_\perp\,|\Delta\vec p|$ when the lever arm is perpendicular to the force. This holds however $\vec F(t)$ varies in time.
>
> *Source: Morin §8.6, eqs. (8.58)–(8.61)*

^thm-b3-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Integrate $d\vec L_A/dt = \vec\tau_A^{\text{ext}}$ from $t_1$ to $t_2$ ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]], componentwise). For a single force at fixed $\vec\rho$, $\vec\tau_A = \vec\rho\times\vec F(t)$ and the constant $\vec\rho$ comes out of the integral: $\int\vec\rho\times\vec F\,dt = \vec\rho\times\int\vec F\,dt = \vec\rho\times\Delta\vec p$ by [[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]].

^der-b3-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], [[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]], [[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^def-b3-3-1|Def. §B3.3.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where the content is
> Both impulse theorems are integrals of the equations of motion and say nothing new by themselves; Morin calls them "definitions, devoid of any content". The content is the ratio. Even when nothing is known about $\vec F(t)$ or its duration, so that neither $\Delta\vec p$ nor $\Delta\vec L$ can be computed, their relation $\Delta\vec L = \vec\rho\times\Delta\vec p$ is known. Morin's limerick ends: "He wrote down the right guess / For their quotient: the lever arm's $\ell$."

^rem-b3-3-1

> [!model] Model §B3.3.2: Impulsive Force
> A force is **impulsive** if it acts for a time $\Delta t$ so short that
> - its impulse $\vec J = \int\vec F\,dt$ stays finite as $\Delta t \to 0$ (the force is huge);
> - the positions of all bodies do not change during $\Delta t$, while velocities and angular velocities jump;
> - every finite force (gravity, a spring, a normal force of ordinary size, kinetic friction against a surface) contributes an impulse of order $F\Delta t \to 0$ and can be ignored during the blow.
>
> Constraint forces that prevent a jump, such as a pivot, a rigid floor, or the glue between two bodies that stick, become impulsive themselves and must be kept.
>
> *Valid when:* the contact time is short compared with the time over which the configuration changes. *Fails when:* the "collision" lasts, like the friction of [[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^thm-b3-3-4|Theorem §B3.3.4]], which acts over an extended time, though the impulse relations still hold there.
> *Source: Morin §8.6 (Striking a stick: "the stick doesn't have time to move much while the hammer is in contact"); level-A form: [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^cau-a5-2-1|Caution in §A5.2]]*

## A blow on a free rigid body

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.3: Blow on a Rigid Body; Centre of Percussion
> A rigid body of mass $M$ in plane motion, with $I_{\text{cm}} = Mk^2$ ($k$ the **radius of gyration**), receives an impulsive blow $J$ ([[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^mod-b3-3-2|Model §B3.3.2]]) at a point $S$, along a line of action at perpendicular distance $b$ from the centre of mass.
> 1. The velocity of the centre of mass jumps by $J/M$ and the angular velocity by $\Delta\omega = Jb/(Mk^2)$. On the line through the centre of mass perpendicular to the blow, the point at signed distance $x$ from the centre of mass (positive toward $S$) changes its velocity by
>
> $$
> \Delta v(x) = \frac JM\Bigl(1 + \frac{bx}{k^2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> so the point at distance $k^2/b$ on the far side of the centre of mass does not move at all: a body struck from rest starts to turn about it.
> 2. If the body is held at a point $P$ at distance $d$ from the centre of mass (a pivot or a hand), and struck perpendicular to $PC$ at distance $s$ from $P$, the impulsive reaction at $P$ is
>
> $$
> J_P = J\Bigl(\frac{s}{s_*} - 1\Bigr), \qquad s_* = d + \frac{k^2}{d} = \frac{I_P}{Md} ,
> $$
>
> along the blow when positive. The reaction vanishes when the blow lands at the **centre of percussion** relative to $P$, at distance $s_{\ast}$ from $P$. Then $s_{\ast}$ is the length of the simple pendulum with the period of the body swinging about $P$ ([[§A1.4 Pendulums#^thm-a1-4-3|WO Theorem §A1.4.3]]), and $P$ and the centre of percussion are conjugate: $(s_{\ast} - d)\,d = k^2$.
>
> *Source: Morin §8.6, Example (Striking a stick); Exercises 8.68–8.69*

^thm-b3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Part 1.* By the impulse theorem for the centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]] integrated), $M\,\Delta V = J$. About the centre of mass the blow has lever arm $b$, so by [[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]] and $L_{\text{cm},z} = I_{\text{cm}}\omega$ ([[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]]), $Mk^2\,\Delta\omega = Jb$. A point at distance $x$ from the centre of mass on the perpendicular line has velocity $\vec V + \vec\omega\times\vec x$, whose component along the blow changes by $\Delta V + x\,\Delta\omega = (J/M)(1 + bx/k^2)$, which vanishes at $x = -k^2/b$.
>
> *Part 2.* Take angular momentum about the fixed point $P$. The reaction $J_P$ acts at $P$ and has no torque about it, so $I_P\,\Delta\omega = Js$, and the centre of mass, which circles $P$ at distance $d$, gets $\Delta V = d\,\Delta\omega = Jsd/I_P$. The total impulse on the body is $J + J_P = M\,\Delta V = JsMd/I_P = Js/s_{\ast}$, which gives $J_P$. With $I_P = M(k^2 + d^2)$ (parallel axes), $s_{\ast} = d + k^2/d$. Equivalently: by part 1, the point at rest after a blow at distance $b = s - d$ beyond the centre of mass is at $k^2/b$ on the other side; it is $P$ exactly when $(s - d)d = k^2$, and then the hand at $P$ feels nothing. A physical pendulum about $P$ has $\omega^2 = Mgd/I_P = g/s_{\ast}$.

*Uses:* [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]], [[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]], [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]], [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-6|Theorem §B3.2.6]], [[§A1.4 Pendulums#^thm-a1-4-3|WO Theorem §A1.4.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: The struck stick
> A uniform stick of length $\ell$ ($k^2 = \ell^2/12$), at rest, is struck perpendicularly at one end ($b = \ell/2$), and its centre then moves at $v$. Part 1 gives $\omega = 6v/\ell$ and $\Delta v(x) = v(1 + 6x/\ell)$: the struck end moves at $4v$, the far end at $-2v$ (backward), and the point $\ell/6$ from the centre on the far side, $2\ell/3$ from the struck end, stays at rest. Conversely (part 2), a stick held at one end ($d = \ell/2$, $I_P = \tfrac13 M\ell^2$) has its centre of percussion at $s_* = 2\ell/3$ from the hand: a ball hit there does not sting. The two statements are the conjugacy of the two points. For a solid equilateral triangle of side $a$ held at its top vertex, plane vertical, and struck perpendicular to its plane on the vertical altitude (Morin Exercise 8.69), the axis of rotation lies in the plane: $I_{\text{cm}} = Ma^2/24$ about any axis through the centre in the plane of the triangle, and $d = a/\sqrt3$, so $s_* = a/\sqrt3 + \sqrt3\,a/24 = \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{8}a$ from the vertex.

## From sliding to rolling

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.4: From Sliding to Rolling
> A ball or cylinder of mass $M$, radius $R$ and $I_{\text{cm}} = \beta MR^2$ moves on a fixed horizontal surface with friction, starting with centre velocity $V_0$ and angular velocity $\omega_0$ (positive in the sense of rolling forward), so that its contact point slips with velocity $s_0 = V_0 - R\omega_0 \neq 0$. Friction acts until slipping stops. Whatever the friction law,
>
> $$
> V_f = R\omega_f = \frac{V_0 + \beta R\omega_0}{1 + \beta}, \qquad T_0 - T_f = \frac{\beta}{1 + \beta}\,\tfrac12 M s_0^2 .
> $$
>
> For constant kinetic friction $\mu Mg$, slipping lasts $t_{\ast} = \dfrac{\beta\,|s_0|}{(1 + \beta)\,\mu g}$, and the work done by friction is $-\mu Mg$ times the distance the surfaces **slide** over each other, $\tfrac12|s_0|t_{\ast}$, which equals $-(T_0 - T_f)$. It is not $-\mu Mg$ times the distance moved by the centre.
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 8.24 (the case $\omega_0 = 0$); Exercise 8.48*

^thm-b3-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Angular momentum about a fixed point $O$ on the surface.* Friction acts along the surface, through $O$'s line, so it has no torque about $O$. The normal force $N$ at the contact point and the weight $Mg$ at the centre lie on the same vertical line and are equal and opposite (no vertical acceleration), so their torques cancel. Hence $L_O$ is conserved, and by [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]] (centre at height $R$),
>
> $$
> MV_0R + \beta MR^2\omega_0 = MV_fR + \beta MR^2\omega_f, \qquad \omega_f = V_f/R ,
> $$
>
> which gives $V_f$. (Morin equivalently writes $F = Ma$ and $-FR = \beta MR^2\alpha$ for the friction force $F$, so that $a = -\beta R\alpha$ at every instant whatever $F(t)$ is, and integrates.) No property of the friction force was used except that it acts at the contact point along the surface.
>
> *Energy.* With $w_0 = R\omega_0$, the loss is
>
> $$
> \tfrac12 M\Bigl[V_0^2 + \beta w_0^2 - \frac{(V_0 + \beta w_0)^2}{1 + \beta}\Bigr] = \frac{\tfrac12 M}{1 + \beta}\Bigl[\beta V_0^2 - 2\beta V_0w_0 + \beta w_0^2\Bigr] = \frac{\beta}{1 + \beta}\,\tfrac12 M(V_0 - w_0)^2 .
> $$
>
> *Constant friction.* While $s = V - R\omega > 0$ (the other sign is symmetric), $\dot V = -\mu g$ and $\beta MR^2\dot\omega = \mu MgR$, so $\dot s = -\mu g(1 + 1/\beta)$ and $s$ falls linearly to zero at $t_{\ast}$. The power of friction is the force times the velocity of the material point it acts on, $-\mu Mg\,s$ ([[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]), so its work is $-\mu Mg\int_0^{t_{\ast}}s\,dt = -\mu Mg\cdot\tfrac12 s_0t_{\ast}$, and substituting $t_{\ast}$ gives $-\tfrac{\beta}{1+\beta}\tfrac12 Ms_0^2$. Friction's work on the centre-of-mass motion, $-\mu Mg$ times the centre's displacement, is the change of $\tfrac12 MV^2$ alone ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]]); the spin gains energy from the same force.

^der-b3-3-4

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]], [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]], [[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]], [[§A3.8 Friction#^law-a3-8-1|Law §A3.8.1]], [[§A6.5 Rolling#^def-a6-5-1|Def. §A6.5.1]]

![[ph-cm-b3-3-2.svg]]
*Sliding to rolling for a solid sphere ($\beta = \tfrac25$) with constant kinetic friction. Friction slows the centre (blue, $V$) and spins up the rim speed (red, $R\omega$) until they meet; from then on the sphere rolls. Left: launched without spin, it ends at $\tfrac57V_0$ after $t_{\ast} = \tfrac27V_0/\mu g$. Right: launched with backspin $R\omega_0 = -V_0$, it ends at $\tfrac37V_0$ after twice as long.*

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the result
> - With $\omega_0 = 0$: $V_f = V_0/(1 + \beta)$ and the loss is the fraction $\beta/(1 + \beta)$ of $T_0$. For $\beta \to 0$ (all mass at the centre) nothing is lost; for $\beta \to \infty$ (a spool sliding on a thin axle) everything is, like a block that cannot rotate. With constant friction the centre moves $d = \dfrac{\beta(2 + \beta)}{(1 + \beta)^2}\dfrac{V_0^2}{2\mu g}$ while slipping, more than the sliding distance $\dfrac{\beta}{1 + \beta}\dfrac{V_0^2}{2\mu g}$; using $d$ in the work gives a wrong energy balance (Morin's warning).
> - Backspin can make $V_f$ zero or negative: $V_0 + \beta R\omega_0 = 0$ stops the body dead, which is how a coin launched along a table with backspin $\omega_0 = -2V_0/R$ ($\beta = \tfrac12$) comes to rest, after $d = V_0^2/(2\mu g)$, translation and rotation stopping together (Morin Exercise 8.48). More backspin brings it back.
> - Since $V_f$ is fixed by the conserved $L_O$, the final state does not depend on how friction varies along the way, only on its acting at the contact point.

## Collisions involving rigid bodies

> [!remark] Remark: Which conservation laws, about which point
> In a collision between free bodies (no impulsive external forces), the total momentum and the total angular momentum are conserved; kinetic energy only if the collision is elastic. A rotating body has one more velocity, $\omega$, and conservation of angular momentum supplies one more equation, so a well-posed problem still closes. Angular momentum must be taken about a fixed point or the centre of mass of the **whole system** ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]). The centre of a struck stick is not allowed: it accelerates during the blow. Within that freedom choose the point that kills the most terms, for instance the fixed point where the centre of mass of a body happens to be at the moment of impact, which makes its orbital term $\vec R\times M\vec V$ vanish when $\vec V$ points away from it. Energy can disappear into heat, the random motion of molecules. Momentum cannot hide in microscopic motion, since $\vec P = M\vec V$. Neither can the angular momentum of a rigid body, since it is $I\omega$ (Morin §8.5).

^rem-b3-3-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.5: Elastic Collision of a Particle with a Free Stick
> A particle of mass $M$ moving with speed $V_0$ hits a free stick (mass $m$, $I_{\text{cm}} = I$, at rest, centre of mass at its centre) perpendicularly at distance $d$ from its centre, and the collision is elastic. Then:
> 1. The relative velocity of the particle and the **contact point of the stick** reverses: $(v + \omega d) - V = V_0$, where $V$ is the particle's velocity after, and $v$, $\omega$ are the stick's centre velocity and angular velocity.
> 2. At the contact point the stick responds like a free particle of **effective mass**
>
> $$
> m_{\text{eff}} = \Bigl(\frac1m + \frac{d^2}{I}\Bigr)^{-1} ,
> $$
>
> so that
>
> $$
> V = \frac{M - m_{\text{eff}}}{M + m_{\text{eff}}}\,V_0, \qquad v = \frac{M(V_0 - V)}{m} = \frac{2V_0}{1 + m/M + md^2/I}, \qquad \omega = \frac{md}{I}\,v .
> $$
>
> *Source: Morin Problems 8.18–8.19; §8.5, Example (Elastic collision)*

^thm-b3-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Conservation of momentum, of angular momentum about the fixed point at the stick's initial centre (the stick's centre of mass moves straight away from it, so the stick contributes only its spin), and of energy:
>
> $$
> MV_0 = MV + mv, \qquad MV_0d = MVd + I\omega, \qquad MV_0^2 = MV^2 + mv^2 + I\omega^2 .
> $$
>
> The first two give $mvd = I\omega$, so $I\omega^2 = mv\,(\omega d)$ and the energy equation reads $M(V_0 - V)(V_0 + V) = mv\,(v + \omega d)$. Dividing by the momentum equation, $M(V_0 - V) = mv$, leaves $V_0 + V = v + \omega d$: part 1. (The trivial root $V = V_0$, $v = \omega = 0$, the "no collision" solution, is excluded by the division.)
>
> For part 2: by [[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]], an impulse $J$ at the contact point changes the contact point's velocity by $J/m + Jd^2/I = J/m_{\text{eff}}$, exactly as for a particle of mass $m_{\text{eff}}$. With the relative-velocity reversal of part 1, the collision is a one-dimensional elastic collision of $M$ with $m_{\text{eff}}$ at rest, and [[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^thm-a5-3-2|Theorem §A5.3.2]] gives $V$; then $v$ and $\omega$ follow from the first two conservation laws. Substituting $m_{\text{eff}}$, $v = 2Mm_{\text{eff}}V_0/\bigl[m(M + m_{\text{eff}})\bigr]$ simplifies to the form stated, which is Morin's.

^der-b3-3-5

*Uses:* [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-4|Theorem §A6.6.4]], [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]], [[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]], [[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^thm-a5-3-2|Theorem §A5.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Checks
> - Hit at the centre ($d = 0$): $m_{\text{eff}} = m$ and the particle formulas of level A return. Hit far out ($md^2 \gg I$): $m_{\text{eff}} \approx I/d^2$, smaller and smaller, and the stick gets away easily.
> - Morin's example: equal masses, uniform stick of length $\ell$ ($I = m\ell^2/12$). For the particle and the stick's centre to leave with equal speeds, momentum forces $V = v = V_0/2$, so $M - m_{\text{eff}} = \tfrac12(M + m_{\text{eff}})$, $m_{\text{eff}} = m/3$, $md^2/I = 2$ and $d = \ell/\sqrt6$, with $\omega = md\,v/I = \sqrt6\,V_0/\ell$, as Morin finds from the three conservation laws. After half a turn the stick's far side meets the particle again; that second collision leaves the stick at rest and the particle moving on at $V_0$, the other solution of the same conservation equations.
> - Part 1 is the rigid-body version of the reversal of relative velocity in one-dimensional elastic collisions ([[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^thm-a5-3-2|Theorem §A5.3.2]]); Morin points to it ("Theorem 5.3").

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.3.1: Where to Strike a Pool Ball
> At what height should a pool ball (radius $R$, $I = \tfrac25 mR^2$) be struck horizontally so that it immediately rolls without slipping?
>
> During the short blow the table's friction is a finite force and gives no impulse ([[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^mod-b3-3-2|Model §B3.3.2]]). A cue impulse $J$ at height $h$ above the centre gives $V = J/m$ and, by [[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]] about the centre, $\tfrac25 mR^2\omega = Jh$. Rolling requires $V = R\omega$:
>
> $$
> \frac Jm = \frac{5Jh}{2mR} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = \tfrac25 R ,
> $$
>
> that is, $\tfrac75R$ above the table. This is part 1 of [[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]] once more: the contact point, at distance $R$ below the centre, must be the point left at rest, so $R = k^2/h$ with $k^2 = \tfrac25 R^2$. Struck lower, the ball starts with backspin relative to rolling and [[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^thm-b3-3-4|Theorem §B3.3.4]] takes over; struck at the centre it starts by sliding, and ends at $\tfrac57$ of its initial speed.
>
> *Source: Morin Exercise 8.67*

^ex-b3-3-1

> [!example] Example §B3.3.2: A Mass Sticks to the End of a Stick
> A particle of mass $m$ moving at $v_0$ perpendicular to a free uniform stick (mass $m$, length $\ell$, at rest) hits one end and sticks. Find the angular velocity afterwards.
>
> The combined centre of mass is $\ell/4$ from the struck end, and moves on at $v_0/2$ by momentum conservation. About it the system has, with parallel axes,
>
> $$
> I_{\text{cm}} = \Bigl[\frac{m\ell^2}{12} + m\Bigl(\frac\ell4\Bigr)^2\Bigr] + m\Bigl(\frac\ell4\Bigr)^2 = \frac5{24}m\ell^2 .
> $$
>
> Three legal origins give the same answer:
> - *the fixed point where the system's centre of mass is at impact:* $mv_0\,\tfrac\ell4 = \tfrac5{24}m\ell^2\omega + 0$ (the centre of mass moves straight away from it);
> - *the fixed point at the stick's initial centre:* $mv_0\,\tfrac\ell2 = \tfrac5{24}m\ell^2\omega + (2m)\tfrac{v_0}{2}\tfrac\ell4$ (spin plus orbital term of [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]);
> - *the moving centre of mass itself:* before the impact the particle and the stick move relative to it at $\pm v_0/2$, on lines $\ell/4$ to either side, so $2\cdot m\tfrac{v_0}{2}\tfrac\ell4 = \tfrac5{24}m\ell^2\omega$.
>
> Each gives
>
> $$
> \omega = \frac{6v_0}{5\ell} .
> $$
>
> The kinetic energy afterwards is $\tfrac12(2m)\bigl(\tfrac{v_0}2\bigr)^2 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac5{24}m\ell^2\omega^2 = \tfrac14 mv_0^2 + \tfrac3{20}mv_0^2 = \tfrac25 mv_0^2$: one fifth of the initial energy is lost. The stick's own centre is *not* a legal origin: the glue's impulsive force accelerates it.
>
> *Source: Morin §8.5, Example (Inelastic collision), eqs. (8.54)–(8.57)*

> [!example] Example §B3.3.3: A Disc and a Ring Collide on a Frictionless Track
> A solid disc (mass $m$, radius $R$) is released from rest on an incline with its centre at height $h_1 + R$, rolls down without slipping, and continues on a horizontal **frictionless** track. A ring (mass $m$, radius $R$) rolls without slipping down an incline from the other side, from centre height $h_2 + R$. On the flat they collide head on, stick together, and stop. Find $h_2/h_1$ and the energy dissipated.
>
> *Speeds.* With $T = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 I_{\text{cm}}\omega^2$ ([[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]]) and $v = R\omega$, energy conservation ([[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-3|Theorem §A6.5.3]]) gives
>
> $$
> mgh_1 = \tfrac12 mv_d^2 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 mR^2\frac{v_d^2}{R^2} = \tfrac34 mv_d^2, \qquad mgh_2 = \tfrac12 mv_r^2 + \tfrac12 mR^2\frac{v_r^2}{R^2} = mv_r^2 .
> $$
>
> On the frictionless flat each keeps its $v$ and $\omega$: rolling at constant speed needs no friction.
>
> *Collision.* If the pair stops, the momentum before was zero: $mv_d = mv_r$, so $\tfrac43 gh_1 = gh_2$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{h_2}{h_1} = \frac43 .
> $$
>
> If the pair is at rest afterwards, all the kinetic energy, $mgh_1 + mgh_2 = \tfrac73\,mgh_1$, is dissipated.
>
> *Angular momentum says the pair cannot stop completely.* Just before impact the total momentum is zero, so the angular momentum is the same about every point ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]]), and both spins survive the trip along the frictionless flat: the disc, rolling forward, spins one way with $\tfrac12 mRv$; the ring, rolling the other way, spins the other way with $mRv$. The net angular momentum, $\tfrac12 mRv$ in the ring's sense, is not zero. The only impulsive external forces are the track's normal forces at the two bottom points, at horizontal distance $R$ on either side of the pair's centre of mass. They are vertical and can only push, with impulses $J_1, J_2 \geq 0$. To remove $\tfrac12 mRv$ they need $R\,|J_1 - J_2| = \tfrac12 mRv$, and then the pair leaves the track with upward momentum $J_1 + J_2 \geq \tfrac12 mv$. If the pair kept an angular velocity $\omega$, one of the two bottom points would move into the track unless the centre of mass rose at least at $\lvert\omega\rvert R$; if it lost all its spin, the impulses would throw it up at $(J_1 + J_2)/2m \ge v/4$. Either way the stuck pair leaves the track immediately after the collision: it cannot be at rest. "Stop" can refer only to the horizontal motion. Less than $\tfrac73 mgh_1$ is dissipated, by an amount that depends on how the pair and the track interact, which the statement does not fix.
>
> *Source: PHY 421, Midterm 2 (Fall 2021), Problem 2*
