*Sources: Morin, Introduction to Classical Mechanics, §5.1, §5.5–5.6, §8.1.2, §8.4 · Helliwell & Sahakian, Modern Classical Mechanics, §7.7 · Landau & Lifshitz, Mechanics, §7–10.*

Level A proved three facts about a system of particles: internal forces drop out of $d\vec P/dt = \vec F_{\text{ext}}$ ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]]), the centre of mass moves like a single particle ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]), and $d\vec L/dt = \vec\tau_{\text{ext}}$ about a fixed point ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]]). This section states exactly what the internal forces must satisfy for these to hold, splits momentum, angular momentum and kinetic energy into a part carried by the centre of mass and a part relative to it, and finds about which *moving* points $\vec\tau = d\vec L/dt$ remains true. That last result is what licenses the rigid-body equations about an accelerating centre of mass used in [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis|§B3.2]] and [[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies|§B3.3]].

Notation for the whole chapter: particles $m_i$ at $\vec r_i$ with velocities $\vec v_i$ ($i = 1, \dots, N$); total mass $M = \sum_i m_i$; $\vec R$, $\vec V$ the position and velocity of the centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]]). Kinetic energy is written $T$ (level A writes $K$), potential energy $U$ (Morin writes $V$).

## Internal forces and the third law

> [!definition] Definition §B3.1.1: Weak and Strong Forms of the Third Law
> Let $\vec F_i^{\text{ext}}$ be the external force on particle $i$ and $\vec F_{ij}$ the force on particle $i$ exerted by particle $j$ ($\vec F_{ii} = \vec 0$), so that Newton's second law for each particle reads
>
> $$
> m_i\ddot{\vec r}_i = \vec F_i^{\text{ext}} + \vec F_i^{\text{int}}, \qquad \vec F_i^{\text{int}} = \sum_j \vec F_{ij} .
> $$
>
> The internal forces obey the third law in its **weak form** if $\vec F_{ij} = -\vec F_{ji}$ for every pair, and in its **strong form** if in addition each $\vec F_{ij}$ points along the line joining the two particles, $\vec F_{ij} \parallel (\vec r_i - \vec r_j)$. Internal forces of the strong form are called **central**.
>
> *Source: Morin §5.5.1, §8.4.2, Problem 8.9 · Landau §7*

^def-b3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: The status of the two forms
> - The weak form is Newton's third law as stated at level A ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|Principle §A3.5.1]]). The strong form is an extra assumption. It holds for gravitation and for the electrostatic force between point charges, and it is the usual model for the pushes and pulls between neighbouring molecules of a solid.
> - It fails for the magnetic forces between moving charges ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|EM Principle §A7.1.1]]), and there even the weak form can fail: the two particles' momenta need not add to a constant, because the electromagnetic field carries momentum too ([[§A12.4 Momentum and Radiation Pressure#^thm-a12-4-1|EM Theorem §A12.4.1]]). Conservation is restored for particles plus field. In Morin's words, each particle then interacts with the field, not with the other particle.
> - Landau starts from the other end. A closed system's Lagrangian is unchanged by a translation or a rotation of the whole system. The first symmetry gives conservation of $\vec P$ and, as a consequence, $\sum_i \vec F_i = \vec 0$, which is the weak third law. The second gives conservation of $\vec L$ (Landau §7, §9). That route, from symmetries to conservation laws, is [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-5|Theorem §B6.4.5]]. Morin remarks that which of the two counts as the postulate and which as the theorem is largely a matter of where one starts.

^rem-b3-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.1: Internal Forces Exert No Net Force and No Net Torque
> 1. If the internal forces obey the weak third law, they add to zero: $\sum_i \vec F_i^{\text{int}} = \vec 0$.
> 2. If they obey the strong third law, their torques add to zero about **every** point $A$, fixed or moving:
>
> $$
> \sum_i (\vec r_i - \vec r_A)\times\vec F_i^{\text{int}} = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> So the internal forces never have to be known to apply $d\vec P/dt = \vec F_{\text{ext}}$, or the torque equations below.
>
> *Source: Morin §5.5.1, §8.4.2, Problem 8.9*

^thm-b3-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Forces.* Relabelling the summation indices $i \leftrightarrow j$ does not change a double sum, so with the weak law
>
> $$
> \sum_i\sum_j \vec F_{ij} = \sum_j\sum_i \vec F_{ji} = -\sum_i\sum_j \vec F_{ij} ,
> $$
>
> and a vector equal to its own negative is zero.
>
> *Torques about the origin.* The same relabelling gives $\vec\tau_{\text{int}} = \sum_{i,j}\vec r_i\times\vec F_{ij} = \sum_{i,j}\vec r_j\times\vec F_{ji} = -\sum_{i,j}\vec r_j\times\vec F_{ij}$. Adding the first and last forms,
>
> $$
> 2\,\vec\tau_{\text{int}} = \sum_{i,j}(\vec r_i - \vec r_j)\times\vec F_{ij} = \vec 0 ,
> $$
>
> because each $\vec F_{ij}$ is parallel to $\vec r_i - \vec r_j$ and the cross product of parallel vectors vanishes. The pair's two forces are equal and opposite and have the same lever arm.
>
> *Any point.* $\sum_i(\vec r_i - \vec r_A)\times\vec F_i^{\text{int}} = \vec\tau_{\text{int}} - \vec r_A\times\sum_i\vec F_i^{\text{int}} = \vec 0 - \vec 0$ by the first two parts. Nothing here uses the motion of $A$.

^der-b3-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]] (distributivity, $\vec a\times\vec a = \vec 0$)

> [!caution] Caution: The weak form does not cancel internal torques
> Two particles that pull on each other with equal and opposite forces along parallel but *different* lines form a couple: the forces cancel, their torques do not, and an isolated pair would start to spin by itself. Level A used the strong form as a proviso in a derivation ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^der-a6-6-2|derivation of Theorem §A6.6.2]]: the forces must act along the line joining the particles), without separating it from the third law. Morin's remark that "a rigid object with no external forces won't spontaneously start rotating" is exactly the content of part 2.

^cau-b3-1-1

## Centre-of-mass frame and change of frame

> [!definition] Definition §B3.1.2: Centre-of-Mass Frame; Relative Coordinates
> Let $\vec P = \sum_i m_i\vec v_i$ be the total momentum in an inertial frame. The **centre-of-mass (CM) frame** is the frame whose axes stay parallel to the original ones and whose origin moves with the velocity
>
> $$
> \vec V = \frac{\vec P}{M} = \frac{\sum_i m_i\vec v_i}{M} = \dot{\vec R}
> $$
>
> of the centre of mass; it is the unique such frame in which the total momentum vanishes (Landau: the system is "at rest as a whole"). The **relative coordinates** and velocities are $\vec r_i^{\,\prime} = \vec r_i - \vec R$ and $\vec v_i^{\,\prime} = \vec v_i - \vec V$, and by the definition of $\vec R$
>
> $$
> \sum_i m_i\,\vec r_i^{\,\prime} = \vec 0, \qquad \sum_i m_i\,\vec v_i^{\,\prime} = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> The CM frame is inertial when $\vec F_{\text{ext}} = \vec 0$; otherwise it is a translating, accelerating frame, which is still useful because of [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]].
>
> *Source: Morin §5.6.1, eqs. (5.55)–(5.58) · Landau §8, eqs. (8.1)–(8.3)*

^def-b3-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.2: Momentum, Energy and Angular Momentum in Two Frames
> Let the frame $K'$ move relative to the inertial frame $K$ with constant velocity $\vec u$, with parallel axes, and let the two origins coincide at the instant considered. Primed quantities refer to $K'$. Then
>
> $$
> \vec P = \vec P' + M\vec u, \qquad T = T' + \vec u\cdot\vec P' + \tfrac12 M u^2, \qquad \vec L = \vec L' + M\,\vec R\times\vec u .
> $$
>
> Within one frame, moving the origin from $O$ to a point $O'$ at position $\vec a$ relative to $O$ changes the angular momentum by
>
> $$
> \vec L_O = \vec L_{O'} + \vec a\times\vec P ,
> $$
>
> so $\vec L$ is independent of the origin exactly when $\vec P = \vec 0$.
>
> *Source: Landau §8, eqs. (8.1), (8.5); §9, eqs. (9.4)–(9.5) · Morin §5.6, eqs. (5.55), (5.63)*

^thm-b3-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The velocities are related by $\vec v_i = \vec v_i^{\,\prime} + \vec u$ (Galilean addition, [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|Theorem §A2.8.1]]), and at the instant considered $\vec r_i = \vec r_i^{\,\prime}$. Multiply by $m_i$ and sum: $\vec P = \vec P' + M\vec u$. Square and sum:
>
> $$
> T = \tfrac12\sum_i m_i\bigl(\vec v_i^{\,\prime} + \vec u\bigr)^2 = \tfrac12\sum_i m_i v_i'^2 + \vec u\cdot\sum_i m_i\vec v_i^{\,\prime} + \tfrac12\Bigl(\sum_i m_i\Bigr)u^2 = T' + \vec u\cdot\vec P' + \tfrac12 Mu^2 .
> $$
>
> Cross with $\vec r_i$ and sum: $\vec L = \sum_i m_i\vec r_i\times\vec v_i^{\,\prime} + \bigl(\sum_i m_i\vec r_i\bigr)\times\vec u = \vec L' + M\vec R\times\vec u$. For the change of origin, $\vec r_i = \vec r_i^{\,O'} + \vec a$ with $\vec r_i^{\,O'}$ measured from $O'$, so $\vec L_O = \sum_i(\vec r_i^{\,O'} + \vec a)\times m_i\vec v_i = \vec L_{O'} + \vec a\times\vec P$.

^der-b3-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|Theorem §A2.8.1]], [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-1|Theorem §A1.5.1]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the transformation laws imply
> - Since $\vec P$ and $\vec P'$ differ by the constant $M\vec u$, momentum conserved in one inertial frame is conserved in every other. Since $T$ and $T'$ differ by $\vec u\cdot\vec P' + \tfrac12 Mu^2$, which is the same before and after a collision, a collision that is elastic in one frame is elastic in all (Morin §5.6.2). Collisions viewed from the CM frame are the subject of [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames|§B4.1]].
> - Landau reads the frame dependence of $\vec P$ the other way round: the conservation of momentum gives a natural definition of the velocity of a system *as a whole*, $\vec V = \vec P/M$, and with it the centre of mass.

^rem-b3-1-2

## Decomposition about the centre of mass

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.3: Decomposition about the Centre of Mass
> For any system of particles, with $\vec r_i^{\,\prime}$, $\vec v_i^{\,\prime}$ the coordinates relative to the centre of mass ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^def-b3-1-2|Def. §B3.1.2]]) and $O$ the origin of an inertial frame:
>
> $$
> \vec P = M\vec V, \qquad \vec L_O = \vec R\times M\vec V + \vec L_{\text{cm}}, \qquad T = \tfrac12 MV^2 + T_{\text{cm}},
> $$
>
> where $\vec L_{\text{cm}} = \sum_i \vec r_i^{\,\prime}\times m_i\vec v_i^{\,\prime}$ is the angular momentum about the centre of mass and $T_{\text{cm}} = \tfrac12\sum_i m_i v_i'^2$ the kinetic energy of the motion relative to it. In words: the angular momentum (kinetic energy) of a system is that of a point mass $M$ at the centre of mass, plus the angular momentum (kinetic energy) of the motion relative to the centre of mass. If the internal forces derive from a potential energy $U_{\text{int}}$ that depends only on the relative positions, the energy splits the same way,
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac12 MV^2 + E_{\text{int}}, \qquad E_{\text{int}} = T_{\text{cm}} + U_{\text{int}},
> $$
>
> with $E_{\text{int}}$ the **internal energy**. The two splittings of $\vec L_O$ and $T$ are often called König's theorems.
>
> *Source: Morin §8.1.2, Theorems 8.1–8.2, §5.6.2, eq. (5.63) · Landau §8, eq. (8.4); §9, eq. (9.6)*

^thm-b3-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Substitute $\vec r_i = \vec R + \vec r_i^{\,\prime}$ and $\vec v_i = \vec V + \vec v_i^{\,\prime}$:
>
> $$
> \vec L_O = \sum_i m_i(\vec R + \vec r_i^{\,\prime})\times(\vec V + \vec v_i^{\,\prime}) = M\vec R\times\vec V + \vec R\times\sum_i m_i\vec v_i^{\,\prime} + \Bigl(\sum_i m_i\vec r_i^{\,\prime}\Bigr)\times\vec V + \sum_i\vec r_i^{\,\prime}\times m_i\vec v_i^{\,\prime} ,
> $$
>
> $$
> T = \tfrac12\sum_i m_i\bigl(\vec V + \vec v_i^{\,\prime}\bigr)^2 = \tfrac12 MV^2 + \vec V\cdot\sum_i m_i\vec v_i^{\,\prime} + \tfrac12\sum_i m_i v_i'^2 .
> $$
>
> Every cross term contains $\sum_i m_i\vec r_i^{\,\prime} = \vec 0$ or $\sum_i m_i\vec v_i^{\,\prime} = \vec 0$ and vanishes. $\vec P = M\vec V$ is [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]. The energy statement adds $U_{\text{int}}$, which is unchanged by the substitution because it depends only on the $\vec r_i - \vec r_j = \vec r_i^{\,\prime} - \vec r_j^{\,\prime}$. It is also the case $\vec P' = \vec 0$ of [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]].

^der-b3-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^def-b3-1-2|Def. §B3.1.2]], [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]], [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]

![[ph-cm-b3-1-1.svg]]
*The decomposition about the centre of mass. Each particle's position $\vec r_i$ (blue) is the centre-of-mass position $\vec R$ (red) plus its position $\vec r_i^{\,\prime}$ relative to the centre of mass (green), and likewise for velocities. Because the mass-weighted sums of the green vectors and of their rates of change vanish, $\vec L_O$ and $T$ split into an "orbital" part of the point mass $M$ at $\vec R$ and an intrinsic part about the centre of mass.*

> [!remark] Remark: What is new compared with level A
> - The kinetic-energy split was proved at level A for a rigid body rolling in a plane ([[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-2|Theorem §A6.5.2]]); here it holds for any system, rigid or not, in any motion. For a rigid body in plane motion $T_{\text{cm}} = \tfrac12 I_{\text{cm}}\omega^2$ and $L_{\text{cm},z} = I_{\text{cm}}\omega$ ([[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-3|Theorem §B3.2.3]]).
> - $\vec L_{\text{cm}}$ may be computed with the absolute velocities as well: $\sum_i\vec r_i^{\,\prime}\times m_i\vec v_i = \vec L_{\text{cm}} + \bigl(\sum_i m_i\vec r_i^{\,\prime}\bigr)\times\vec V = \vec L_{\text{cm}}$. Landau calls it the intrinsic angular momentum, and $\vec R\times\vec P$ the angular momentum due to the motion as a whole.
> - The split works only about the centre of mass. About any other reference point the cross terms survive (Morin, footnote to Theorem 8.1). For two particles the relative motion is that of one particle of the reduced mass: [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential|§B2.1]].

## Torque about a moving point

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.4: Torque and Angular Momentum about a Moving Point
> Let $A$ be any point, with position $\vec r_A(t)$, velocity $\vec v_A$ and acceleration $\vec a_A$ in an inertial frame. Define the angular momentum about $A$ with velocities relative to $A$, and the external torque about $A$:
>
> $$
> \vec L_A = \sum_i(\vec r_i - \vec r_A)\times m_i(\vec v_i - \vec v_A), \qquad \vec\tau_A^{\text{ext}} = \sum_i(\vec r_i - \vec r_A)\times\vec F_i^{\text{ext}} .
> $$
>
> If the internal forces obey the strong third law, then
>
> $$
> \frac{d\vec L_A}{dt} = \vec\tau_A^{\text{ext}} - M(\vec R - \vec r_A)\times\vec a_A .
> $$
>
> Hence $d\vec L_A/dt = \vec\tau_A^{\text{ext}}$ holds, and $\vec L_A$ is conserved when $\vec\tau_A^{\text{ext}} = \vec 0$, whenever
> 1. $\vec a_A = \vec 0$: $A$ is fixed, or moves with constant velocity; or
> 2. $A$ is the centre of mass; or
> 3. $\vec R - \vec r_A$ is parallel to $\vec a_A$.
>
> In particular $d\vec L_{\text{cm}}/dt = \vec\tau_{\text{cm}}^{\text{ext}}$ for every system, however its centre of mass accelerates.
>
> *Source: Morin §8.4.3, eqs. (8.43)–(8.46), Corollary 8.3*

^thm-b3-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate with the product rule for the cross product:
>
> $$
> \frac{d\vec L_A}{dt} = \sum_i(\vec v_i - \vec v_A)\times m_i(\vec v_i - \vec v_A) + \sum_i(\vec r_i - \vec r_A)\times m_i(\ddot{\vec r}_i - \vec a_A) .
> $$
>
> The first sum vanishes term by term. In the second, $m_i\ddot{\vec r}_i = \vec F_i^{\text{ext}} + \vec F_i^{\text{int}}$, and the internal torques about $A$ add to zero by [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]] (part 2, which holds for a moving point). What is left is
>
> $$
> \frac{d\vec L_A}{dt} = \vec\tau_A^{\text{ext}} - \Bigl(\sum_i m_i\vec r_i - M\vec r_A\Bigr)\times\vec a_A = \vec\tau_A^{\text{ext}} - M(\vec R - \vec r_A)\times\vec a_A ,
> $$
>
> using $\sum_i m_i\vec r_i = M\vec R$. The extra term vanishes under each of the three conditions. For $A$ the centre of mass, $\vec L_A = \vec L_{\text{cm}}$ of [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]].

^der-b3-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]], [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]] (product rule, componentwise)

> [!remark] Remark: The extra term is a fictitious torque
> In the frame that translates with $A$, every particle feels a fictitious force $-m_i\vec a_A$ ([[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]]). Like uniform gravity, it acts in effect at the centre of mass, so its torque about $A$ is $(\vec R - \vec r_A)\times(-M\vec a_A)$: the extra term. Condition 3 says that the centre of mass lies "above or below" $A$ in this fictitious gravity, so that it has no lever arm. Morin advises using only conditions 1 and 2, "CM's and fixed points", in practice; [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^ex-b3-1-2|Example §B3.1.2]] shows the one common use of condition 3.

^rem-b3-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: What this licenses
> Level A applied $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ about the axis through the centre of mass of a rolling body ([[§A6.5 Rolling#^der-a6-5-4|derivation of Theorem §A6.5.4]]) and of a tumbling gymnast ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^ex-a6-6-2|Example §A6.6.2]]), although that axis accelerates. Condition 2 is the justification. With [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]] it splits the motion of any system into two independent equations, $M\dot{\vec V} = \vec F_{\text{ext}}$ for the centre of mass and $d\vec L_{\text{cm}}/dt = \vec\tau_{\text{cm}}^{\text{ext}}$ for the motion about it.

^rem-b3-1-5

> [!caution] Caution: Which angular momentum about a moving point
> Some texts define the angular momentum about $A$ with absolute velocities, $\vec L_A^{\text{abs}} = \sum_i(\vec r_i - \vec r_A)\times m_i\vec v_i$. The same computation gives $d\vec L_A^{\text{abs}}/dt = \vec\tau_A^{\text{ext}} - \vec v_A\times\vec P$, a different correction term. The two definitions agree when $A$ is the centre of mass, and then both correction terms vanish. In collision problems the trap is an origin that looks natural but accelerates, such as the centre of a stick that is being struck (Morin §8.5): use a fixed point or the centre of mass.

^cau-b3-1-2

## Energy of a system

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.5: Work–Energy Theorem for a System
> With the internal forces obeying the weak third law,
>
> $$
> \frac{dT}{dt} = \sum_i\vec F_i^{\text{ext}}\cdot\vec v_i + \sum_{i<j}\vec F_{ij}\cdot(\vec v_i - \vec v_j) .
> $$
>
> 1. Internal forces can do work. Their power vanishes if they are central and the distances $|\vec r_i - \vec r_j|$ are fixed (a rigid body). If instead $\vec F_{ij}$ derives from a potential $U_{ij}(|\vec r_i - \vec r_j|)$, the internal power is $-dU_{\text{int}}/dt$ and $d(T + U_{\text{int}})/dt = \sum_i\vec F_i^{\text{ext}}\cdot\vec v_i$.
> 2. The centre-of-mass part obeys
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(\tfrac12 MV^2\Bigr) = \vec F_{\text{ext}}\cdot\vec V, \qquad \Delta\Bigl(\tfrac12 MV^2\Bigr) = \int\vec F_{\text{ext}}\cdot d\vec R ,
> $$
>
> the net external force times the displacement of the **centre of mass**, not of the points where the forces act. The rest of the work goes into $T_{\text{cm}}$ and the internal energy.
>
> *Source: Morin §5.1, eq. (5.13) and the braking car; §5.6.2 · Landau §8*

^thm-b3-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate $T = \tfrac12\sum_i m_i\vec v_i\cdot\vec v_i$ and use the second law for each particle:
>
> $$
> \frac{dT}{dt} = \sum_i m_i\vec v_i\cdot\ddot{\vec r}_i = \sum_i\vec F_i^{\text{ext}}\cdot\vec v_i + \sum_i\sum_{j}\vec F_{ij}\cdot\vec v_i .
> $$
>
> Group the double sum by pairs: $\vec F_{ij}\cdot\vec v_i + \vec F_{ji}\cdot\vec v_j = \vec F_{ij}\cdot(\vec v_i - \vec v_j)$ by the weak law. If $\vec F_{ij} = f_{ij}\,(\vec r_i - \vec r_j)$ for a scalar $f_{ij}$, then
>
> $$
> \vec F_{ij}\cdot(\vec v_i - \vec v_j) = f_{ij}\,(\vec r_i - \vec r_j)\cdot(\vec v_i - \vec v_j) = \tfrac12 f_{ij}\,\frac{d}{dt}\lvert\vec r_i - \vec r_j\rvert^2 ,
> $$
>
> which is zero when the distance is fixed. If $\vec F_{ij} = -\nabla_i U_{ij}(\rho_{ij})$ with $\rho_{ij} = |\vec r_i - \vec r_j|$, then $\vec F_{ij} = -U_{ij}'(\rho_{ij})\,(\vec r_i - \vec r_j)/\rho_{ij}$ and the pair's power is $-U_{ij}'\,\dot\rho_{ij} = -dU_{ij}/dt$ by the chain rule. For part 2, differentiate $\tfrac12 MV^2$ and use $M\dot{\vec V} = \vec F_{\text{ext}}$ ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]); integrate with $\vec V\,dt = d\vec R$. The remainder, $dT/dt - \vec F_{\text{ext}}\cdot\vec V$, is $dT_{\text{cm}}/dt$ by [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]].

^der-b3-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]], [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]], [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]], [[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]] (power $\vec F\cdot\vec v$), [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]], [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Friction that does no work
> A car brakes without skidding. The only horizontal external force is the friction of the road on the tyres, which acts at contact points that are momentarily at rest, so it does no work. Yet by part 2 it removes $\int\vec F_{\text{ext}}\cdot d\vec R$ of kinetic energy of the centre of mass: the total energy of the car is unchanged, and $\tfrac12 MV^2$ turns into heat in the brakes, an internal energy (Morin §5.1). The same bookkeeping explains why static friction takes no energy from a body rolling without slipping ([[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-3|Theorem §A6.5.3]]): its power is zero and a rigid body has no internal power. When the body *slips*, the work of friction is the force times the distance the surfaces slide over each other, not times the distance the centre moves ([[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]]).

^rem-b3-1-6

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.6: Virial Theorem
> Let a system of particles move so that all positions and velocities stay bounded for all $t \ge 0$, and write $\langle f\rangle = \lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac1\tau\int_0^\tau f\,dt$ for long-time averages (assumed to exist). Then
>
> $$
> 2\langle T\rangle = -\Bigl\langle\sum_i\vec F_i\cdot\vec r_i\Bigr\rangle ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec F_i$ the total force on particle $i$; the right side is the **virial** (of Clausius). If all forces derive from a potential energy $U(\vec r_1, \dots, \vec r_N)$ that is homogeneous of degree $k$ in the positions, $U(\lambda\vec r_1, \dots, \lambda\vec r_N) = \lambda^kU(\vec r_1, \dots, \vec r_N)$, then
>
> $$
> 2\langle T\rangle = k\,\langle U\rangle, \qquad \langle U\rangle = \frac{2E}{k + 2}, \qquad \langle T\rangle = \frac{kE}{k + 2} \quad (k \neq -2) .
> $$
>
> For harmonic forces ($k = 2$) $\langle T\rangle = \langle U\rangle$; for gravitational or Coulomb interactions ($k = -1$) $2\langle T\rangle = -\langle U\rangle$ and $E = -\langle T\rangle$, so bounded motion requires $E < 0$. For a periodic motion the averages may be taken over one period, without the limit.
>
> *Source: Landau §10, eqs. (10.4)–(10.7) · H&S §7.7, eqs. (7.79)–(7.84), Example 7.5*

^thm-b3-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $G = \sum_i\vec p_i\cdot\vec r_i$ with $\vec p_i = m_i\vec v_i$. By the second law $\dot{\vec p}_i = \vec F_i$, and $\vec p_i\cdot\dot{\vec r}_i = m_iv_i^2$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{dG}{dt} = \sum_i\vec F_i\cdot\vec r_i + 2T .
> $$
>
> Average over $[0, \tau]$: the left side gives $\bigl(G(\tau) - G(0)\bigr)/\tau$ by the fundamental theorem of calculus, which tends to zero because $G$ is bounded (and is exactly zero over a period of a periodic motion). This is the first statement. If $\vec F_i = -\nabla_iU$ with $U$ homogeneous of degree $k$, differentiate $U(\lambda\vec r_1, \dots) = \lambda^kU(\vec r_1, \dots)$ with respect to $\lambda$ at $\lambda = 1$ by the chain rule: $\sum_i\vec r_i\cdot\nabla_iU = kU$ (Euler's theorem on homogeneous functions). Hence $-\sum_i\vec F_i\cdot\vec r_i = kU$ and $2\langle T\rangle = k\langle U\rangle$. With $T + U = E$ constant, $\langle T\rangle + \langle U\rangle = E$; solving the two linear equations gives $\langle U\rangle$ and $\langle T\rangle$.

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|Principle §A3.3.3]], [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]] (energy conservation), [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Checks and uses
> - For a Kepler ellipse ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]) the theorem gives $\langle U\rangle = 2E = -\alpha/a$ over a period, so the time average of $1/r$ is $1/a$, although $r$ ranges from $a(1 - e)$ to $a(1 + e)$; on a circle it is the level-A statement $T = \lvert E\rvert$ ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-5|Theorem §A8.2.5]], with level A's $K$ for $T$).
> - For $N$ bodies interacting by gravity, the pair forces are central and $\vec F_{ij}\cdot(\vec r_i - \vec r_j) = U_{ij}$ (H&S Example 7.5), so $2\langle T\rangle = -\langle U\rangle$ for the total potential energy, whatever $N$ is. Zwicky (1933) applied it to the galaxies of the Coma cluster and found the kinetic energy far too large for the visible mass: the first evidence for dark matter (H&S §7.7).
> - The scaling behind the theorem, Landau's mechanical similarity, also gives Kepler's $T^2 \propto a^3$ without solving anything ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^rem-b2-2-3|Remark in §B2.2]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.1.1: Two Legal Origins for a Rolling Cylinder
> A uniform cylinder (mass $M$, radius $R$) rolls without slipping on a fixed plane inclined at $\theta$. A string wound round it leaves its top parallel to the plane, runs up over a massless pulley, and holds a hanging mass $m$. Find the acceleration of $m$, and when the cylinder rolls down.
>
> Take $a_1$ the cylinder's acceleration down the plane, $\alpha$ its angular acceleration in the rolling sense, $a_2$ the upward acceleration of $m$, $T$ the tension and $F$ the friction (up the plane). The top of a rolling cylinder moves twice as fast as its centre ([[§A6.5 Rolling#^rem-a6-5-1|Remark in §A6.5]]), so $a_2 = 2a_1$; also $\alpha = a_1/R$.
>
> *About the centre of mass* (condition 2):
>
> $$
> T - mg = ma_2, \qquad Mg\sin\theta - T - F = Ma_1, \qquad (F - T)R = \tfrac12 MR^2\alpha .
> $$
>
> The last two give $Mg\sin\theta - 2T = \tfrac32 Ma_1$; with $T = m(g + a_2)$ and $a_1 = a_2/2$,
>
> $$
> a_2 = \frac{(M\sin\theta - 2m)\,g}{\tfrac34 M + 2m} .
> $$
>
> *About the fixed point of the plane where the cylinder touches it* (condition 1): friction and the normal force pass through it, gravity has lever arm $R$ for its component along the plane, the tension has lever arm $2R$. The angular momentum is that of the centre of mass plus the spin ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]), $L = Mv_1R + \tfrac12 MR^2\omega$, so
>
> $$
> MgR\sin\theta - 2RT = Ma_1R + \tfrac12 MR^2\alpha ,
> $$
>
> which is $R$ times the second equation above plus the third: the unknown friction never appears. The cylinder rolls down ($a_1 > 0$) when $M/m > 2/\sin\theta$. As $\theta \to \pi/2$ this tends to $2$, not $1$, because friction then helps the string hold the cylinder up: at balance $F = T = \tfrac12 Mg\sin\theta$. Near $\theta = \pi/2$ that needs a very large coefficient of friction, since the normal force $Mg\cos\theta$ goes to zero (Morin).
>
> *Source: Morin §8.4, Example (Fig. 8.17), eqs. (8.48)–(8.49)*

> [!example] Example §B3.1.2: Rolling about the Contact Point
> A round body of radius $R$, with its centre of mass at its centre and $I_{\text{cm}} = \beta MR^2$, rolls without slipping on a flat surface. Let $A$ be the material point of the rim that touches the ground at the instant $t_0$. Rolling means $\vec v_A = \vec 0$ at $t_0$, and the acceleration of $A$ is then $R\omega^2$ straight toward the centre: the tangential parts cancel ($a_{\text{cm}} = R\alpha$), leaving the centripetal part. So $\vec R - \vec r_A$ and $\vec a_A$ are both perpendicular to the surface, and condition 3 of [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]] holds. Relative to $A$, a material point of the body, the body simply rotates, so $L_A = (I_{\text{cm}} + MR^2)\,\omega$ at all times (parallel axes, [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-3|Theorem §A6.2.3]]).
>
> On an incline of angle $\theta$, friction and the normal force act at $A$ and have no torque about it; gravity has lever arm $R$ for its component along the slope. One equation gives the whole answer of [[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-4|Theorem §A6.5.4]]:
>
> $$
> (1 + \beta)MR^2\,\alpha = MgR\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_{\text{cm}} = R\alpha = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + \beta} .
> $$
>
> The shortcut fails if the centre of mass is off the geometric centre (a wheel with a weight on its rim), or on a curved surface: then $\vec R - \vec r_A$ is not parallel to $\vec a_A$ and the correction term $-M(\vec R - \vec r_A)\times\vec a_A$ must be kept.
>
> *Source: Morin §8.4.3 (Remark on the third condition: a dot on a rolling wheel)*
