Conservation laws leave the direction of an elastic collision open ([[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]]); the force and the impact parameter decide it. Quantum Mechanics level A derived Rutherford's formula as Tipler does, for a point nucleus held fixed, with the deflection from an impulse integral ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-3|QM Theorem §A2.1.3]]) and the counting rate from the geometry of rings and cones ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-4|QM Theorem §A2.1.4]]). This section is the classical theory in full generality: for any central potential the deflection angle is an integral fixed by the conservation of energy and angular momentum ([[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle|§B1.2]]), and the differential cross-section follows from the deflection function. The hard sphere and the Coulomb field are the two exactly solvable cases; Rutherford's formula comes out for either sign of the force and for a recoiling target, by working in the centre-of-mass frame and converting to the laboratory; and the small-angle limit gives the impulse approximation that level A used.

## Cross-sections

> [!definition] Definition §B4.2.1: Impact Parameter, Deflection Function, Differential Cross-Section
> A uniform parallel beam of identical particles, with $n$ particles crossing unit area perpendicular to the beam per unit time, falls on a scattering centre.
> - The **impact parameter** $b$ of a particle is the distance from the centre to the straight line along which it approaches: the distance of closest approach if there were no force ([[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^def-a2-1-2|QM Def. §A2.1.2]]).
> - The **scattering angle** $\chi \in [0, \pi]$ is the angle between the final and initial velocities; $\chi(b)$ is the **deflection function**.
> - If $dN$ particles per unit time are scattered into the solid angle $d\Omega = \sin\chi\,d\chi\,d\varphi$ about the direction $(\chi, \varphi)$, the **differential cross-section** is
>
> $$
> d\sigma = \frac{dN}{n}, \qquad\text{written as}\qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\,d\Omega ,
> $$
>
> an area; the **total cross-section** is $\sigma = \int(d\sigma/d\Omega)\,d\Omega$, the area of the beam that is deflected at all.
>
> *Source: Landau §18, eqs. (18.5), (18.8) · H&S §8.5, eq. (8.67) (where $d\sigma/d\Omega$ is written $\sigma(\Theta)$)*

^def-b4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.1: Deflection Angle in a Central Field
> A particle of mass $m$ approaches a fixed centre of force with potential energy $U(r) \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$, with speed $v_\infty$ and impact parameter $b$. Its orbit is symmetric about the line from the centre to the point of closest approach $r_{\min}$, and it is deflected by $\chi = \lvert\pi - 2\varphi_0\rvert$, where
>
> $$
> \varphi_0 = \int_{r_{\min}}^{\infty}\frac{(b/r^2)\,dr}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{b^2}{r^2} - \dfrac{2U(r)}{mv_\infty^2}}} ,
> $$
>
> $r_{\min}$ being the largest zero of the radicand. For two particles interacting through $U(r)$ the same holds for their relative motion with $m$ replaced by the reduced mass $\mu$ and $v_\infty$ the relative speed; $\chi$ is then the scattering angle in the centre-of-mass frame ([[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^def-b4-1-2|Def. §B4.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: Landau §18, eqs. (18.1)–(18.4) (the orbit integral is Landau's eq. (14.7)) · H&S §8.5*

^thm-b4-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The force is central, so angular momentum about the centre is conserved ([[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]) and the motion stays in the plane through the centre perpendicular to $\vec L$; energy is conserved ([[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]). Far away the particle moves on a straight line at distance $b$ from the centre, so
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac12 mv_\infty^2, \qquad L = mv_\infty b .
> $$
>
> The orbit then follows from the quadratures of the central-field problem ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-6|Theorem §B2.1.6]], one body, so $\mu = m$): on a leg on which $r$ increases,
>
> $$
> \frac{d\varphi}{dr} = \frac{L/r^2}{\sqrt{2m\bigl(E - U(r)\bigr) - L^2/r^2}} = \frac{b/r^2}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{b^2}{r^2} - \dfrac{2U(r)}{mv_\infty^2}}} ,
> $$
>
> dividing numerator and denominator by $mv_\infty$. The radicand is $2(E - U_{\text{eff}})/mv_\infty^2$, with the effective potential $U_{\text{eff}} = U + L^2/2mr^2$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]]). The particle comes in from infinity to the first zero of the radicand it meets, the largest one, $r_{\min}$, and goes out again. The orbit is symmetric about the line from the centre through this turning point ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]], part 5), so integrating from $r_{\min}$ to $\infty$ gives the angle $\varphi_0$ between that line and the outgoing asymptote, and the incoming asymptote makes the same angle on the other side. The direction of motion therefore turns by $\pi - 2\varphi_0$ (with no force, $\varphi_0 = \pi/2$ and there is no deflection). For two particles, the relative coordinate obeys the same equations with $\mu$ for $m$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]), and its velocity, $-\vec v$ in the convention of [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^cau-b4-1-1|Caution: Sign of the relative velocity]], is turned through the same angle as $\vec v$, namely $\chi$.

> [!remark] Remark: Attractive fields
> For an attractive field $\pi - 2\varphi_0$ can be negative (the particle swings round the centre, deflected toward it) or even smaller than $-\pi$ (it loops). The observable scattering angle is the one in $[0, \pi]$ with the same outgoing direction; Landau's formulas use $\chi$ in this sense, and nothing below depends on the sign of $\pi - 2\varphi_0$.
>
> *Source: Landau §18–19*

^rem-b4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.2: Cross-Section from the Deflection Function
> If the deflection angle decreases monotonically with the impact parameter, so that $b(\chi)$ is single-valued,
>
> $$
> d\sigma = 2\pi b\,\Bigl\lvert\frac{db}{d\chi}\Bigr\rvert\,d\chi, \qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{b(\chi)}{\sin\chi}\,\Bigl\lvert\frac{db}{d\chi}\Bigr\rvert .
> $$
>
> If several impact parameters $b_i$ lead to the same $\chi$, the contributions of all branches are added. Equivalently, $d\sigma = \pi\,\lvert d(b^2)\rvert$.
>
> *Source: Landau §18, eqs. (18.6)–(18.8) and footnote · H&S §8.5, eq. (8.67)*

^thm-b4-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The field is axially symmetric about the beam direction through the centre. Particles with impact parameters between $b$ and $b + db$ cross a ring of area $2\pi b\,\lvert db\rvert$, so $dN = n\cdot2\pi b\lvert db\rvert$ of them arrive per unit time; by monotonicity they, and no others, are deflected into the angles between $\chi$ and $\chi + d\chi$, a cone of solid angle $d\Omega = 2\pi\sin\chi\,\lvert d\chi\rvert$ (the area of the corresponding band on the unit sphere). Dividing by $n$ gives $d\sigma$ ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^def-b4-2-1|Def. §B4.2.1]]); dividing by $d\Omega$ gives the second form. The modulus is needed because $db/d\chi$ is usually negative: smaller $b$, larger deflection. If several rings feed the same cone, their particle numbers add.

^der-b4-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^def-b4-2-1|Def. §B4.2.1]], [[§18 Surface Integrals#^ex-18-2|452 Ex. §18.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Finite and infinite total cross-sections
> If $U = 0$ beyond some range $R$, particles with $b > R$ are not deflected, and $\sigma = \pi b_{\max}^2$ with $b_{\max} \le R$: a classical particle either "hits" the target or does not. If $U$ is nonzero at all distances, every particle is deflected a little and $\sigma = \infty$: for Rutherford's formula $d\sigma/d\Omega$ is not integrable at $\chi \to 0$. Measurements count particles in a detector of finite solid angle away from the beam, so they test $d\sigma/d\Omega$, not $\sigma$.

^rem-b4-2-2

## Two exact cases

> [!model] Model §B4.2.3: Hard-Sphere Scatterer
> The target is a perfectly rigid, smooth sphere of radius $a$: $U = \infty$ for $r < a$, $U = 0$ for $r > a$. A particle moves on straight lines and reflects elastically at the surface, the angle of reflection equal to the angle of incidence, measured from the normal (the radius to the point of impact).
> - *Assumes:* no friction or spin transfer, no deformation, an infinitely steep wall.
> - *Applies to:* collisions of billiard balls and of molecules in a gas, as a first model; light reflected by a polished bead (H&S).
>
> *Source: Landau §18, Problem 1 · H&S §8.5, Example 8.4*

^mod-b4-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.4: Hard-Sphere Cross-Section
> For the hard sphere, $b = a\cos(\chi/2)$ for $b \le a$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{a^2}{4}, \qquad \sigma = \pi a^2 :
> $$
>
> the scattering is **isotropic**, and the total cross-section is the geometrical cross-section of the sphere.
>
> *Source: Landau §18, Problem 1 · H&S §8.5, eqs. (8.70)–(8.71)*

^thm-b4-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A particle with $b \le a$ strikes the sphere where the radius makes the angle of incidence $\alpha$ with the reversed incoming direction, $b = a\sin\alpha$. Reflection reverses the normal component of the velocity and keeps the tangential one, turning the direction by $\chi = \pi - 2\alpha$. So $b = a\sin\bigl(\tfrac\pi2 - \tfrac\chi2\bigr) = a\cos\tfrac\chi2$, and $\lvert db/d\chi\rvert = \tfrac a2\sin\tfrac\chi2$. By [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]],
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{a\cos(\chi/2)\cdot\tfrac a2\sin(\chi/2)}{2\sin(\chi/2)\cos(\chi/2)} = \frac{a^2}{4},
> $$
>
> and $\sigma = 4\pi\cdot a^2/4 = \pi a^2$. Particles with $b > a$ miss.

^der-b4-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^mod-b4-2-3|Model §B4.2.3]], [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.5: Rutherford's Formula
> Let two particles interact through $U = \alpha/r$, with $\alpha > 0$ (repulsion) or $\alpha < 0$ (attraction): for charges $\alpha = kq_1q_2$ ([[§A1.2 Coulomb's Law#^pr-a1-2-2|EM Principle §A1.2.2]]; H&S use Gaussian units, $\alpha = q_1q_2$), for gravity $\alpha = -Gm_1m_2$. In the centre-of-mass frame, with relative speed $v_\infty$ and reduced mass $\mu$,
>
> $$
> b = \frac{\lvert\alpha\rvert}{\mu v_\infty^2}\cot\frac\chi2, \qquad
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \Bigl(\frac{\alpha}{2\mu v_\infty^2}\Bigr)^2\frac{1}{\sin^4(\chi/2)} = \Bigl(\frac{\alpha}{4E}\Bigr)^2\frac{1}{\sin^4(\chi/2)} ,
> $$
>
> where $E = \tfrac12\mu v_\infty^2$ is the energy in the centre-of-mass frame. The cross-section is the same for attraction and repulsion.
>
> *Source: Landau §19, eqs. (19.1)–(19.3) · H&S §8.5, eqs. (8.64)–(8.69)*

^thm-b4-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $d = \alpha/\mu v_\infty^2$ and $u = 1/r$ in [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]] (with $\mu$ for $m$), so $(b/r^2)\,dr = -b\,du$ and the radicand is $1 - b^2u^2 - 2du$. Completing the square, $1 - b^2u^2 - 2du = R^2 - w^2$ with $w = bu + d/b$ and $R^2 = 1 + d^2/b^2$. As $r$ runs from $r_{\min}$ to $\infty$, $w$ runs from $R$ down to $d/b$, so
>
> $$
> \varphi_0 = \int_{d/b}^{R}\frac{dw}{\sqrt{R^2 - w^2}} = \arccos\frac{d/b}{\sqrt{1 + d^2/b^2}}, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \cot\varphi_0 = \frac db .
> $$
>
> For $\alpha > 0$: $0 < \varphi_0 < \pi/2$, $\chi = \pi - 2\varphi_0$, and $b = d\tan\varphi_0 = d\cot\frac\chi2$. For $\alpha < 0$: $\varphi_0 > \pi/2$, the particle is deflected toward the centre by $\chi = 2\varphi_0 - \pi$, and $b = \lvert d\rvert\cot\frac\chi2$ again. Then $\lvert db/d\chi\rvert = \tfrac{\lvert d\rvert}{2}\csc^2\tfrac\chi2$ and [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]] give
>
> $$
> \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{\lvert d\rvert\cot(\chi/2)\cdot\tfrac{\lvert d\rvert}{2}\csc^2(\chi/2)}{2\sin(\chi/2)\cos(\chi/2)} = \frac{d^2}{4\sin^4(\chi/2)} ,
> $$
>
> which is the formula, since $d/2 = \alpha/2\mu v_\infty^2 = \alpha/4E$. Only $d^2$ appears, so the sign of $\alpha$ drops out. (H&S reach $\cot\frac\chi2 = 2bE/\lvert\alpha\rvert$ from the eccentricity of the hyperbolic orbit; it is the same relation.)

^der-b4-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]], [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]], [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^rem-b4-2-1|Remark: Attractive fields]]

> [!remark] Remark: What this adds to the level-A derivation
> With the target held fixed ($m_2 \to \infty$, so $\mu \to m_1$ and $\chi$ is the lab angle) and $\alpha = kq_\alpha Q$, the relation $b = (\alpha/m_1v^2)\cot(\chi/2)$ is [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-3|QM Theorem §A2.1.3]], and the count of [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-4|QM Theorem §A2.1.4]] is $\Delta N = N_0\,nt\,(A_{sc}/r^2)\,d\sigma/d\Omega$ for a foil of $n$ nuclei per unit volume and thickness $t$. The derivation here is the general classical one: it uses only the two conservation laws instead of the impulse integral (which is exact only because the force is inverse-square), holds for attraction as well as repulsion, takes the recoil of a finite-mass target into account through $\mu$ and the centre-of-mass frame, and leads to the laboratory formulas of [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-6|Theorem §B4.2.6]]. The orbit itself is a hyperbola of eccentricity $\sqrt{1 + (b/d)^2}$ with the centre at a focus: for attraction the branch curving round the focus ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]), for repulsion the other branch.
>
> *Source: H&S §8.5 · Landau §19*

## From the centre-of-mass frame to the laboratory

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.6: Cross-Sections in the Laboratory Frame
> Let particle 2 be at rest in the lab and $\gamma = m_1/m_2$. The number of scattered particles is the same in both frames; only the solid-angle element changes. With $\chi(\theta_1)$ from [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]],
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega_1}\Bigr)_{\!L} = \Bigl(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\Bigr)_{\!C}\,\frac{\bigl(1 + \gamma^2 + 2\gamma\cos\chi\bigr)^{3/2}}{\lvert1 + \gamma\cos\chi\rvert}
> $$
>
> for the scattered particle (summed over both values of $\chi$ when $\gamma > 1$), and
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega_2}\Bigr)_{\!L} = \Bigl(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\Bigr)_{\!C}\,4\cos\theta_2, \qquad \chi = \pi - 2\theta_2 ,
> $$
>
> for the recoiling target. Special cases:
> - $m_2 \gg m_1$: the lab and C frames coincide, and Rutherford's formula reads $(\alpha/4E_1)^2/\sin^4(\theta_1/2)$ with $E_1 = \tfrac12m_1v_\infty^2$;
> - $m_1 = m_2$: $\chi = 2\theta_1$ and the factor is $4\cos\theta_1$ ($\theta_1 < \pi/2$). Rutherford: $(\alpha/E_1)^2\cos\theta_1/\sin^4\theta_1$; hard sphere: $a^2\cos\theta_1$;
> - recoils, Rutherford: $(\alpha/\mu v_\infty^2)^2/\cos^3\theta_2$; hard sphere: $a^2\cos\theta_2$;
> - two identical particles, both counted, at the common angle $\theta$: Rutherford $(\alpha/E_1)^2\bigl(\sin^{-4}\theta + \cos^{-4}\theta\bigr)\cos\theta$.
>
> *Source: Landau §18 (conversion via (17.4)) and Problem 1; §19, eqs. (19.4)–(19.7). The general factor for the scattered particle is derived here.*

^thm-b4-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A particle scattered into $d\Omega$ about $\chi$ in the C frame appears in $d\Omega_1$ about $\theta_1$ in the lab, with the same azimuth; so $d\sigma$ is the same and $(d\sigma/d\Omega_1) = (d\sigma/d\Omega)\,\lvert d\cos\chi/d\cos\theta_1\rvert$. From $\tan\theta_1 = \sin\chi/(\gamma + \cos\chi)$, with $D = 1 + \gamma^2 + 2\gamma\cos\chi$ the squared length of $(\gamma + \cos\chi, \sin\chi)$,
>
> $$
> \cos\theta_1 = \frac{\gamma + \cos\chi}{\sqrt D}, \qquad \frac{d\cos\theta_1}{d\cos\chi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt D} - \frac{\gamma(\gamma + \cos\chi)}{D^{3/2}} = \frac{1 + \gamma\cos\chi}{D^{3/2}} ,
> $$
>
> which gives the factor. For $\gamma > 1$ each $\theta_1 < \theta_{1,\max}$ comes from two $\chi$'s ([[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^cau-b4-1-2|Caution: One lab angle, two collisions]]), and the counts add ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]). For the target, $\theta_2 = (\pi - \chi)/2$ gives $\cos\chi = -\cos2\theta_2 = 1 - 2\cos^2\theta_2$, so $\lvert d\cos\chi/d\cos\theta_2\rvert = 4\cos\theta_2$.
>
> Special cases. $\gamma \to 0$: $D \to 1$, factor $1$, $\mu \to m_1$, $\chi \to \theta_1$. $\gamma = 1$: $D = 2(1 + \cos\chi) = 4\cos^2\frac\chi2$, so the factor is $8\cos^3\frac\chi2/(2\cos^2\frac\chi2) = 4\cos\theta_1$; with $\mu = m_1/2$, $(\alpha/2\mu v_\infty^2)^2 = (\alpha/2E_1)^2$, and $4\cos\theta_1(\alpha/2E_1)^2/\sin^4\theta_1$ is the stated result; the hard sphere gives $4\cos\theta_1\cdot a^2/4$. Recoils: $\sin(\chi/2) = \cos\theta_2$, so Rutherford gives $4\cos\theta_2(\alpha/2\mu v_\infty^2)^2/\cos^4\theta_2$. Identical particles cannot be told apart after the collision, so the counts of scattered and recoiling particles at the same angle add; with $\mu v_\infty^2 = E_1$ for equal masses the recoil term is $(\alpha/E_1)^2/\cos^3\theta$.

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]], [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^cau-b4-1-2|Caution: One lab angle, two collisions]], [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]], [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]], [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]]

## Small deflections

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.7: Small-Angle Scattering
> If the deflection is small (the field weak along the whole path, as for large impact parameters), the lab scattering angle of a particle of mass $m_1$ is
>
> $$
> \theta_1 \approx -\frac{2b}{m_1v_\infty^2}\int_b^{\infty}\frac{dU}{dr}\,\frac{dr}{\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}} ,
> $$
>
> and $d\sigma = (b/\theta_1)\,\lvert db/d\theta_1\rvert\,d\Omega_1$. For $U = \alpha/r$ this gives $\theta_1 = 2\alpha/(m_1v_\infty^2b)$, the small-angle limit of Rutherford's $b(\chi)$. For $U = \alpha/r^n$, $\theta_1 \propto b^{-n}$ and $d\sigma/d\Omega_1 \propto \theta_1^{-2-2/n}$.
>
> *Source: Landau §20, eqs. (20.1)–(20.4), Problem 2*

^thm-b4-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take $x$ along the incoming momentum $p = m_1v_\infty$ and $y$ in the plane of scattering. For a small deflection $\theta_1 \approx p_y'/p$, where $p_y'$ is the transverse momentum acquired, $p_y' = \int F_y\,dt$ ([[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]). Since $p_y'$ is already of first order in $U$, evaluate the integral along the undeflected path $y = b$, $x = v_\infty t$: $F_y = -\frac{dU}{dr}\frac{b}{r}$ and $dt = dx/v_\infty$. As $x$ runs over the whole line, $r = \sqrt{x^2 + b^2}$ runs from $\infty$ down to $b$ and back, with $dx = r\,dr/\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}$, so
>
> $$
> p_y' = -\frac{2b}{v_\infty}\int_b^\infty\frac{dU}{dr}\,\frac{dr}{\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}} ,
> $$
>
> and dividing by $m_1v_\infty$ gives $\theta_1$. The cross-section is [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]] with $\sin\theta_1 \approx \theta_1$. For $U = \alpha/r$, $dU/dr = -\alpha/r^2$, and with $r = b\sec s$, $\int_b^\infty dr/(r^2\sqrt{r^2 - b^2}) = b^{-2}\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos s\,ds = b^{-2}$, so $\theta_1 = 2\alpha/(m_1v_\infty^2b)$; Rutherford's $b = (\alpha/m_1v_\infty^2)\cot(\theta_1/2)$ with $\cot(\theta_1/2) \approx 2/\theta_1$ agrees. For $U = \alpha/r^n$ the same substitution gives $\theta_1 = C\,\alpha/(m_1v_\infty^2b^n)$ with a number $C$ depending on $n$; then $b \propto \theta_1^{-1/n}$, $\lvert db/d\theta_1\rvert \propto \theta_1^{-1/n - 1}$ and $d\sigma/d\Omega_1 \propto \theta_1^{-2/n - 2}$.

^der-b4-2-7

*Uses:* [[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]], [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]], [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: The impulse method, placed
> This is the method of [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^thm-a2-1-3|QM Theorem §A2.1.3]], to first order: there the momentum transfer was integrated along the true orbit, which happens to be possible exactly for an inverse-square force; here it is integrated along the straight line, which works for any weak field. The lab frame can be used directly; in the C frame the same formula holds with $\mu$ for $m_1$ and $\chi$ for $\theta_1$, the small angles being related by $\theta_1 = m_2\chi/(m_1 + m_2)$.
>
> *Source: Landau §20 (and its footnote)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.2.1: How Much Energy Does the Target Take?
> A particle of mass $m_1$ and speed $v_\infty$ scatters elastically off a particle of mass $m_2$ at rest. Find the cross-section $d\sigma$ for transferring energy between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon + d\epsilon$ to the target, for (a) a hard sphere and (b) a Coulomb interaction.
>
> By [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]], $\epsilon = \epsilon_{\max}\sin^2(\chi/2)$ with $\epsilon_{\max} = 2\mu^2v_\infty^2/m_2$. Use $d\sigma = \pi\lvert d(b^2)\rvert$ ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]) and express $b^2$ through $\epsilon$.
>
> (a) $b^2 = a^2\cos^2(\chi/2) = a^2(1 - \epsilon/\epsilon_{\max})$, so $d\sigma = \pi a^2\,d\epsilon/\epsilon_{\max}$: all energy transfers from $0$ to $\epsilon_{\max}$ are equally likely.
>
> (b) $b^2 = d^2\cot^2(\chi/2) = d^2(\epsilon_{\max}/\epsilon - 1)$ with $d = \alpha/\mu v_\infty^2$, so
>
> $$
> d\sigma = \pi d^2\epsilon_{\max}\frac{d\epsilon}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{2\pi\alpha^2}{m_2v_\infty^2}\,\frac{d\epsilon}{\epsilon^2}, \qquad 0 < \epsilon \le \epsilon_{\max} .
> $$
>
> Small transfers dominate. At a given speed the cross-section is proportional to $\alpha^2/m_2$: for the same strength of interaction, a light target takes energy far more readily than a heavy one.
>
> *Source: Landau §18, Problem 2; §19, eq. (19.8) (solved here)*

^ex-b4-2-1

> [!example] Example §B4.2.2: Gravitational Focusing
> Particles of mass $m_1$ arrive with speed $v_\infty$ at a planet of mass $m_2 \gg m_1$ and radius $R$, which attracts them by gravity. What is the cross-section for hitting the planet?
>
> A particle hits if its closest approach to the centre is at most $R$. The grazing orbit has its turning point at $R$, where the velocity $v_R$ is tangential. Angular momentum and energy give
>
> $$
> m_1v_RR = m_1v_\infty b, \qquad \tfrac12m_1v_R^2 - \frac{Gm_1m_2}{R} = \tfrac12m_1v_\infty^2 ,
> $$
>
> so $b_{\max}^2 = R^2(1 + 2Gm_2/Rv_\infty^2)$ and
>
> $$
> \sigma = \pi R^2\Bigl(1 + \frac{v_{\text{esc}}^2}{v_\infty^2}\Bigr), \qquad v_{\text{esc}}^2 = \frac{2Gm_2}{R}
> $$
>
> ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^def-a8-2-1|Def. §A8.2.1]]). Slow particles are pulled in from far outside the planet's geometric shadow; for $v_\infty \to \infty$ the cross-section falls to $\pi R^2$.
>
> *Source: Landau §18, Problem 6 (solved here)*
