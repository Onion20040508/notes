Level A solved collisions in the laboratory: momentum is always conserved, kinetic energy in elastic collisions ([[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^def-a5-3-1|Def. §A5.3.1]]), with the one-dimensional elastic formulas ([[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^thm-a5-3-2|Theorem §A5.3.2]]) and two-dimensional problems by components; the centre of mass moves uniformly when no external force acts ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]). Level B does elastic collisions in the frame in which the centre of mass is at rest. There an elastic collision does nothing but turn the relative velocity, and every laboratory result (deflection angles, the largest possible deflection, the energy given to the target) follows from one vector addition. Conservation laws fix everything except the direction of the turn; that direction depends on the force and the impact parameter, the subject of [[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering|§B4.2]].

## The two frames

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.1: Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames; Relative Velocity
> Two particles of masses $m_1$, $m_2$ have velocities $\vec v_1$, $\vec v_2$ in an inertial frame, the **laboratory frame** (L frame) in which the data are given. Write $M = m_1 + m_2$. The **centre-of-mass frame** (C frame) is the inertial frame moving relative to the lab with the velocity of the centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]]),
>
> $$
> \vec V = \frac{m_1\vec v_1 + m_2\vec v_2}{M} = \frac{\vec P}{M} ;
> $$
>
> in it the total momentum is zero. The **relative velocity** is $\vec v = \vec v_1 - \vec v_2$, the same in every frame, and $\mu = m_1m_2/M$ is the **reduced mass** ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-2|Def. §B2.1.2]]); for $N$ particles the centre-of-mass frame is [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^def-b3-1-2|Def. §B3.1.2]]. Quantities in the C frame carry a subscript $0$ (Landau) or the label CM.
>
> *Source: Landau §16–17 · Morin §5.6.1 · H&S §1.9*

^def-b4-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.1: Two Bodies in the Centre-of-Mass Frame
> 1. The C-frame velocities and momenta are
>
> $$
> \vec v_{10} = \frac{m_2}{M}\,\vec v, \qquad \vec v_{20} = -\frac{m_1}{M}\,\vec v, \qquad \vec p_{10} = -\vec p_{20} = \mu\vec v .
> $$
>
> 2. The total kinetic energy in the lab splits as
>
> $$
> K = \tfrac12 MV^2 + \tfrac12\mu v^2 ,
> $$
>
> the kinetic energy of the centre-of-mass motion plus the C-frame kinetic energy $K_0 = \tfrac12\mu v^2$.
> 3. In a collision with no external force, $\vec V$ does not change. Hence a collision that conserves kinetic energy in one inertial frame conserves it in all, and at most $K_0 = \tfrac12\mu v^2$ can be converted into other forms, which happens exactly when the bodies stick together.
>
> *Source: Landau §17 (velocities in the C system); §8, eq. (8.4) · Morin §5.6.1–5.6.2, eq. (5.63)*

^thm-b4-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. In the C frame $m_1\vec v_{10} + m_2\vec v_{20} = \vec 0$, and $\vec v_{10} - \vec v_{20} = \vec v$ because a common velocity drops out of differences. Solving these two linear equations gives the velocities; multiplying by the masses gives the momenta.
> 2. With $\vec v_i = \vec V + \vec v_{i0}$,
>
> $$
> K = \sum_i\tfrac12 m_i\lvert\vec V + \vec v_{i0}\rvert^2 = \tfrac12 MV^2 + \vec V\cdot\sum_i m_i\vec v_{i0} + \sum_i\tfrac12 m_iv_{i0}^2 ,
> $$
>
> and the middle sum is the C-frame momentum, zero. By part 1, $\sum_i\tfrac12m_iv_{i0}^2 = \tfrac12\bigl(m_1m_2^2 + m_2m_1^2\bigr)v^2/M^2 = \tfrac12\mu v^2$.
> 3. $\vec V = \vec P/M$ and $\vec P$ is conserved ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]), so $\tfrac12MV^2$ is the same before and after. Any change of $K$ is a change of $\tfrac12\mu v^2$, which is frame-independent; it is largest, $-\tfrac12\mu v^2$, when the final relative velocity is zero. For $N$ bodies the same split holds with $K_0$ the kinetic energy in the C frame ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]).

^der-b4-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: The level-A loss formula, explained
> Level A found that a perfectly inelastic collision keeps $P^2/2M$ ([[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^rem-a5-3-1|Remark: How much kinetic energy can be lost?]]). That is the term $\tfrac12MV^2$; what is lost is $\tfrac12\mu v^2$, all of the C-frame energy. When a heavy body hits a light one at rest, $\mu \approx m_{\text{light}}$ and the loss is only about the light body's share; when equal bodies meet head on with equal speeds, $V = 0$ and everything can be lost.

^rem-b4-1-1

## Elastic collisions

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.2: Elastic Collision in the Centre-of-Mass Frame
> In an elastic collision of two particles (internal states unchanged, no external force during the collision), in the C frame each particle keeps its speed and the two momenta stay opposite: the collision only turns the relative velocity,
>
> $$
> \vec v\,' = v\,\hat n_0, \qquad \lvert\vec v\,'\rvert = \lvert\vec v\rvert = v ,
> $$
>
> with $\hat n_0$ a unit vector. In the lab the final velocities and momenta are
>
> $$
> \vec v_1' = \vec V + \frac{m_2}{M}\,v\,\hat n_0, \quad \vec v_2' = \vec V - \frac{m_1}{M}\,v\,\hat n_0; \qquad \vec p_1' = \mu v\,\hat n_0 + \frac{m_1}{M}\vec P, \quad \vec p_2' = -\mu v\,\hat n_0 + \frac{m_2}{M}\vec P .
> $$
>
> Conservation of momentum and energy determine everything except the direction $\hat n_0$, which depends on the interaction and on how the particles approached.
>
> *Source: Landau §17, eqs. (17.1)–(17.3) · Morin §5.6.1, §5.7.2 · H&S §1.9*

^thm-b4-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the C frame the total momentum is zero before and after, so after the collision too $\vec v_{10}' = (m_2/M)\vec v\,'$ and $\vec v_{20}' = -(m_1/M)\vec v\,'$ ([[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]], part 1, applied to the final state). The C-frame kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\mu v^2$ before and $\tfrac12\mu v'^2$ after; elasticity (with part 3) makes them equal, so $v' = v$ and only the direction $\hat n_0$ of $\vec v\,'$ is free. Adding $\vec V$, unchanged by the collision, gives the lab velocities, and multiplying by $m_1$, $m_2$ the momenta, using $m_1\vec V = (m_1/M)\vec P$.
>
> In one dimension $\hat n_0 = \pm\hat v$: either nothing happens or the relative velocity reverses, $\vec v\,' = -\vec v$, which is [[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^thm-a5-3-2|Theorem §A5.3.2]] (Morin's Theorem 5.3).

^der-b4-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]], [[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^def-a5-3-1|Def. §A5.3.1]], [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.2: Scattering Angles
> In an elastic collision the **centre-of-mass scattering angle** $\chi \in [0, \pi]$ is the angle between $\hat n_0$ and the initial relative velocity $\vec v$: the angle through which each particle is turned in the C frame. When particle 2 is initially at rest in the lab, the **laboratory angles** $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$ are the angles between the initial velocity $\vec v_1$ and the final velocities $\vec v_1'$, $\vec v_2'$.
>
> *Source: Landau §17*

^def-b4-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.3: Target at Rest: Laboratory Angles and Speeds
> Let particle 2 be at rest, so $\vec v = \vec v_1$ and $V = m_1v/M$. Then
>
> $$
> \tan\theta_1 = \frac{m_2\sin\chi}{m_1 + m_2\cos\chi}, \qquad \theta_2 = \frac{\pi - \chi}{2},
> $$
>
> $$
> v_1' = \frac{v}{M}\sqrt{m_1^2 + m_2^2 + 2m_1m_2\cos\chi}, \qquad v_2' = \frac{2m_1v}{M}\sin\frac\chi2 ,
> $$
>
> the two particles moving off on opposite sides of the line of incidence.
>
> *Source: Landau §17, eqs. (17.4)–(17.5) · Morin Problem 5.24 (first solution)*

^thm-b4-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take $\hat x$ along $\vec v_1$ and $\hat n_0 = (\cos\chi, \sin\chi)$ in the plane of the motion. By [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]],
>
> $$
> \vec v_1' = \frac{m_1v}{M}\hat x + \frac{m_2v}{M}(\cos\chi, \sin\chi) = \frac vM\bigl(m_1 + m_2\cos\chi,\ m_2\sin\chi\bigr),
> $$
>
> whose angle and length are the stated $\theta_1$ and $v_1'$. Similarly
>
> $$
> \vec v_2' = \frac{m_1v}{M}\bigl(1 - \cos\chi,\ -\sin\chi\bigr) = \frac{2m_1v}{M}\sin\frac\chi2\,\Bigl(\sin\frac\chi2,\ -\cos\frac\chi2\Bigr),
> $$
>
> using $1 - \cos\chi = 2\sin^2\frac\chi2$ and $\sin\chi = 2\sin\frac\chi2\cos\frac\chi2$. The unit vector makes the angle $\tfrac\pi2 - \tfrac\chi2$ with $\hat x$, on the side opposite to $\vec v_1'$.

^der-b4-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]], [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^def-b4-1-2|Def. §B4.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The velocity diagram
> The final lab velocity of particle 1 is $\vec V$ plus a vector of fixed length $U = m_2v/M$ in a free direction: its tip lies on a circle (a sphere in three dimensions) of radius $U$ centred at the tip of $\vec V$. The circle passes through the tip of $\vec v_1$ (no collision, $\chi = 0$); its far point, at $\chi = \pi$, is the head-on result $(m_1 - m_2)v/M$ of [[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^thm-a5-3-2|Theorem §A5.3.2]]. Whether the starting point $A$ of $\vec V$ lies outside or inside the circle depends on whether $V = m_1v/M$ exceeds $U = m_2v/M$, i.e. on whether $m_1 > m_2$. Landau draws the same construction with momenta (a circle of radius $\mu v$).
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 5.24 (first solution) · Landau §17, Figs. 15–16*

^rem-b4-1-2

![[ph-cm-b4-1-1.svg]]
*The velocity diagram for a target at rest, drawn to scale for $v = $ 4 units. (a) $m_1 = 2m_2$: $A$ lies outside the circle, and $\vec v_1'$ (red) can deviate from $\vec v$ by at most $\theta_{\max}$, reached when $\vec v_1'$ is tangent to the circle (here $30^\circ$); the example shown has $\chi = 30^\circ$. (b) $m_1 = m_2/2$: $A$ lies inside the circle and every direction is possible, including backward ($\chi = 140^\circ$ gives $\theta_1 = 112^\circ$).*

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.4: Deflection Limit, Energy Transfer, Equal Masses
> For an elastic collision with particle 2 initially at rest:
> 1. if $m_1 > m_2$, particle 1 is deflected by at most $\theta_{1,\max}$ with $\sin\theta_{1,\max} = m_2/m_1$; if $m_1 < m_2$, every $\theta_1 \in [0, \pi]$ occurs; if $m_1 = m_2$, $\theta_1 \le \pi/2$;
> 2. the energy given to the target is
>
> $$
> E_2' = \frac{4m_1m_2}{M^2}\,E_1\sin^2\frac\chi2 \ \le\ \frac{4m_1m_2}{M^2}\,E_1 , \qquad E_1 = \tfrac12 m_1v^2,
> $$
>
> the maximum reached in a head-on collision ($\chi = \pi$, target recoiling along $\vec v_1$), where the target gets $v_2' = 2m_1v/M$;
> 3. if $m_1 = m_2$: $\theta_1 = \chi/2$, $\theta_2 = (\pi - \chi)/2$, so $\theta_1 + \theta_2 = \pi/2$: unless the collision is head-on, the particles leave at right angles, with speeds $v\cos\frac\chi2$ and $v\sin\frac\chi2$.
>
> *Source: Landau §17, eqs. (17.6)–(17.10) · Morin Problems 5.19, 5.24 · H&S §1.9, eqs. (1.137)–(1.139)*

^thm-b4-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. In the velocity diagram the direction of $\vec v_1'$ is that of a line from $A$ to a point of the circle. If $A$ is outside ($V > U$, i.e. $m_1 > m_2$), these lines fill the cone bounded by the tangents from $A$; at the point of tangency the radius is perpendicular to the tangent, so $\sin\theta_{1,\max} = U/V = m_2/m_1$. If $A$ is inside, lines from $A$ meet the circle in every direction. If $A$ is on the circle ($m_1 = m_2$), the directions are those of chords from $A$, which make at most a right angle with the diameter through $A$. (Morin's second solution reaches the same bound in the lab frame: the quadratic for $v_1'$ has real roots only if $\cos^2\theta_1 \ge 1 - m_2^2/m_1^2$.)
> 2. By [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]], $E_2' = \tfrac12m_2\bigl(\tfrac{2m_1v}{M}\bigr)^2\sin^2\tfrac\chi2 = \tfrac{4m_1m_2}{M^2}\cdot\tfrac12m_1v^2\cdot\sin^2\tfrac\chi2$, largest at $\chi = \pi$, where $\theta_2 = 0$.
> 3. With $m_1 = m_2$, $\tan\theta_1 = \sin\chi/(1 + \cos\chi) = \tan\frac\chi2$, and the speeds follow from Theorem §B4.1.3. Directly (H&S): momentum gives $\vec v_1 = \vec v_1' + \vec v_2'$ and energy $v_1^2 = v_1'^2 + v_2'^2$; squaring the first and subtracting the second leaves $\vec v_1'\cdot\vec v_2' = 0$.

^der-b4-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]], [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^rem-b4-1-2|Remark: The velocity diagram]], [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: One lab angle, two collisions
> The map from $\chi$ to $\theta_1$ is one-to-one only for $m_1 < m_2$. For $m_1 > m_2$ each $\theta_1 < \theta_{1,\max}$ comes from two values of $\chi$ (the two intersections of a line from $A$ with the circle), with different final speeds ([[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^ex-b4-1-1|Example §B4.1.1]]). Converting a C-frame angular distribution to the lab must then add both branches ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering|§B4.2]]). Also, $\chi$ ranges over $[0, \pi]$ while $\theta_2$ never exceeds $\pi/2$: a target at rest is never knocked backward.

^cau-b4-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Decay of a moving particle
> The same construction describes a particle moving with velocity $\vec V$ that splits into two. In its rest frame the fragments fly apart with opposite momenta of a magnitude $p_0$ fixed by the released energy $\epsilon$, $p_0^2/2\mu = \epsilon$; in the lab a fragment's velocity ends on a sphere of radius $v_0 = p_0/m$ about the tip of $\vec V$. If $V > v_0$ the fragment is confined to a forward cone, $\sin\theta_{\max} = v_0/V$. If the decays are isotropic in the rest frame, the fragments' lab kinetic energies are spread uniformly between $\tfrac12m(v_0 - V)^2$ and $\tfrac12m(v_0 + V)^2$.
>
> *Source: Landau §16, eqs. (16.1)–(16.8)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.1.1: Two Speeds at One Laboratory Angle
> A particle of mass $m_1$ hits a particle of mass $m_2$ at rest, elastically, and is observed at the lab angle $\theta_1$. Find its speed and that of the target.
>
> In the velocity diagram, $\vec v_1' - \vec V$ has length $U = m_2v/M$; with $\vec V = (m_1v/M)\hat x$ this reads $v_1'^2 - 2Vv_1'\cos\theta_1 + V^2 = U^2$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{v_1'}{v} = \frac{m_1\cos\theta_1 \pm \sqrt{m_2^2 - m_1^2\sin^2\theta_1}}{m_1 + m_2} .
> $$
>
> For $m_1 < m_2$ only the $+$ sign gives $v_1' > 0$: one collision per angle. For $m_1 > m_2$ both roots are positive for $\theta_1 < \theta_{1,\max}$ and merge at $\theta_{1,\max}$. For the target, $\sin\frac\chi2 = \cos\theta_2$ ([[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]]) gives $v_2' = (2m_1v/M)\cos\theta_2$.
>
> Numbers: an α particle on a hydrogen nucleus, $m_1/m_2 \approx 4$. The α particle is turned by at most $\arcsin\frac14 = 14.5^\circ$; at $\theta_1 = 10^\circ$ it has $v_1'/v = \bigl(4\cos10^\circ \pm \sqrt{1 - 16\sin^2 10^\circ}\bigr)/5 = 0.932$ or $0.644$. The proton can take at most $4m_1m_2/M^2 = 16/25 = 64\%$ of the α particle's energy.
>
> *Source: Landau §17, Problem (solved here)*

^ex-b4-1-1

> [!example] Example §B4.1.2: A Collision That Exchanges Mass
> Two particles move toward each other on a line, $m_1$ with $v_1 > 0$ and $m_2$ with $v_2 < 0$. In the collision they exchange part of their mass, leaving with masses $m_1'$, $m_2'$ and velocities $v_1'$, $v_2'$; kinetic energy is conserved. (a) Write the conservation laws in terms of the centre-of-mass velocities $V$, $V'$ and relative velocities $v = v_1 - v_2$, $v' = v_1' - v_2'$. (b) Solve for $V'$, $v'$, assuming $m_1' + m_2' = m_1 + m_2$. (c) What if the total mass is not conserved?
>
> (a) With $M = m_1 + m_2$, $\mu = m_1m_2/M$ and the primed versions for the final state, [[§B4.1 Elastic Collisions in the Laboratory and Centre-of-Mass Frames#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]] turns the two conservation laws into
>
> $$
> MV = M'V', \qquad \tfrac12MV^2 + \tfrac12\mu v^2 = \tfrac12M'V'^2 + \tfrac12\mu'v'^2 .
> $$
>
> (b) With $M' = M$: $V' = V$, and then $\mu'v'^2 = \mu v^2$. The particles separate, so $v'$ has the sign opposite to $v$:
>
> $$
> V' = V, \qquad v' = -\sqrt{\mu/\mu'}\;v .
> $$
>
> The lab velocities follow as $v_1' = V + (m_2'/M)v'$, $v_2' = V - (m_1'/M)v'$. For $m_i' = m_i$ this is the reversal of the relative velocity; a mass exchange that lowers the reduced mass speeds up the relative motion.
>
> (c) Then $V' = MV/M'$, and energy gives $\mu'v'^2 = \mu v^2 + MV^2\bigl(1 - M/M'\bigr)$: the centre-of-mass motion now feeds or drains the relative motion. (In Newtonian mechanics the total mass of a closed system is conserved, so (c) is a formal exercise: it shows which step used mass conservation.)
>
> *Source: PHY 421, Midterm 2 (Fall 2015), Problem 2 (statement; solved here)*

^ex-b4-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The C frame of two bodies is the frame of the two-body reduction ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]): the relative coordinate moves like one particle of mass $\mu$, and its deflection by the interaction gives $\chi$ ([[§B4.2 Scattering Cross-Sections and Rutherford Scattering|§B4.2]]).
> - Collisions of extended bodies, with angular impulse and rolling, are [[§B3.3 Angular Impulse, Rolling and Collisions of Rigid Bodies]].
> - At relativistic speeds the same C-frame reasoning applies with four-momentum (Relativity, level B, planned; relativistic momentum [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|REL Def. §A3.1.1]]).
