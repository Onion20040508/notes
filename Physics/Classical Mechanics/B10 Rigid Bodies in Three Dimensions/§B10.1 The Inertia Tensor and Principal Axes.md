Level A described rotation about a fixed axis by one number, the moment of inertia ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]]), with $K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2$ ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-1|Theorem §A6.2.1]]) and $L_z = I\omega$; the angular momentum points along the axis only for a body symmetric about it ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-3|Theorem §A6.6.3]]). The fixed-axis theory is developed further in [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis]]. When the axis may turn, one number is not enough: $\vec L$ and $\vec\omega$ are related by a symmetric matrix, the inertia tensor, and in general they point in different directions. This section defines the tensor, shifts it between origins, and shows by the spectral theorem that every body has three perpendicular principal axes in which the tensor is diagonal.

## Rigid-body kinematics

> [!model] Model §B10.1.1: Rigid Body
> A **rigid body** is a system of particles (or a continuous mass distribution) whose mutual distances never change. Its configuration is fixed by the position of one of its points and by its orientation, three numbers each: six degrees of freedom. A body turning about a fixed axis ([[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^mod-a6-1-1|Model §A6.1.1]]) is the special case with one.
> *Valid when:* deformations are negligible on the scale of the motion; no real body is perfectly rigid, and Earth's elasticity, for example, shows up in its wobble ([[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^ex-b10-2-2|Example §B10.2.2]]).
> *Source: Morin §9.1 · H&S §12.2–12.3*

^mod-b10-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.2: Velocity Field of a Rigid Body
> At each instant there is a single vector $\vec\omega$, the **angular velocity** of the body, such that for any two points $P$, $Q$ of the body
>
> $$
> \vec v_Q = \vec v_P + \vec\omega\times(\vec r_Q - \vec r_P) .
> $$
>
> The motion is a translation with the velocity of any chosen point $P$ plus a rotation with angular velocity $\vec\omega$ about an axis through $P$ (Chasles' theorem); $\vec\omega$ is the same for every choice of $P$, and only the translational part depends on it.
>
> *Source: Morin §9.1, Theorems 9.1–9.2 · H&S §12.2, §12.5*

^thm-b10-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Paint an orthonormal triad on the body with origin at $P$; it is a moving frame ([[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^def-b9-1-1|Def. §B9.1.1]]) with some angular velocity $\vec\omega$ ([[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]]). Rigidity means that every point $Q$ of the body has constant coordinates in this frame, so its velocity in the frame is zero, and [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-4|Theorem §B9.1.4]] gives $\vec v_Q = \vec v_P + \vec\omega\times(\vec r_Q - \vec r_P)$. A triad painted at another point $P'$ can be chosen parallel to the first; parallel triads have the same $\hat e_i(t)$ and hence the same $\vec\omega$ by the formula of Theorem §B9.1.1. (Morin argues instead that a rigid motion of a sphere into itself leaves a diameter fixed.)

## The inertia tensor

> [!definition] Definition §B10.1.1: Inertia Tensor
> For a body of point masses $m_a$ at positions $\vec r_a = (x_{a1}, x_{a2}, x_{a3})$ from an origin $O$, in a chosen orthonormal basis, the **inertia tensor** about $O$ is the $3\times3$ matrix
>
> $$
> I_{jk} = \sum_a m_a\bigl(r_a^2\,\delta_{jk} - x_{aj}x_{ak}\bigr), \qquad
> \mathsf I = \begin{pmatrix} \sum m(y^2 + z^2) & -\sum mxy & -\sum mzx \\ -\sum mxy & \sum m(z^2 + x^2) & -\sum myz \\ -\sum mzx & -\sum myz & \sum m(x^2 + y^2) \end{pmatrix} ,
> $$
>
> with $\sum_a m_a \to \int dm$ for a continuous body. The diagonal entries are the moments of inertia about the coordinate axes ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]]); the off-diagonal entries are the **products of inertia**. $\mathsf I$ is real and symmetric and depends only on the body, the origin and the axes. In another orthonormal basis, related by a rotation matrix $\mathsf A$, it becomes $\mathsf A\,\mathsf I\,\mathsf A^{\mathsf T}$.
>
> *Source: Morin §9.2.1, eq. (9.8) · H&S §12.6–12.7, eq. (12.78)*

^def-b10-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.3: Angular Momentum and Kinetic Energy of a Rigid Body
> For a rigid body turning with angular velocity $\vec\omega$ about a point $O$ fixed in an inertial frame,
>
> $$
> \vec L = \mathsf I\,\vec\omega, \qquad T = \tfrac12\,\vec\omega^{\mathsf T}\mathsf I\,\vec\omega = \tfrac12\,\vec\omega\cdot\vec L ,
> $$
>
> with $\mathsf I$ about $O$. (Level B writes $T$ for kinetic energy, level A $K$.) For a body that also translates, take $O$ at the centre of mass, moving with velocity $\vec V$ at $\vec R$: then $\vec L = M\vec R\times\vec V + \mathsf I_{\text{cm}}\vec\omega$ and $T = \tfrac12 MV^2 + \tfrac12\vec\omega^{\mathsf T}\mathsf I_{\text{cm}}\vec\omega$, the decompositions of [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]. For rotation about a single axis $\hat n$, $\vec\omega = \omega\hat n$, $T = \tfrac12 I_n\omega^2$ with $I_n = \hat n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf I\,\hat n$ the moment of inertia about that axis, which is [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-1|Theorem §A6.2.1]].
>
> *Source: Morin §9.2.1–9.2.2, eqs. (9.8), (9.15)–(9.17) · H&S §12.6, §12.9*

^thm-b10-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $O$ fixed, each mass moves with $\vec v_a = \vec\omega\times\vec r_a$ ([[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]). Then, by the expansion of the triple product ([[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]),
>
> $$
> \vec L = \sum_a m_a\,\vec r_a\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r_a) = \sum_a m_a\bigl(r_a^2\,\vec\omega - (\vec r_a\cdot\vec\omega)\,\vec r_a\bigr), \qquad L_j = \sum_k\Bigl[\sum_a m_a\bigl(r_a^2\delta_{jk} - x_{aj}x_{ak}\bigr)\Bigr]\omega_k = \sum_k I_{jk}\,\omega_k .
> $$
>
> For the energy, $|\vec\omega\times\vec r|^2 = (\vec\omega\times\vec r)\cdot(\vec\omega\times\vec r) = \vec\omega\cdot\bigl(\vec r\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r)\bigr)$ (cyclic property of the triple product), so $T = \tfrac12\sum_a m_a|\vec\omega\times\vec r_a|^2 = \tfrac12\vec\omega\cdot\vec L$. With $\hat n$ fixed, $|\hat n\times\vec r_a|$ is the distance of $m_a$ from the axis, so $\hat n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf I\,\hat n = \sum_a m_a|\hat n\times\vec r_a|^2$ is the moment of inertia of [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]]. For translating bodies, split $\vec r_a = \vec R + \vec r_a'$ and $\vec v_a = \vec V + \vec\omega\times\vec r_a'$; the cross terms contain $\sum_a m_a\vec r_a' = \vec 0$ and drop out.

> [!remark] Remark: Why the angular momentum leans
> A single mass $m$ circling the $z$ axis at radius $r$ in the plane $z = z_0$ has, about the origin, $\vec L = m\omega(-xz_0, -yz_0, r^2)$ (Morin §9.2.1, Example 2): at the instant it crosses the $yz$ plane it moves along $-\hat x$ at height $z_0$, so it has angular momentum about the $y$ axis as well as the $z$ axis. That is what the products of inertia $-\sum mxz$, $-\sum myz$ record (for rotation about a fixed axis, [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]). A second mass at the opposite point of the circle cancels them, and $\vec L$ lines up with $\vec\omega$. A body whose $\vec L$ is not along $\vec\omega$ cannot turn at constant $\vec\omega$ without a torque, since $\vec L$ then sweeps round a cone with it: a wheel whose mass is not distributed symmetrically about its axle shakes its bearings (dynamic imbalance; the torque is computed in [[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^ex-b10-2-1|Example §B10.2.1]]). Since $T = \tfrac12\sum m|\vec\omega\times\vec r|^2 \ge 0$, the quadratic form $\vec\omega^{\mathsf T}\mathsf I\vec\omega$ is never negative.

^rem-b10-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.4: Parallel-Axis Theorem, Tensor Form
> Let $\mathsf I^{\text{cm}}$ be the inertia tensor of a body of mass $M$ about its centre of mass, and let $\vec D$ be the vector from a point $O$ to the centre of mass. With axes parallel to those at the centre of mass,
>
> $$
> I^{O}_{jk} = I^{\text{cm}}_{jk} + M\bigl(D^2\,\delta_{jk} - D_jD_k\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The added matrix is the inertia tensor of a point mass $M$ at the centre of mass. Its diagonal entries give $I = I_{\text{cm}} + Md^2$ with $d$ the perpendicular distance between the parallel axes, which is [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-3|Theorem §A6.2.3]]; the theorem shifts all three axes at once, and the off-diagonal entries $-MD_jD_k$ are new.
>
> *Source: Morin §9.2.3, eq. (9.19); Problem 9.4 · H&S §12.7, eqs. (12.95)–(12.96)*

^thm-b10-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write each position from $O$ as $\vec r_a = \vec D + \vec r_a'$, with $\vec r_a'$ measured from the centre of mass, so $\sum_a m_a\vec r_a' = \vec 0$ ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]]). Then
>
> $$
> r_a^2\delta_{jk} - x_{aj}x_{ak} = \bigl(D^2 + 2\vec D\cdot\vec r_a' + r_a'^2\bigr)\delta_{jk} - \bigl(D_j + x'_{aj}\bigr)\bigl(D_k + x'_{ak}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Multiply by $m_a$ and sum. Every term linear in $\vec r_a'$ sums to zero, leaving $M(D^2\delta_{jk} - D_jD_k) + \sum_a m_a(r_a'^2\delta_{jk} - x'_{aj}x'_{ak})$. For a diagonal entry, $D^2 - D_j^2$ is the squared distance of the centre of mass from the $j$ axis through $O$.

## Principal axes

> [!definition] Definition §B10.1.2: Principal Axes; Principal Moments
> A unit vector $\hat n$ is a **principal axis** of the body about $O$ if $\mathsf I\,\hat n = I_n\,\hat n$: an angular velocity along $\hat n$ gives an angular momentum along $\hat n$. The number $I_n$ is the **principal moment** about it. Three mutually perpendicular principal axes $\hat e_1, \hat e_2, \hat e_3$ form a **principal-axis frame**; in it
>
> $$
> \mathsf I = \begin{pmatrix} I_1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & I_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & I_3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec L = (I_1\omega_1,\ I_2\omega_2,\ I_3\omega_3), \qquad T = \tfrac12\bigl(I_1\omega_1^2 + I_2\omega_2^2 + I_3\omega_3^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The principal axes are fixed in the body and turn with it.
>
> *Source: Morin §9.3, eqs. (9.22)–(9.24) · H&S §12.7, eqs. (12.80)–(12.83)*

^def-b10-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.5: Principal Axes Exist
> For every body and every origin $O$:
> 1. there is an orthonormal basis of principal axes, so $\mathsf I$ is diagonal in a suitable rotated basis;
> 2. the principal moments are the roots of $\det(\mathsf I - \lambda\,\mathbb 1) = 0$; they are real and non-negative;
> 3. any two of them add up to at least the third: $I_1 + I_2 \ge I_3$, with equality exactly for a flat body in the plane of axes 1 and 2 (the perpendicular-axis theorem, [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]).
>
> Principal axes belonging to different principal moments are automatically perpendicular.
>
> *Source: Morin §9.3, Theorem 9.4; Appendix D, Theorem D.1 · H&S §12.7, eq. (12.85); Problem 12.17*

^thm-b10-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With the dot product, $\mathbb R^3$ is an inner-product space and a real symmetric matrix is a self-adjoint operator on it ([[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-10|LADR Def. 7.10]]). By the real spectral theorem ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]) $\mathbb R^3$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $\mathsf I$, which is (1). A nonzero $\vec u$ with $(\mathsf I - \lambda\mathbb 1)\vec u = \vec 0$ exists exactly when $\det(\mathsf I - \lambda\mathbb 1) = 0$ ([[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-51|LADR Thm. 9.51]]), and eigenvalues of self-adjoint operators are real ([[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-12|LADR Thm. 7.12]]). Each principal moment is non-negative: $I_n = \hat n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf I\,\hat n = \sum_a m_a|\hat n\times\vec r_a|^2 \ge 0$ ([[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]]), so $\mathsf I$ is a positive operator ([[§24 Positive Operators#^ladr-7-38|LADR Thm. 7.38]]).
>
> Morin's Appendix D proves (1)–(2) directly. If $\mathsf I\vec u = \lambda\vec u$ with possibly complex $\lambda$, $\vec u$, dot with $\vec u^*$; conjugating the equation and dotting with $\vec u$ gives $\vec u\cdot\mathsf I\vec u^* = \lambda^*\,\vec u\cdot\vec u^*$, and symmetry gives $\vec u^*\cdot\mathsf I\vec u = \vec u\cdot\mathsf I\vec u^*$; so $(\lambda - \lambda^*)\,|\vec u|^2 = 0$ and $\lambda$ is real. If $\mathsf I\vec u_1 = \lambda_1\vec u_1$ and $\mathsf I\vec u_2 = \lambda_2\vec u_2$, then $\lambda_1\,\vec u_2\cdot\vec u_1 = \vec u_2\cdot\mathsf I\vec u_1 = \vec u_1\cdot\mathsf I\vec u_2 = \lambda_2\,\vec u_1\cdot\vec u_2$, so $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\,\vec u_1\cdot\vec u_2 = 0$: eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are perpendicular ([[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-22|LADR Thm. 7.22]]). For a repeated eigenvalue, the whole plane of its eigenvectors qualifies, and two perpendicular ones can be picked.
>
> For (3), in the principal frame $I_1 = \sum m(x_2^2 + x_3^2)$, $I_2 = \sum m(x_3^2 + x_1^2)$, $I_3 = \sum m(x_1^2 + x_2^2)$, so $I_1 + I_2 - I_3 = 2\sum m\,x_3^2 \ge 0$, zero exactly when every mass has $x_3 = 0$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.6: Principal Axes from Symmetry
> Let the origin $O$ be fixed.
> 1. If two principal moments are equal, $I_1 = I_2 = I$, every axis through $O$ in the plane of $\hat e_1, \hat e_2$ is principal with moment $I$; if all three are equal, every axis is.
> 2. If the body is unchanged by reflection in a plane through $O$, the normal to that plane is a principal axis.
> 3. If the body is unchanged by a rotation through an angle $\theta \ne 180^\circ$ (and $\ne 0$) about an axis through $O$, that axis is principal and every axis through $O$ perpendicular to it is principal with one common moment. (A hexagonal plate, a square bar and a cylinder about their centres are examples.)
>
> *Source: Morin §9.3, Theorems 9.5–9.6 · H&S Example 12.4*

^thm-b10-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $\mathsf I(a\hat e_1 + b\hat e_2) = aI\hat e_1 + bI\hat e_2 = I(a\hat e_1 + b\hat e_2)$; likewise in three dimensions.
> (2) Take the plane as $z = 0$. Reflection pairs each mass at $(x, y, z)$ with an equal mass at $(x, y, -z)$, so $\sum mxz = \sum myz = 0$ and $\mathsf I\hat z = I_{zz}\hat z$.
> (3) Let the symmetry axis be $\hat z$. Rotating the body leaves it unchanged, so an angular velocity $\omega\hat z$, which the rotation also leaves unchanged, must produce an $\vec L$ that the rotation leaves unchanged; a vector unchanged by a rotation through $\theta \ne 0$ lies along the axis, so $\hat z$ is principal. The other two principal axes then lie in the $xy$ plane (Theorem §B10.1.5). Rotating one of them, $\hat n_0$, through $\theta$ gives a principal axis $\hat n_\theta$ with the same moment, by the symmetry. For $\theta \ne 180^\circ$, $\hat n_0$ and $\hat n_\theta$ are not parallel, so they span the plane, and the argument of (1) applies.

^der-b10-1-6

*Uses:* [[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^def-b10-1-2|Def. §B10.1.2]], [[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^thm-b10-1-5|Theorem §B10.1.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Principal axes are the torque-free axes
> Morin gives a third, physical, characterisation: a body pivoted at $O$, with no torque about $O$, can rotate at constant $\vec\omega$ only about a principal axis. Then $\vec L = I_n\vec\omega$ is constant, so $d\vec L/dt = \vec 0$ is consistent with zero torque ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]]); about any other fixed axis $\vec L$ turns round a cone and a torque is needed. A uniform square plate pivoted at a corner turns steadily about its diagonal, about the line in its plane through the corner perpendicular to the diagonal, or about its normal, but not about one of its edges (Morin §9.3, Example; Appendix E).

^rem-b10-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: The ellipsoid of constant kinetic energy
> For a fixed kinetic energy $T$, the angular velocities allowed by $\vec\omega^{\mathsf T}\mathsf I\vec\omega = 2T$ fill an ellipsoid whose axes are the principal axes, with semi-axes $\sqrt{2T/I_k}$: long along the axis of smallest moment. (Scaled by $1/\sqrt{2T}$ it is the inertia ellipsoid $\vec x^{\mathsf T}\mathsf I\vec x = 1$, whose distance from $O$ along any $\hat n$ is $1/\sqrt{I_n}$.) The gradient of $\tfrac12\vec\omega^{\mathsf T}\mathsf I\vec\omega$ with respect to $\vec\omega$ is $\mathsf I\vec\omega = \vec L$, so $\vec L$ is the normal to the ellipsoid at the point $\vec\omega$. Only at the ends of the principal axes is the normal along the radius: the geometric form of $\vec L \parallel \vec\omega$ exactly for principal axes. This is the picture of a symmetric matrix as stretching along perpendicular axes ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]], remark). H&S Example 12.12 uses the companion ellipsoid in $\vec L$-space for free rotation ([[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]]).

^rem-b10-1-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B10.1.1: A Cube about Its Corner
> Find the inertia tensor of a uniform solid cube (mass $M$, side $\ell$) about a corner, with axes along the edges, and its principal axes there.
>
> *The tensor.* By symmetry all diagonal entries are $\int(y^2 + z^2)\,dm$ and all off-diagonal entries are $-\int xy\,dm$, with $dm = (M/\ell^3)\,dx\,dy\,dz$ over $[0, \ell]^3$:
>
> $$
> \int(y^2 + z^2)\,dm = \tfrac23M\ell^2, \qquad -\int xy\,dm = -\frac{M}{\ell^3}\,\ell\Bigl(\frac{\ell^2}{2}\Bigr)^2 = -\tfrac14M\ell^2, \qquad
> \mathsf I = M\ell^2\begin{pmatrix} 2/3 & -1/4 & -1/4 \\ -1/4 & 2/3 & -1/4 \\ -1/4 & -1/4 & 2/3\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> Spinning about the edge $\hat z$, the cube has $\vec L = M\ell^2\omega(-\tfrac14, -\tfrac14, \tfrac23)$, not along $\hat z$.
>
> *Check by the parallel-axis theorem.* About its centre all three principal moments of the cube are equal, $\tfrac16M\ell^2$, so $\mathsf I^{\text{cm}} = \tfrac16M\ell^2\,\mathbb 1$ (Theorem §B10.1.6). With $\vec D = \tfrac\ell2(1, 1, 1)$, $D^2 = \tfrac34\ell^2$, [[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^thm-b10-1-4|Theorem §B10.1.4]] gives diagonal entries $\tfrac16 + \tfrac34 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac23$ and off-diagonal entries $-\tfrac14$, in units of $M\ell^2$, as above.
>
> *Principal axes.* A matrix $a\mathbb 1 + b(\mathsf J - \mathbb 1)$, with $\mathsf J$ the matrix of ones, has eigenvector $(1, 1, 1)$ with eigenvalue $a + 2b$, and every vector perpendicular to it with eigenvalue $a - b$. Here $a = \tfrac23$, $b = -\tfrac14$:
>
> $$
> I_{\text{diag}} = \tfrac16M\ell^2 \ \text{ about the body diagonal}, \qquad I_\perp = \tfrac{11}{12}M\ell^2 \ \text{ about every axis through the corner perpendicular to it.}
> $$
>
> Both are expected: the diagonal passes through the centre, where every moment is $\tfrac16M\ell^2$, and a perpendicular axis through the corner is at distance $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\ell$ from the centre, giving $\tfrac16 + \tfrac34 = \tfrac{11}{12}$. The repeated eigenvalue makes the perpendicular plane a plane of principal axes (Theorem §B10.1.6 (1)). The same steps for a square plate about a corner give $\tfrac{7}{12}$, $\tfrac{1}{12}$, $\tfrac23$ times $Ma^2$ (Morin Appendix E).
>
> *Source: Morin §9.2.1 (Example: cube with origin at corner); Exercise 9.34*

^ex-b10-1-1

> [!example] Example §B10.1.2: Striking a Floating Triangle
> Three masses joined by light rods float in space: $A$ ($m$) at $(-2a, -a, 0)$, $B$ ($m$) at $(2a, -a, 0)$, $C$ ($2m$) at $(0, a, 0)$, positions from the centre of mass (an isosceles right triangle with hypotenuse $AB = 4a$). $B$ is struck with an impulse $P$ along $-\hat z$. Find the velocities just after the blow.
>
> *Angular momentum.* About the centre of mass, the angular impulse is $\vec L = \vec r_B\times(0, 0, -P) = aP\,(1, 2, 0)$.
>
> *Principal moments.* The products of inertia vanish ($\sum mxy = m(2a^2) + m(-2a^2) = 0$, and $z = 0$), so $x, y, z$ are principal, with $I_x = \sum my^2 = 4ma^2$, $I_y = \sum mx^2 = 8ma^2$, $I_z = 12ma^2$.
>
> *Angular velocity.* $I_x\omega_x = aP$, $I_y\omega_y = 2aP$, $\omega_z = 0$ give $\vec\omega = \dfrac{P}{4ma}(1, 1, 0)$, which is not parallel to $\vec L \propto (1, 2, 0)$.
>
> *Velocities.* Relative to the centre of mass $\vec u = \vec\omega\times\vec r$: $\vec u_A = \vec u_C = (0, 0, P/4m)$, $\vec u_B = (0, 0, -3P/4m)$. The centre of mass moves at $\vec P/4m = (0, 0, -P/4m)$. Adding,
>
> $$
> \vec v_A = \vec 0, \qquad \vec v_B = (0, 0, -P/m), \qquad \vec v_C = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> $B$ takes the whole impulse and $A$, $C$ stay momentarily at rest: $B$ can move a distance $\epsilon$ while changing its distances to $A$ and $C$ only by order $\epsilon^2$, so the rods need not push on them. Afterwards the body tumbles: $\vec L$ is fixed but $\vec\omega$ is not ([[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Morin §9.4.1*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Diagonalising the inertia tensor is the real spectral theorem ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]) applied to the quadratic form $2T = \vec\omega^{\mathsf T}\mathsf I\vec\omega$ ([[§32 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-23|LADR Thm. 9.23]]). The rule $\mathsf I \to \mathsf A\mathsf I\mathsf A^{\mathsf T}$ for a change of orthonormal basis is what makes $\mathsf I$ a tensor; the rotation matrices $\mathsf A$ have orthonormal columns ([[§25 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-49|LADR Thm. 7.49]]).
> - The same algebra (a symmetric matrix, real eigenvalues, perpendicular eigenvectors) gives the normal modes of coupled oscillators ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|WO Theorem §B2.1.5]]) and, with Hermitian matrices, the observables of quantum mechanics (Morin Appendix D, Remark).
> - Moments of inertia about the coordinate axes ([[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^def-b3-2-1|Def. §B3.2.1]]) are the diagonal of $\mathsf I$; the perpendicular-axis theorem is [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]].
