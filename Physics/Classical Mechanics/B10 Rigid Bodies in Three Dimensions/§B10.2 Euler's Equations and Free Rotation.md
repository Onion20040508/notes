The law of motion for rotation is $d\vec L/dt = \vec\tau$, about a point fixed in an inertial frame ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]]) or about the centre of mass ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]). For a rigid body $\vec L = \mathsf I\vec\omega$ is simple only in the principal axes ([[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^def-b10-1-2|Def. §B10.1.2]]), and those turn with the body. Writing $d\vec L/dt$ in the turning frame with [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]] gives Euler's equations. With no torque they describe free rotation: the wobble of a thrown plate or of the Earth, and the tumbling of a tennis racket spun about its middle axis.

## Euler's equations

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.1: Euler's Equations
> Let $\hat e_1, \hat e_2, \hat e_3$ be principal axes of a rigid body about a point $O$ that is either fixed in an inertial frame or the centre of mass, with principal moments $I_1, I_2, I_3$. Let $\omega_i$ and $\tau_i$ be the components of the angular velocity and of the external torque about $O$ along the instantaneous principal axes. Then
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \tau_1 &= I_1\dot\omega_1 + (I_3 - I_2)\,\omega_2\omega_3, \\
> \tau_2 &= I_2\dot\omega_2 + (I_1 - I_3)\,\omega_3\omega_1, \\
> \tau_3 &= I_3\dot\omega_3 + (I_2 - I_1)\,\omega_1\omega_2 .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> The second and third follow from the first by cyclic permutation $1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1$.
>
> *Source: Morin §9.5, eq. (9.45) · H&S §12.12, eq. (12.183)*

^thm-b10-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]] applied to the frame of the principal axes, which turns with the body's angular velocity $\vec\omega$ ([[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]),
>
> $$
> \vec\tau = \Bigl(\frac{d\vec L}{dt}\Bigr)_{\text{in}} = \Bigl(\frac{d\vec L}{dt}\Bigr)_{\text{body}} + \vec\omega\times\vec L .
> $$
>
> In the principal frame $\vec L = (I_1\omega_1, I_2\omega_2, I_3\omega_3)$ with constant $I_i$, so $(d\vec L/dt)_{\text{body}} = (I_1\dot\omega_1, I_2\dot\omega_2, I_3\dot\omega_3)$. The first component of $\vec\omega\times\vec L$ is $\omega_2 I_3\omega_3 - \omega_3 I_2\omega_2 = (I_3 - I_2)\omega_2\omega_3$, and cyclically. The relation $d\vec L/dt = \vec\tau$ holds about $O$ by [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]] for a fixed $O$ and by [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]] for the centre of mass.

*Uses:* [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]], [[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]], [[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^def-b10-1-2|Def. §B10.1.2]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]], [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]

> [!caution] Caution: Components along moving axes
> Both sides are projections onto the axes *at this instant*: $\tau_1$ is $\vec\tau\cdot\hat e_1(t)$, and on the left one differentiates $\vec L$ first and projects afterwards. A torque with constant components $(0, 0, \tau_3)$ is not a constant vector; it turns with $\hat e_3$. And $\dot\omega_i$ is the rate of change of the component $\omega_i$, not a component of a body-frame derivative of some other vector (for $\vec\omega$ itself the two agree, [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]). Morin notes that Euler's equations are never indispensable: $\vec\tau = (d\vec L/dt)_{\text{body}} + \vec\omega\times\vec L$ can always be used directly, and for steady motions that is usually quicker.

^cau-b10-2-1

> [!example] Example §B10.2.1: A Stick Swinging in a Cone
> A uniform stick of mass $m$ and length $\ell$, pivoted at its upper end, swings round the vertical at constant angular speed $\omega$, always at angle $\theta$ to the vertical. Find $\omega$.
>
> Use the pivot as origin. The principal axes are the stick, $\hat e_s$ ($I_s = 0$), the perpendicular $\hat e_1$ in the vertical plane of the stick ($I_1 = m\ell^2/3$), and the horizontal perpendicular $\hat e_2$ ($I_2 = m\ell^2/3$). The angular velocity $\omega\hat z$ is vertical, so $\vec\omega = \omega\sin\theta\,\hat e_1 + \omega\cos\theta\,\hat e_s$ and
>
> $$
> \vec L = \tfrac13 m\ell^2\,\omega\sin\theta\;\hat e_1 ,
> $$
>
> not along $\vec\omega$. The components of $\vec L$ in the body frame are constant, so $d\vec L/dt = \vec\omega\times\vec L$, horizontal, of magnitude $\tfrac13m\ell^2\omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta$: the tip of $\vec L$ runs round a horizontal circle of radius $L\cos\theta$ at rate $\omega$. Gravity at the centre of mass gives a torque about the pivot of magnitude $\tfrac12 mg\ell\sin\theta$ in the same direction. Equating,
>
> $$
> \omega = \sqrt{\frac{3g}{2\ell\cos\theta}} .
> $$
>
> A point mass on a light rod of length $\ell$ gives the conical pendulum's $\sqrt{g/(\ell\cos\theta)}$ ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^ex-a3-9-2|Example §A3.9.2]]), so the uniform stick behaves like a point mass at $\tfrac23\ell$. As $\theta \to 0$ the frequency tends to $\sqrt{3g/2\ell}$, not to zero. The torque $\vec\omega\times\vec L$ needed to keep a body turning steadily about a non-principal axis is what an unbalanced wheel exerts on its bearings.
>
> *Source: Morin §9.4.2*

## Free rotation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.2: Constants of Free Rotation
> With no torque about the centre of mass, $\vec L$ is a constant vector in the inertial frame and the kinetic energy of rotation $T$ (level A's $K$) is constant. In body components,
>
> $$
> L_1^2 + L_2^2 + L_3^2 = L^2, \qquad \frac{L_1^2}{I_1} + \frac{L_2^2}{I_2} + \frac{L_3^2}{I_3} = 2T ,
> $$
>
> so the tip of $\vec L$, as seen from the body, moves on the intersection of a sphere and an ellipsoid. If $I_1 \ge I_2 \ge I_3$, then $2TI_3 \le L^2 \le 2TI_1$ and the two surfaces always meet.
>
> *Source: H&S §12.12, Example 12.12 · Morin Exercise 9.33*

^thm-b10-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\vec L$ is constant by [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-4|Theorem §A6.6.4]] (about the centre of mass, [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]), so its length is constant, whatever frame its components are taken in. For the energy, put $\tau_i = 0$ in [[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]], multiply the $i$-th equation by $\omega_i$ and add: the three $\omega_1\omega_2\omega_3$ terms carry the coefficients $(I_3 - I_2) + (I_1 - I_3) + (I_2 - I_1) = 0$, so $\frac{d}{dt}\tfrac12\sum I_i\omega_i^2 = 0$. With $L_i = I_i\omega_i$, $2T = \sum L_i^2/I_i$ ([[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^def-b10-1-2|Def. §B10.1.2]]). Finally $2TI_1 - L^2 = \sum_i L_i^2(I_1 - I_i)/I_i \ge 0$, and likewise $L^2 - 2TI_3 \ge 0$, so the ellipsoid's shortest semi-axis $\sqrt{2TI_3}$ is at most the sphere's radius $L$ and its longest $\sqrt{2TI_1}$ at least $L$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.3: The Free Symmetric Top
> Let $I_1 = I_2 = I$ (symmetry axis $\hat e_3$) and let no torque act about the centre of mass.
> 1. *Body view.* $\omega_3$ is constant, and the perpendicular part $(\omega_1, \omega_2)$ of $\vec\omega$, with $\vec L$, turns about $\hat e_3$ at the constant rate
>
>    $$
>    \Omega_b = \frac{I_3 - I}{I}\,\omega_3 ,
>    $$
>
>    in the sense of the spin for an **oblate** top ($I_3 > I$, a coin) and against it for a **prolate** top ($I_3 < I$, a pencil).
> 2. *Space view.* $\vec L$ is fixed; $\vec L$, $\vec\omega$ and $\hat e_3$ stay in one plane, and $\vec\omega = (L/I)\hat L - \Omega_b\,\hat e_3$. The symmetry axis and $\vec\omega$ precess about $\vec L$ at constant angles, with angular velocity
>
>    $$
>    \omega_{\text{pr}} = \frac{L}{I} .
>    $$
>
> 3. The angles $\alpha$ between $\hat e_3$ and $\vec L$ and $\beta$ between $\hat e_3$ and $\vec\omega$ satisfy $\tan\alpha = (I/I_3)\tan\beta$: $\vec L$ lies between $\vec\omega$ and $\hat e_3$ for an oblate top, $\vec\omega$ between $\vec L$ and $\hat e_3$ for a prolate one.
> 4. *Cones.* The line of $\vec\omega$ sweeps out the **body cone** about $\hat e_3$ (half-angle $\beta$) in the body and the **space cone** about $\vec L$ (half-angle $|\beta - \alpha|$) in space. The body cone rolls without slipping on the fixed space cone: on its outside for a prolate top, and enclosing it for an oblate top.
>
> *Source: Morin §9.6, eqs. (9.46)–(9.56); Problem 9.15 · H&S §12.11, eqs. (12.171), (12.178)*

^thm-b10-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) With $\tau_i = 0$ and $I_1 = I_2 = I$, [[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]] reads $I\dot\omega_1 + (I_3 - I)\omega_2\omega_3 = 0$, $I\dot\omega_2 - (I_3 - I)\omega_1\omega_3 = 0$, $I_3\dot\omega_3 = 0$. So $\omega_3$ is constant and $\dot\omega_1 = -\Omega_b\,\omega_2$, $\dot\omega_2 = \Omega_b\,\omega_1$, solved by $\omega_1 = A\cos(\Omega_b t + \phi)$, $\omega_2 = A\sin(\Omega_b t + \phi)$: a circle traced counterclockwise about $\hat e_3$ if $\Omega_b > 0$. $L_1 = I\omega_1$, $L_2 = I\omega_2$ turn with it.
>
> (2) Write $\vec\omega = \vec\omega_\perp + \omega_3\hat e_3$ and $\vec L = I\vec\omega_\perp + I_3\omega_3\hat e_3$. Eliminating $\vec\omega_\perp$, $\vec L = I\vec\omega + (I_3 - I)\omega_3\hat e_3 = I(\vec\omega + \Omega_b\hat e_3)$, which is the stated $\vec\omega$ and shows the three vectors are coplanar. $\vec L$ is constant ([[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]]). The body-fixed $\hat e_3$ changes as $\dot{\hat e}_3 = \vec\omega\times\hat e_3 = (L/I)\hat L\times\hat e_3$ ([[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]]), which is rotation about the fixed $\hat L$ at rate $L/I$; $\vec\omega$, a fixed combination of $\hat L$ and $\hat e_3$, follows.
>
> (3) $\tan\beta = \omega_\perp/\omega_3$ and $\tan\alpha = L_\perp/L_3 = I\omega_\perp/(I_3\omega_3)$. For $I_3 > I$, $\alpha < \beta$.
>
> (4) The points of the body on the line of $\vec\omega$ through the centre of mass are momentarily at rest relative to it ([[§B10.1 The Inertia Tensor and Principal Axes#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]]). The body cone is fixed in the body and the space cone is fixed in space; at each instant they touch along the line of $\vec\omega$, and since the body's points on that line are at rest, the body cone rolls on the space cone without slipping. The angle between $\vec\omega$ and $\vec L$ is $|\beta - \alpha|$. For a prolate top $\vec\omega$ lies between $\vec L$ and $\hat e_3$, so the two cones lie on opposite sides of their common line; for an oblate top $\vec L$ lies inside the body cone.
>
> *Consistency.* The body turns relative to the plane of $\vec L$, $\vec\omega$, $\hat e_3$ at $-\Omega_b\hat e_3$ (by (1)) and that plane turns in space at $(L/I)\hat L$ (by (2)); by [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]] the body's angular velocity is the sum $(L/I)\hat L - \Omega_b\hat e_3$, which is $\vec\omega$ (Morin §9.6.2, Remark 2).

> [!caution] Caution: Two precession rates
> The body-frame rate $\Omega_b = (I_3 - I)\omega_3/I$ and the space-frame rate $L/I$ describe the same motion seen from two frames and need not be equal; for a nearly spherical body $\Omega_b$ is tiny while $L/I \approx \omega_3$. Neither is a torque-driven precession: that of a heavy top is [[§B10.3 The Heavy Symmetric Top#^thm-b10-3-5|Theorem §B10.3.5]], and the slow precession of Earth's axis under the tidal torques of Sun and Moon is a third, unrelated motion. Note also that $\vec\omega$ does not obey $\dot{\vec\omega} = \vec\omega\times\vec\omega = \vec 0$: the rule $\dot{\vec A} = \vec\omega\times\vec A$ holds only for vectors fixed in the body.

^cau-b10-2-2

> [!example] Example §B10.2.2: Earth's Free Wobble
> Earth is a slightly oblate top, $(I_3 - I)/I \approx 1/300$ (Morin uses $1/320$), spinning at $\omega_3 = 2\pi/(1\ \text{day})$. By [[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]] (1), if the rotation axis is not exactly the figure axis, an observer on Earth sees the rotation axis circle the figure axis with period
>
> $$
> \frac{2\pi}{\Omega_b} = \frac{I}{I_3 - I}\,(1\ \text{day}) \approx 300\ \text{days} .
> $$
>
> The observed motion, the **Chandler wobble**, has a period of about 430 days: an elastic Earth yields to its own rotation and the wobble slows. The rotation axis pierces the surface about $10\ \mathrm m$ from the figure pole, so the half-angle of the body cone is about $10\ \mathrm m/R_E \approx 2\times10^{-6}$ rad. The pole of the sky, found as the centre of star trails in a long exposure, moves by too little to notice from night to night. Without a driving force (now attributed to pressure changes in the oceans and the atmosphere) the non-rigid Earth would damp the wobble away.
>
> *Source: Morin §9.6.1 (Example: the earth) · H&S §12.11, eq. (12.172)*

^ex-b10-2-2

> [!example] Example §B10.2.3: A Wobbling Plate
> A thin uniform disc has $I_3 = 2I$ (perpendicular-axis theorem, [[§B3.2 Rotation about a Fixed Axis#^thm-b3-2-4|Theorem §B3.2.4]]). Thrown with its axis slightly off its angular momentum, by a small angle $\alpha$, how fast does it wobble compared with its spin?
>
> In the body frame $\Omega_b = (2I - I)\omega_3/I = \omega_3$. In space the axis precesses about $\vec L$ at $L/I$, and $L\cos\alpha = L_3 = I_3\omega_3 = 2I\omega_3$, so
>
> $$
> \omega_{\text{pr}} = \frac{L}{I} = \frac{2\omega_3}{\cos\alpha} \approx 2\,\omega_3 :
> $$
>
> the wobble one sees is twice the rate at which the plate turns about its own axis. The angle between axis and $\vec\omega$ is $\beta$ with $\tan\beta = 2\tan\alpha$ ([[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]] (3)), so $\vec L$ lies between $\vec\omega$ and the axis, as for any oblate body.
>
> *Source: Morin §9.6 (applied to $I_3 = 2I$); Problem 9.15*

## Stability of rotation about the principal axes

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.4: Stability of Free Rotation (Tennis-Racket Theorem)
> Let a torque-free body have distinct principal moments $I_1 > I_2 > I_3$. Rotation about the axis of largest moment ($\hat e_1$) or of smallest moment ($\hat e_3$) is stable: if it starts close to the axis it stays close, and to first order the small components oscillate at
>
> $$
> \Omega_{(1)} = \omega_1\sqrt{\frac{(I_1 - I_2)(I_1 - I_3)}{I_2I_3}}, \qquad \Omega_{(3)} = \omega_3\sqrt{\frac{(I_1 - I_3)(I_2 - I_3)}{I_1I_2}} .
> $$
>
> Rotation about the intermediate axis $\hat e_2$ is unstable: small components grow as $e^{\gamma t}$ with
>
> $$
> \gamma = \omega_2\sqrt{\frac{(I_1 - I_2)(I_2 - I_3)}{I_1I_3}} ,
> $$
>
> until the body tumbles. A book or a racket thrown spinning about its middle axis turns over; about the other two it spins cleanly.
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 9.14; Exercise 9.33 · H&S §12.12, eqs. (12.184)–(12.186), Example 12.12*

^thm-b10-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Linearisation.* Let $\omega_1$ be large and $\omega_2, \omega_3$ small. The first Euler equation ([[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]], $\tau = 0$) gives $I_1\dot\omega_1 = (I_2 - I_3)\omega_2\omega_3$, second order, so $\omega_1$ is constant to first order. The other two become $I_2\dot\omega_2 = -(I_1 - I_3)\omega_1\omega_3$ and $I_3\dot\omega_3 = (I_1 - I_2)\omega_1\omega_2$. Differentiating the first and substituting the second,
>
> $$
> \ddot\omega_2 = -\frac{(I_1 - I_2)(I_1 - I_3)}{I_2I_3}\,\omega_1^2\,\omega_2 ,
> $$
>
> and the same for $\omega_3$. The coefficient is negative when $I_1$ is the largest or the smallest moment (both factors have the same sign), giving oscillation, and positive when $I_1$ is intermediate, giving exponential growth. Relabelling the axes gives the three cases stated.
>
> *A bound without linearisation.* From [[§B10.2 Euler's Equations and Free Rotation#^thm-b10-2-2|Theorem §B10.2.2]],
>
> $$
> 2TI_1 - L^2 = I_2(I_1 - I_2)\,\omega_2^2 + I_3(I_1 - I_3)\,\omega_3^2, \qquad L^2 - 2TI_3 = I_1(I_1 - I_3)\,\omega_1^2 + I_2(I_2 - I_3)\,\omega_2^2 ,
> $$
>
> both constant, with positive coefficients. Near $\hat e_1$ the first is small, and it then keeps $\omega_2$ and $\omega_3$ small for all time; near $\hat e_3$ the second does the same for $\omega_1, \omega_2$. Near $\hat e_2$ the corresponding constant is $L^2 - 2TI_2 = I_1(I_1 - I_2)\,\omega_1^2 - I_3(I_2 - I_3)\,\omega_3^2$, a difference: $\omega_1$ and $\omega_3$ can grow together without changing it, and the linear analysis shows that they do. Geometrically (H&S Example 12.12): on the sphere $|\vec L| = L$ the energy curves near the ends of $\hat e_1$ and $\hat e_3$ are small loops around them, while through the ends of $\hat e_2$ pass the two curves that separate the loops around $\hat e_1$ from those around $\hat e_3$.

*Euler's equations integrated numerically for $I_1 : I_2 : I_3 = 3 : 2 : 1$. > [!remark] Remark: What the tumble looks like
> In the lower panel $\omega_2$ goes from $+1$ to $-1$ in a few time units and stays there for a long while before flipping back. Since $\vec L$ is fixed in space, a reversal of the body component $L_2$ means that the body itself has turned over: the racket flips its face while still spinning about the same direction in space. The closer the start to the axis, the longer the quiet stretches between flips. With $I_1 : I_2 : I_3 = 3 : 2 : 1$ the conservation laws fix the size of the excursion: when $\omega_2$ passes through zero, $9\omega_1^2 + \omega_3^2 = 4$ and $3\omega_1^2 + \omega_3^2 = 2$ give $\omega_1 = 1/\sqrt3$ and $\omega_3 = \pm1$, as in the figure.

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The linearised equations are a $2\times2$ linear system whose matrix has eigenvalues $\pm i\Omega$ (stable) or $\pm\gamma$ (unstable); this is the same eigenvalue test as for small oscillations about an equilibrium ([[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^thm-b6-5-2|Theorem §B6.5.2]]), applied here to a steady rotation. Linear stability of fixed points of ODEs: Mathematical Methods (planned).
> - The two conserved quadratic forms of Theorem §B10.2.2 are diagonal in the same (principal) basis; the motion lies on the intersection of their level sets. With the heavy top ([[§B10.3 The Heavy Symmetric Top#^thm-b10-3-3|Theorem §B10.3.3]]) the torque breaks the conservation of $\vec L$ and only $L_z$ and $L_3$ survive.
