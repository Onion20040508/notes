*Sources: Morin, Introduction to Classical Mechanics, ch. 15 (bonus chapter), Problem 15.3 · Helliwell & Sahakian, Modern Classical Mechanics, §11.5–11.6, §15.8 · Series Part II (the user's notes), §8.*

Hamilton's equations ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations]]) say how the coordinates and momenta move; physics usually asks about other functions of the state, such as the energy or an angular momentum. Level A found the conservation laws one at a time from Newton's laws (energy, [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]; momentum, [[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]; angular momentum, [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-4|Theorem §A6.6.4]]), and [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem]] derived them from symmetries of the Lagrangian. The Poisson bracket gives one formula for the rate of change of every function on phase space. It turns "is this conserved?" into algebra, and it shows that a conserved quantity generates the symmetry that conserves it. Its algebra is the classical template for the commutators of quantum mechanics.

Throughout, the system has $n$ degrees of freedom with coordinates $q = (q_1, \dots, q_n)$, momenta $p = (p_1, \dots, p_n)$ and Hamiltonian $H(q, p, t)$ as in [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations]]. Potential energy is written $U$, as at level A (the series writes $V$).

## The bracket and its algebra

> [!definition] Definition §B8.1.1: Poisson Bracket
> For differentiable functions $f(q, p, t)$ and $g(q, p, t)$ on phase space, the **Poisson bracket** is the function
>
> $$
> \{f, g\} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\right),
> $$
>
> evaluated point by point; $t$, if present, is held fixed. This is the sign convention of the series, of Helliwell & Sahakian and of Morin; Landau & Lifshitz use the opposite sign, which reverses every bracket below.
>
> *Source: Series II Def. 8.1 · H&S §11.5, eq. (11.117) · Morin Problem 15.3*

^def-b8-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.1: Fundamental Brackets
> The canonical variables satisfy
>
> $$
> \{q_i, q_j\} = 0, \qquad \{p_i, p_j\} = 0, \qquad \{q_i, p_j\} = \delta_{ij} .
> $$
>
> More generally, for any differentiable $f$, $\{f, p_i\} = \partial f/\partial q_i$ and $\{q_i, f\} = \partial f/\partial p_i$: bracketing with $p_i$ differentiates along $q_i$, bracketing with $q_i$ differentiates along $p_i$.
>
> *Source: Series II §8.1, eq. (134) · H&S §11.5, eqs. (11.119)–(11.120)*

^thm-b8-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In $\{f, p_i\}$, $\partial p_i/\partial q_k = 0$ and $\partial p_i/\partial p_k = \delta_{ik}$, so only the term $(\partial f/\partial q_i)\cdot 1$ survives. Likewise $\{q_i, f\} = \sum_k \delta_{ik}\,\partial f/\partial p_k = \partial f/\partial p_i$. Taking $f = q_j$ in the first gives $\{q_j, p_i\} = \delta_{ij}$; $f = p_j$ gives $\{p_j, p_i\} = 0$; $f = q_j$ in the second gives $\{q_i, q_j\} = 0$.

^der-b8-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: The order of the entries
> The bracket is antisymmetric, so $\{p_i, q_j\} = -\delta_{ij}$. Helliwell & Sahakian write $\{p_a, q_b\} = \delta_{ab}$ and test canonicity with $\{P_a, Q_b\} = \delta_{ab}$ (eqs. (11.119), (11.121)); with their own definition (11.117) both left sides equal $-\delta_{ab}$. Their later formulas use the correct $\{q, p\} = 1$ (eq. (11.146)).

^cau-b8-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.2: Algebra of the Poisson Bracket
> For $C^2$ functions $f, g, h$ on phase space and constants $a, b$:
> 1. **Bilinearity:** $\{af + bg, h\} = a\{f, h\} + b\{g, h\}$, and likewise in the second slot.
> 2. **Antisymmetry:** $\{f, g\} = -\{g, f\}$; in particular $\{f, f\} = 0$.
> 3. **Leibniz rule:** $\{fg, h\} = f\{g, h\} + \{f, h\}\,g$. Consequently, for a differentiable function $F$ of one variable, $\{f, F(g)\} = F'(g)\,\{f, g\}$.
> 4. **Jacobi identity:**
>
> $$
> \{f, \{g, h\}\} + \{g, \{h, f\}\} + \{h, \{f, g\}\} = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Series II §8.1, items 1–4 and App. F · H&S §11.5, eqs. (11.131)–(11.134)*

^thm-b8-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Items 1–3 follow from the definition. Partial derivatives are linear, which gives 1. Exchanging $f$ and $g$ exchanges the two terms of each summand, which gives 2. For 3, apply the product rule $\partial(fg) = f\,\partial g + g\,\partial f$ inside both terms and regroup; the same with the chain rule $\partial F(g) = F'(g)\,\partial g$ gives the consequence.
>
> For the Jacobi identity, collect the $2n$ variables into $z = (q_1, \dots, q_n, p_1, \dots, p_n)$ and write $f_a = \partial f/\partial z_a$, $f_{ab} = \partial^2 f/\partial z_a\partial z_b$. Then
>
> $$
> \{f, g\} = \sum_{a,b} J_{ab}\,f_a\,g_b, \qquad J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \mathbb 1_n \\ -\mathbb 1_n & 0 \end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> with $J$ constant and antisymmetric, $J_{ba} = -J_{ab}$. Since $J$ is constant,
>
> $$
> \{f, \{g, h\}\} = \sum_{a,b,c,d} J_{ab}J_{cd}\,f_a\bigl(g_{bc}\,h_d + g_c\,h_{bd}\bigr),
> $$
>
> and every term of the Jacobi sum contains exactly one second derivative. Sort the terms by which function carries it. The second derivatives of $h$ come from two places: $T_1 = \sum J_{ab}J_{cd}\,f_a g_c h_{bd}$ from $\{f, \{g, h\}\}$ and $T_2 = \sum J_{ab}J_{cd}\,g_a h_{bc} f_d$ from $\{g, \{h, f\}\}$; in $\{h, \{f, g\}\}$, $h$ is differentiated only once. Rename the summation indices of $T_2$ by $(a, b, c, d) \to (c, d, b, a)$:
>
> $$
> T_2 = \sum J_{cd}J_{ba}\,g_c\,h_{db}\,f_a = -\sum J_{ab}J_{cd}\,f_a\,g_c\,h_{bd} = -T_1 ,
> $$
>
> using $J_{ba} = -J_{ab}$ and $h_{db} = h_{bd}$ (equality of mixed partials, [[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]). So the $h''$ terms cancel. The Jacobi sum is unchanged by the cyclic relabelling $f \to g \to h \to f$, so the $f''$ and $g''$ terms cancel in the same way, and the sum is zero. The identity is the symmetry of second derivatives, used three times. The series' Appendix F carries out the same sorting for one degree of freedom.

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]], [[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The structure behind the four rules
> Bilinearity, antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity make the phase-space functions a **Lie algebra** under the bracket; the Leibniz rule says that $g \mapsto \{f, g\}$ is a derivation, a first-order differential operator. At a single point, $\{f, g\} = \omega(\nabla f, \nabla g)$ with $\omega(u, v) = \sum_{a,b} J_{ab}u_av_b$, an alternating bilinear form on $\mathbb R^{2n}$ ([[§32 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-14|LADR Def. 9.14]], [[§32 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-16|LADR Thm. 9.16]]). Helliwell & Sahakian observe that these four properties alone already carry the dynamics ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^ex-b8-1-1|Example §B8.1.1]]), so anything else that obeys them can carry a dynamics too; the matrix commutator does.
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 8.5 · H&S §11.5, §15.8*

## Dynamics as a bracket

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.3: Time Evolution of a Phase-Space Function
> Along any solution of Hamilton's equations, every $C^1$ function $f(q, p, t)$ evolves by
>
> $$
> \frac{df}{dt} = \{f, H\} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> In particular:
> - $\dot q_i = \{q_i, H\}$ and $\dot p_i = \{p_i, H\}$: Hamilton's equations, with coordinates and momenta on the same footing;
> - $dH/dt = \partial H/\partial t$, because $\{H, H\} = 0$;
> - a function $f(q, p)$ with no explicit time dependence is a **constant of the motion** (constant along every solution) if and only if $\{f, H\} = 0$ on all of phase space.
>
> *Source: Series II Prop. 8.3, eqs. (136)–(139) · H&S §11.6, eqs. (11.135)–(11.138) · Morin Problem 15.3*

^thm-b8-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the chain rule along the trajectory ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]) and then Hamilton's equations $\dot q_i = \partial H/\partial p_i$, $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q_i$ ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations]]),
>
> $$
> \frac{df}{dt} = \sum_i\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i + \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i\right) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = \sum_i\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\right) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> For $f = q_i$ or $p_i$, [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]] gives $\{q_i, H\} = \partial H/\partial p_i$ and $\{p_i, H\} = -\partial H/\partial q_i$. For the last statement: if $\{f, H\} = 0$ then $df/dt = 0$. Conversely, through every point of phase space passes a solution (start one there), and $df/dt = \{f, H\}$ at that point, so a function constant along all solutions has $\{f, H\} = 0$ everywhere.

^der-b8-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]], [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Energy conservation as a bracket
> Level A proved conservation of mechanical energy from the work–energy theorem ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]). Here it is the one-line statement $dH/dt = \partial H/\partial t$: $H$ is conserved exactly when it has no explicit time dependence, and it is the mechanical energy when $H = T + U$ ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations]] says when that holds). Every other conservation law now has the same form, $\{f, H\} = 0$, to be checked by algebra rather than by integrating anything.

^rem-b8-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.4: Poisson's Theorem
> For $C^2$ functions $f(q, p, t)$ and $g(q, p, t)$, along any solution of Hamilton's equations
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\{f, g\} = \Bigl\{\frac{df}{dt}, g\Bigr\} + \Bigl\{f, \frac{dg}{dt}\Bigr\},
> $$
>
> where $df/dt = \{f, H\} + \partial f/\partial t$ is regarded as a function on phase space. Hence if $f$ and $g$ are constants of the motion, so is $\{f, g\}$.
>
> *Source: Series II Cor. 8.4 (time-independent case) · H&S §11.6 (stated, with the time-dependent version mentioned)*

^thm-b8-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]], $\frac{d}{dt}\{f, g\} = \{\{f, g\}, H\} + \partial_t\{f, g\}$. The Jacobi identity with antisymmetry rearranges the first term:
>
> $$
> \{\{f, g\}, H\} = -\{H, \{f, g\}\} = \{f, \{g, H\}\} + \{g, \{H, f\}\} = \{f, \{g, H\}\} + \{\{f, H\}, g\} .
> $$
>
> The bracket involves only $q$- and $p$-derivatives, so $\partial_t\{f, g\} = \{\partial_t f, g\} + \{f, \partial_t g\}$. Adding, and using bilinearity, gives $\{\{f, H\} + \partial_t f, g\} + \{f, \{g, H\} + \partial_t g\}$, which is the claim. If $df/dt$ and $dg/dt$ vanish as functions on phase space, the right side is zero.

^der-b8-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: New constants from old, sometimes
> Poisson's theorem manufactures constants of the motion by pure algebra, but the bracket often closes on what was already known (for angular momentum, [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^ex-b8-1-2|Example §B8.1.2]]) or gives a constant, or a function of known constants. Helliwell & Sahakian note that in many cases it yields nothing independent.

## Generators and symmetries

> [!definition] Definition §B8.1.2: Flow Generated by a Function
> Let $G(q, p)$ be a differentiable function on phase space. The **flow generated by** $G$ is the one-parameter family of maps of phase space obtained by solving Hamilton's equations with $G$ in place of $H$ and a parameter $s$ in place of $t$:
>
> $$
> \frac{dq_i}{ds} = \frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \frac{dp_i}{ds} = -\frac{\partial G}{\partial q_i}, \qquad\text{so that}\qquad \frac{df}{ds} = \{f, G\}
> $$
>
> for every function $f(q, p)$. $G$ is the **generator** of the flow. To first order in a small parameter $\epsilon$, the flow changes $f$ by $\delta f = \epsilon\,\{f, G\}$. Time evolution is the flow generated by $H$ (when $H$ has no explicit time dependence), with $s = t$.
>
> *Source: Series II §8.3, eq. (140) · H&S §11.6, eq. (11.140)*

^def-b8-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.5: Conserved Quantities Generate Symmetries
> Let $H(q, p)$ and $G(q, p)$ have no explicit time dependence. The following are equivalent:
> 1. $G$ is a constant of the motion;
> 2. $\{G, H\} = 0$;
> 3. $H$ is invariant along the flow generated by $G$: $dH/ds = 0$.
>
> If a point transformation with infinitesimal generator $\eta(q)$ is a symmetry with Noether charge $I = \sum_i p_i\,\eta_i(q)$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem]]), the flow generated by $I$ moves the coordinates by $dq_i/ds = \eta_i(q)$: it is the symmetry transformation itself. Examples: the momentum $p$ generates the translations $q \mapsto q + s$; the angular momentum $L_z$ generates rotations about the $z$ axis ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]); $H$ generates translations in time.
>
> *Source: Series II §8.3, eqs. (140)–(142) · H&S §11.6, eqs. (11.141)–(11.144), Example 11.11*

^thm-b8-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) ⟺ (2) is [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]. Along the flow of $G$, [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-2|Def. §B8.1.2]] gives $dH/ds = \{H, G\} = -\{G, H\}$, so (2) ⟺ (3) is antisymmetry and nothing more. For $I = \sum_j p_j\eta_j(q)$, $dq_i/ds = \partial I/\partial p_i = \eta_i(q)$. For $G = p$ (one degree of freedom), $dq/ds = 1$ and $dp/ds = 0$, so $q \mapsto q + s$ and $\delta f = \epsilon\,\{f, p\} = \epsilon\,\partial f/\partial q$, the first-order change of $f$ under $q \to q + \epsilon$.

^der-b8-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-2|Def. §B8.1.2]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]], [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem]]

> [!remark] Remark: Noether's theorem, both ways
> [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem]] runs one way: a symmetry of the Lagrangian gives a conserved quantity. In Hamiltonian form the two directions are one equation, $\{G, H\} = 0$, read from either end: $G$ is conserved by the flow of $H$, and $H$ is unchanged by the flow of $G$. The generator need not come from a point transformation; any function generates a flow. This is the form that survives into quantum mechanics, where $\hat p$ generates translations, $\hat L_z$ rotations and $\hat H$ time evolution.
>
> *Source: Series II §8.3 · H&S §11.6*

^rem-b8-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.6: Angular-Momentum Brackets
> For a particle with angular momentum $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p$ about the origin ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-1|Def. §A6.6.1]]), in Cartesian coordinates,
>
> $$
> \{L_x, L_y\} = L_z, \qquad \{L_y, L_z\} = L_x, \qquad \{L_z, L_x\} = L_y,
> $$
>
> $$
> \{L_z, x\} = y, \quad \{L_z, y\} = -x, \quad \{L_z, p_x\} = p_y, \quad \{L_z, p_y\} = -p_x, \quad \{L_z, z\} = \{L_z, p_z\} = 0,
> $$
>
> and $\{L_z, L^2\} = 0$ with $L^2 = L_x^2 + L_y^2 + L_z^2$; the same holds for $L_x$ and $L_y$ by cyclic relabelling of the axes. The flow generated by $L_z$ rotates $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ together about the $z$ axis through the angle $s$:
>
> $$
> (x, y) \mapsto (x\cos s - y\sin s,\ x\sin s + y\cos s), \qquad (p_x, p_y) \mapsto (p_x\cos s - p_y\sin s,\ p_x\sin s + p_y\cos s).
> $$
>
> For several particles, $\vec L$ is the sum over particles and the same brackets hold, since functions of different particles' variables have zero bracket.
>
> *Source: Series II §8.2 (stated, $\{L_x, L_y\} = L_z$), §8.3 (flow of $L_z$) · H&S §11.6*

^thm-b8-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $L_x = yp_z - zp_y$ and $L_y = zp_x - xp_z$, the only nonzero products in [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]] come from the $z$ pair:
>
> $$
> \{L_x, L_y\} = \frac{\partial L_x}{\partial z}\frac{\partial L_y}{\partial p_z} - \frac{\partial L_x}{\partial p_z}\frac{\partial L_y}{\partial z} = (-p_y)(-x) - (y)(p_x) = xp_y - yp_x = L_z .
> $$
>
> The other two follow by cyclic relabelling $x \to y \to z \to x$. With $L_z = xp_y - yp_x$, [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]] gives $\{L_z, x\} = -\{x, L_z\} = -\partial L_z/\partial p_x = y$, $\{L_z, y\} = -\partial L_z/\partial p_y = -x$, $\{L_z, p_x\} = \partial L_z/\partial x = p_y$, $\{L_z, p_y\} = \partial L_z/\partial y = -p_x$. By the Leibniz rule,
>
> $$
> \{L_z, L^2\} = 2L_x\{L_z, L_x\} + 2L_y\{L_z, L_y\} + 2L_z\{L_z, L_z\} = 2L_xL_y - 2L_yL_x + 0 = 0 .
> $$
>
> The flow equations are $dx/ds = \{x, L_z\} = -y$, $dy/ds = x$, $dp_x/ds = -p_y$, $dp_y/ds = p_x$, solved by the rotations shown.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.1.1: Dynamics from the Algebra Alone
> Take $H = p^2/2m + U(q)$ for one degree of freedom, and use only the rules of [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]] and $\{q, p\} = 1$, $\{q, q\} = \{p, p\} = 0$, never the derivative formula. By the Leibniz rule $\{q, p^2\} = p\{q, p\} + \{q, p\}p = 2p$, and by its consequence $\{q, U(q)\} = U'(q)\{q, q\} = 0$, so
>
> $$
> \dot q = \{q, H\} = \frac{1}{2m}\{q, p^2\} + \{q, U(q)\} = \frac pm .
> $$
>
> Similarly $\{p, p^2\} = 0$ and $\{p, U(q)\} = U'(q)\{p, q\} = -U'(q)$, so $\dot p = -U'(q)$. Together, $m\ddot q = -U'(q)$: Newton's second law with the force derived from the potential energy ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]]). Nothing beyond the four rules and the fundamental brackets was used, so any operation obeying them (the commutator divided by $i\hbar$, [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^rem-b8-1-5|the remark below]]) reproduces these equations step for step.
>
> *Source: H&S Example 11.12 (there with a Taylor expansion of $U$ in place of the chain rule)*

^ex-b8-1-1

> [!example] Example §B8.1.2: Angular Momentum in a Central Field
> A particle moves in a central field, $H = p^2/2m + U(r)$ with $r = |\vec r|$. Show by brackets that $\vec L$ is conserved, and see what Poisson's theorem adds.
>
> Write $p^2 = p_x^2 + p_y^2 + p_z^2$ and $U(r) = F(r^2)$ with $F(u) = U(\sqrt u)$. From [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]] and the Leibniz rule,
>
> $$
> \{L_z, p^2\} = 2p_x\,p_y + 2p_y(-p_x) + 0 = 0, \qquad \{L_z, r^2\} = 2x\,y + 2y(-x) + 0 = 0,
> $$
>
> and $\{L_z, F(r^2)\} = F'(r^2)\{L_z, r^2\} = 0$. So $\{L_z, H\} = 0$: $L_z$ is conserved ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]), and read the other way, $H$ is unchanged by rotations about $z$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-5|Theorem §B8.1.5]]). Relabelling the axes, $L_x$ and $L_y$ are conserved too. Level A obtained this from the vanishing torque of a central force ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-4|Theorem §A6.6.4]]); here it is the rotational symmetry of $H$.
>
> Poisson's theorem ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-4|Theorem §B8.1.4]]) gives $\{L_x, L_y\} = L_z$ conserved, which was already known: the bracket closes. It still says something: *any* Hamiltonian that conserves two components of $\vec L$ conserves the third, so invariance under rotations about two axes forces invariance about the third. The three components cannot all be new momenta of one canonical set, since momenta have zero mutual brackets ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]]) and these do not; $L^2$ and $L_z$ pass that test, $\{L_z, L^2\} = 0$.
>
> *Source: Series II §8.2 (Poisson's theorem and $\{L_x, L_y\} = L_z$) · H&S §11.6*

^ex-b8-1-2

## Toward quantum mechanics

> [!remark] Remark: From brackets to commutators
> Quantum mechanics keeps the whole algebra of [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]] and changes the objects: phase-space functions become operators, and
>
> $$
> \{f, g\} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{i\hbar}\,[\hat f, \hat g], \qquad [\hat f, \hat g] = \hat f\hat g - \hat g\hat f .
> $$
>
> The commutator is bilinear, antisymmetric, obeys the Leibniz rule (in the operator order shown in item 3) and the Jacobi identity. In particular $\{q, p\} = 1$ becomes $[\hat q, \hat p] = i\hbar$, from which the uncertainty relation ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|QM Theorem §A3.5.1]]) can be derived, and $\dot q = \{q, H\}$ becomes $i\hbar\,d\hat q/dt = [\hat q, \hat H]$. For the oscillator the steps of [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^ex-b8-1-1|Example §B8.1.1]] give $d\hat q/dt = \hat p/m$ and $d\hat p/dt = -k\hat q$ (H&S Example 15.6). This is a prescription, not a derivation: classical mechanics does not imply quantum mechanics, the state space and the meaning of operators must be added, and products such as $qp$ have no unique operator ordering. The operator formalism is Quantum Mechanics level B (planned).
>
