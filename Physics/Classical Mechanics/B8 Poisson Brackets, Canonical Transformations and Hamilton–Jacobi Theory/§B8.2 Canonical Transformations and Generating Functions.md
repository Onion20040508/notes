*Sources: Morin, Introduction to Classical Mechanics, ch. 15 (bonus chapter), Problem 15.5 · Helliwell & Sahakian, Modern Classical Mechanics, §11.4–11.6 · Series Part II (the user's notes), §8.1, §10.1–10.2.*

Lagrange's equations keep their form under any smooth change of the coordinates $q$ ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-3|Theorem §B6.2.3]]), and a good choice can split a problem apart, as normal coordinates do for small oscillations ([[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^thm-b6-5-3|Theorem §B6.5.3]]). The Hamiltonian formalism has twice as many variables, and nothing in Hamilton's equations ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-2|Theorem §B7.1.2]]) distinguishes coordinates from momenta except a sign. This section asks which changes of phase-space variables, mixing $q$ and $p$ freely, keep Hamilton's equations Hamiltonian. It gives two tests, one through the Poisson brackets of [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets]] and one through the action, and a way to produce such changes from a single function, the generating function. The examples include one that turns the harmonic oscillator into a free rotation of a phase angle, which is the idea that the Hamilton–Jacobi method of [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation]] makes systematic.

## Canonical transformations

> [!definition] Definition §B8.2.1: Canonical Transformation
> A smooth, invertible, possibly time-dependent change of phase-space variables
>
> $$
> Q_i = Q_i(q, p, t), \qquad P_i = P_i(q, p, t), \qquad i = 1, \dots, n,
> $$
>
> is **canonical** if for every Hamiltonian $H(q, p, t)$ there is a function $K(Q, P, t)$, the **new Hamiltonian**, such that every solution of Hamilton's equations for $(q, p; H)$ is carried to a solution of
>
> $$
> \dot Q_i = \frac{\partial K}{\partial P_i}, \qquad \dot P_i = -\frac{\partial K}{\partial Q_i} .
> $$
>
> The demand "for every $H$" makes this a property of the change of variables, not of one system.
>
> *Source: Series II Def. 10.1 · H&S §11.4, eqs. (11.56)–(11.60)*

^def-b8-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.1: Bracket Criterion
> A smooth invertible time-independent transformation $Q(q, p)$, $P(q, p)$ whose new variables obey the fundamental brackets ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]]), computed in the old variables,
>
> $$
> \{Q_i, Q_j\} = 0, \qquad \{P_i, P_j\} = 0, \qquad \{Q_i, P_j\} = \delta_{ij},
> $$
>
> is canonical, and the new Hamiltonian is the old one rewritten: $K(Q, P, t) = H(q, p, t)$.
>
> *Source: Series II Prop. 10.2 · H&S §11.5, eq. (11.121) (as a test)*

^thm-b8-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Define $K$ by $K(Q(q, p), P(q, p), t) = H(q, p, t)$. Since $Q_i$ has no explicit time dependence, [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]] gives $\dot Q_i = \{Q_i, H\}$ along every solution. Write $H = K(Q, P, t)$ and expand the bracket with the chain rule ([[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]), $\partial H/\partial p_a = \sum_j\bigl(\partial K/\partial Q_j\,\partial Q_j/\partial p_a + \partial K/\partial P_j\,\partial P_j/\partial p_a\bigr)$ and likewise for $\partial H/\partial q_a$. Grouping the terms by the derivative of $K$ they carry,
>
> $$
> \{Q_i, H\} = \sum_j \frac{\partial K}{\partial Q_j}\,\{Q_i, Q_j\} + \sum_j \frac{\partial K}{\partial P_j}\,\{Q_i, P_j\} = \frac{\partial K}{\partial P_i} .
> $$
>
> The same computation for $P_i$ gives $\dot P_i = \sum_j \partial K/\partial Q_j\,\{P_i, Q_j\} + \sum_j \partial K/\partial P_j\,\{P_i, P_j\} = -\partial K/\partial Q_i$, using $\{P_i, Q_j\} = -\{Q_j, P_i\} = -\delta_{ij}$. Both of Hamilton's equations hold in the new variables, for any $H$.

^der-b8-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]], [[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: The converse needs a normalization
> The series (Example 8.2, "precisely when") and Helliwell & Sahakian (§11.5) also state the converse, that a canonical transformation preserves the fundamental brackets; the series proves only the direction above (Prop. 10.2), which is all its applications use. Under [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^def-b8-2-1|Def. §B8.2.1]] as written this fails for rescalings: $Q = \lambda q$, $P = p$ with a constant $\lambda \neq 0, 1$ carries Hamilton's equations for $H$ into Hamilton's equations for $K = \lambda H$ (check: $\dot Q = \lambda\,\partial H/\partial p = \partial K/\partial P$ and $\dot P = -\partial H/\partial q = -\partial K/\partial Q$), yet $\{Q, P\} = \lambda$. The transformations produced by the total-derivative identity of [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]], which include all those from generating functions, are the ones for which Helliwell & Sahakian state that every bracket is preserved, $\{A, B\}_{q,p} = \{A, B\}_{Q,P}$ (eq. (11.118)); the rescaling is not among them, since $p\dot q - H - (P\dot Q - K) = (1 - \lambda)(p\dot q - H)$ is not a total time derivative.

^cau-b8-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Normal coordinates are canonical
> For small oscillations with mass matrix $\mathsf M$ and $\mathsf M$-orthonormal mode shapes $\vec v_r$ ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|WO Theorem §B2.1.5]], [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^thm-b6-5-3|Theorem §B6.5.3]]; for the double pendulum, [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^ex-b7-1-3|Example §B7.1.3]]), the mode variables $\xi_r = \vec v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\,\vec q$ and $\pi_r = \vec v_r^{\mathsf T}\vec p$ satisfy
>
> $$
> \{\xi_r, \pi_s\} = \sum_i (\mathsf M\vec v_r)_i\,(\vec v_s)_i = \vec v_s^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\,\vec v_r = \delta_{rs},
> $$
>
> and $\{\xi_r, \xi_s\} = \{\pi_r, \pi_s\} = 0$, since each $\xi$ depends on the coordinates only and each $\pi$ on the momenta only. By [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]] the passage to normal coordinates is canonical: the mass-weighted orthonormality of the modes, met there as linear algebra, is exactly the condition for it.
>
> *Source: Series II Example 8.2*

^rem-b8-2-1

## The action criterion

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.2: Equivalence up to a Total Derivative
> Let $(q, p) \mapsto (Q, P)$ be smooth and invertible, and suppose there are smooth functions $K(Q, P, t)$ and $F$ (of the phase-space variables and $t$) such that, along every smooth curve in phase space,
>
> $$
> \sum_i p_i\dot q_i - H = \sum_i P_i\dot Q_i - K + \frac{dF}{dt} .
> $$
>
> Then a curve solves Hamilton's equations for $(q, p; H)$ if and only if its image solves Hamilton's equations for $(Q, P; K)$. In particular the transformation is canonical, with new Hamiltonian $K$.
>
> *Source: Series II Prop. 10.3 · H&S §11.4, eqs. (11.61)–(11.64)*

^thm-b8-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Hamilton's equations are the condition that the **phase-space action** $S[q, p] = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\bigl(\sum_i p_i\dot q_i - H\bigr)\,dt$ be stationary when $q$ and $p$ are varied independently ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-3|Theorem §B7.1.3]]). Use variations $\delta q$, $\delta p$ that vanish on whole neighbourhoods of $t_0$ and $t_1$. One integration by parts, whose boundary term vanishes, gives
>
> $$
> \delta S = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\sum_i\Bigl[\Bigl(\dot q_i - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\Bigr)\delta p_i - \Bigl(\dot p_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\Bigr)\delta q_i\Bigr]dt ,
> $$
>
> and by the fundamental lemma ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|Lemma §B5.1.2]]) $\delta S = 0$ for all such variations exactly when Hamilton's equations hold. Now integrate the hypothesis along a curve: $S[q, p] = S'[Q, P] + F\big|_{t_1} - F\big|_{t_0}$, where $S'$ is the phase-space action built from $(Q, P; K)$. A variation vanishing near the ends leaves $F$ at the ends unchanged, so $\delta S = \delta S'$. Since the transformation is smooth and invertible, variations of $(q, p)$ vanishing near the ends correspond exactly to variations of $(Q, P)$ vanishing near the ends. So $\delta S = 0$ for all of them iff $\delta S' = 0$ for all of them, that is, the old equations hold iff the new ones do.

^der-b8-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-3|Theorem §B7.1.3]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|Lemma §B5.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Pinning the ends is not enough
> The usual argument fixes $q$ at the ends of the time interval and declares both boundary terms dead. But when $Q$ mixes coordinates and momenta, $\delta q(t_0) = 0$ does not force $\delta Q(t_0) = 0$; the boundary term $\bigl[\sum_i(p_i\delta q_i - P_i\delta Q_i)\bigr]_{t_0}^{t_1}$ then survives in part. Variations that vanish near both ends remove every boundary term at once, and they are all the fundamental lemma needs.
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 10.4*

^cau-b8-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: The same freedom one level down
> Adding a total derivative $dF(q, t)/dt$ to a Lagrangian does not change its equations of motion (Morin Problem 15.5); Morin notes that this freedom is the seed of canonical transformations. In phase space the function $F$ may depend on momenta too, which is what lets $Q$ and $P$ mix the old coordinates and momenta. In the language of forms, the identity says that the 1-form $\sum_i p_i\,dq_i - H\,dt - \bigl(\sum_i P_i\,dQ_i - K\,dt\bigr)$ on extended phase space is exact, equal to $dF$ ([[§39 Closed and Exact Forms#^def-39-3|452 Def. §39.3]]).
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 15.5 and footnote 16*

^rem-b8-2-2

## Generating functions

> [!definition] Definition §B8.2.2: Generating Function
> A **generating function** of a canonical transformation is a smooth function of $n$ old and $n$ new phase-space variables and $t$, of one of four types,
>
> $$
> F_1(q, Q, t), \qquad F_2(q, P, t), \qquad F_3(p, Q, t), \qquad F_4(p, P, t),
> $$
>
> whose mixed second-derivative matrix in its two sets of arguments is invertible (for example $\det\bigl(\partial^2F_2/\partial q_i\,\partial P_j\bigr) \ne 0$). It defines the transformation and the new Hamiltonian through the relations of [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]].
>
> *Source: Series II §10.2, Props. 10.5, 10.7, 10.8 · H&S §11.4, eqs. (11.66)–(11.90)*

^def-b8-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.3: Canonical Transformations from Generating Functions
> Each generating function defines, locally, a canonical transformation with new Hamiltonian
>
> $$
> K = H + \frac{\partial F}{\partial t}
> $$
>
> (the explicit time derivative of $F$ at fixed values of its own arguments), through
>
> | type | arguments | relations | nondegeneracy |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $F_1$ | $(q, Q, t)$ | $p_i = \partial F_1/\partial q_i$, $\quad P_i = -\partial F_1/\partial Q_i$ | $\det(\partial^2F_1/\partial q\,\partial Q) \neq 0$ |
> | $F_2$ | $(q, P, t)$ | $p_i = \partial F_2/\partial q_i$, $\quad Q_i = \partial F_2/\partial P_i$ | $\det(\partial^2F_2/\partial q\,\partial P) \neq 0$ |
> | $F_3$ | $(p, Q, t)$ | $q_i = -\partial F_3/\partial p_i$, $\quad P_i = -\partial F_3/\partial Q_i$ | $\det(\partial^2F_3/\partial p\,\partial Q) \neq 0$ |
> | $F_4$ | $(p, P, t)$ | $q_i = -\partial F_4/\partial p_i$, $\quad Q_i = \partial F_4/\partial P_i$ | $\det(\partial^2F_4/\partial p\,\partial P) \neq 0$ |
>
> For a time-independent generating function, $K = H$.
>
> *Source: Series II Props. 10.5, 10.7, 10.8 and Remark 10.9 · H&S §11.4, eqs. (11.90)–(11.91)*

^thm-b8-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Type 2.* First, the relations define a transformation. At fixed $q$ and $t$, the map $P \mapsto \partial F_2/\partial q\,(q, P, t)$ has Jacobian matrix $\bigl(\partial^2F_2/\partial q_i\,\partial P_j\bigr)$, invertible by hypothesis, so by the inverse function theorem ([[§16 The Inverse Function Theorem#^thm-16-2|452 Thm. §16.2]]) $p = \partial F_2/\partial q$ can be solved locally and smoothly for $P(q, p, t)$; then $Q = \partial F_2/\partial P$ gives $Q(q, p, t)$. Second, it is canonical. Take $F = F_2 - \sum_i P_iQ_i$ and differentiate along any curve, substituting the three relations:
>
> $$
> \frac{dF}{dt} = \sum_i\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i + \sum_i\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial P_i}\dot P_i + \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial t} - \sum_i\bigl(\dot P_iQ_i + P_i\dot Q_i\bigr) = \sum_i p_i\dot q_i + (K - H) - \sum_i P_i\dot Q_i .
> $$
>
> The $Q_i\dot P_i$ terms cancel, and this is the identity of [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]], which finishes the proof.
>
> *Types 1, 3, 4.* Solvability works the same way, with the invertible mixed matrix of each type. For the identity take $F = F_1$, $F = F_3 + \sum_i p_iq_i$ and $F = F_4 + \sum_i p_iq_i - \sum_i P_iQ_i$ respectively. For type 1, $dF_1/dt = \sum_i p_i\dot q_i - \sum_i P_i\dot Q_i + (K - H)$ directly from the relations. For types 3 and 4 the added products produce $q_i\dot p_i$ (and $Q_i\dot P_i$) terms that cancel those coming from $\partial F/\partial p_i = -q_i$ (and $\partial F/\partial P_i = Q_i$), leaving the same identity. Each sign in the table is the one that makes these cancellations work.

^der-b8-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]], [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^def-b8-2-2|Def. §B8.2.2]], [[§16 The Inverse Function Theorem#^thm-16-2|452 Thm. §16.2]], [[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why four types, and how they are related
> - Each type needs its two sets of arguments to be independent, and each fails for some transformation. The identity $Q = q$ has old and new coordinates dependent, so no $F_1$ generates it; $F_2 = \sum_i q_iP_i$ does. The exchange $Q = p$, $P = -q$ has $P$ determined by $q$, so no $F_2$ generates it; $F_1 = \sum_i q_iQ_i$ does ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^ex-b8-2-1|Example §B8.2.1]]).
> - Where two types exist for one transformation they are Legendre transforms of each other in the exchanged variables, the construction that took $L$ to $H$ ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-1|Def. §B7.1.1]]): $F_2 = F_1 + \sum_i Q_iP_i$, $F_3 = F_1 - \sum_i q_ip_i$, $F_4 = F_1 + \sum_i Q_iP_i - \sum_i q_ip_i$.
> - The four pure types do not exhaust the possibilities: a transformation may need, say, $(q_1, P_2)$ among its independent variables. Mixed choices, index by index, work by the same argument.
> - The four simplest generating functions: $F_1 = \sum q_iQ_i$ gives $p = Q$, $P = -q$; $F_2 = \sum q_iP_i$ the identity; $F_3 = \sum p_iQ_i$ gives $q = -Q$, $P = -p$; $F_4 = \sum p_iP_i$ gives $q = -P$, $Q = p$.
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 10.9 · H&S §11.4, eq. (11.92), Example 11.6*

^rem-b8-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.4: Infinitesimal Canonical Transformations Are Bracket Flows
> The generating function $F_2 = \sum_i q_iP_i + \epsilon\,G(q, P, t)$, with $\epsilon$ small, produces
>
> $$
> Q_i = q_i + \epsilon\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i} + O(\epsilon^2), \qquad P_i = p_i - \epsilon\frac{\partial G}{\partial q_i} + O(\epsilon^2),
> $$
>
> so every function changes by $\delta f = \epsilon\,\{f, G\}$ to first order: the flow generated by $G$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-2|Def. §B8.1.2]]) is canonical, step by step. With $G = H$ and $\epsilon = dt$, the step is the motion of the system during $dt$: **time evolution is a canonical transformation**, generated by the Hamiltonian. The generating function of the finite time step is the action ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-4|Theorem §B8.3.4]]).
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 10.6 · H&S §11.4 Example 11.7, §11.6 eq. (11.140)*

^thm-b8-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The type-2 relations give $p_i = P_i + \epsilon\,\partial G/\partial q_i$ and $Q_i = q_i + \epsilon\,\partial G/\partial P_i$. Since $P - p = O(\epsilon)$, replacing $P$ by $p$ inside the $\epsilon$ terms costs only $O(\epsilon^2)$, which gives the displayed form. Then $\delta q_i = \epsilon\,\partial G/\partial p_i = \epsilon\{q_i, G\}$ and $\delta p_i = -\epsilon\,\partial G/\partial q_i = \epsilon\{p_i, G\}$ ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]]), and by the chain rule $\delta f = \sum_i(\partial f/\partial q_i\,\delta q_i + \partial f/\partial p_i\,\delta p_i) = \epsilon\{f, G\}$. For $G = H$, $\epsilon = dt$, these are $\dot q_i\,dt$ and $\dot p_i\,dt$ by Hamilton's equations.

^der-b8-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]], [[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^def-b8-1-2|Def. §B8.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Canonical maps preserve phase-space area
> For one degree of freedom the Jacobian of $(q, p) \mapsto (Q, P)$ is
>
> $$
> \det\frac{\partial(Q, P)}{\partial(q, p)} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}\frac{\partial P}{\partial p} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial p}\frac{\partial P}{\partial q} = \{Q, P\} = 1,
> $$
>
> so by the change-of-variables formula ([[§25 Change of Variables on General Domains#^thm-25-3|452 Thm. §25.3]]) every region keeps its area. Helliwell & Sahakian reach the same conclusion from a generating function $F_1(q, Q)$: $\oint dF_1 = 0$ around a closed loop gives $\oint p\,dq = \oint P\,dQ$, and Green's theorem ([[§27 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-27-1|452 Thm. §27.1]]) turns both sides into the enclosed areas. Since time evolution is canonical ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-4|Theorem §B8.2.4]]), this is Liouville's theorem again, for one degree of freedom and by a different route ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]], which proves the $2n$-dimensional statement directly from Hamilton's equations). For $n$ degrees of freedom H&S state that the $2n$-dimensional volume is preserved (eq. (11.116)); their argument plane by plane applies as written only to transformations that do not mix the pairs $(q_a, p_a)$.
>
> *Source: H&S §11.5, eqs. (11.106)–(11.116)*

^rem-b8-2-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.2.1: Exchanging Coordinates and Momenta
> For one degree of freedom, $Q = p$, $P = -q$ is canonical. Directly: put $K(Q, P, t) = H(-P, Q, t)$, so $\partial K/\partial P = -\partial H/\partial q$ and $\partial K/\partial Q = \partial H/\partial p$. Along a solution of the old equations,
>
> $$
> \dot Q = \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q} = \frac{\partial K}{\partial P}, \qquad \dot P = -\dot q = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial p} = -\frac{\partial K}{\partial Q} .
> $$
>
> By brackets: $\{Q, P\} = \{p, -q\} = \{q, p\} = 1$. By a generating function: $F_1 = qQ$ gives $p = \partial F_1/\partial q = Q$ and $P = -\partial F_1/\partial Q = -q$, with $K = H$; the sign in $P = -q$ is the sign in the type-1 relation. For the oscillator $H = p^2/2m + \tfrac12m\omega^2q^2$, $K = Q^2/2m + \tfrac12m\omega^2P^2$: the same system with the roles of position and momentum exchanged. No point transformation can do this; in Hamiltonian mechanics coordinates and momenta are two halves of one state.
>
> *Source: Series II §10.1, eqs. (148)–(149), and §10.2, eq. (172) · H&S Example 11.5*

^ex-b8-2-1

> [!example] Example §B8.2.2: Point Transformations; Polar Coordinates
> A point transformation $Q_i = f_i(q, t)$ changes coordinates only. The generating function $F_2 = \sum_j f_j(q, t)\,P_j$ gives
>
> $$
> Q_i = f_i(q, t), \qquad p_i = \sum_j \frac{\partial f_j}{\partial q_i}P_j, \qquad K = H + \sum_j P_j\frac{\partial f_j}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> So every point transformation is canonical once the momenta transform by this rule, which is the transformation law of covector components, $\eta_j = \sum_i (\partial x^i/\partial y^j)\,\xi_i$ in [[§42 The Cotangent Bundle#^prop-42-2|591 Prop. §42.2]] (with $x = q$, $y = Q$, $\xi = p$, $\eta = P$): momenta live in the cotangent bundle ([[§42 The Cotangent Bundle#^def-42-1|591 Def. §42.1]]).
>
> *Polar coordinates.* For $(x, y) \mapsto (r, \varphi)$, take $F_2 = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}\,P_r + \varphi(x, y)\,P_\varphi$. Then $p_x = (x/r)P_r - (y/r^2)P_\varphi$ and $p_y = (y/r)P_r + (x/r^2)P_\varphi$, which invert to
>
> $$
> P_r = \frac{xp_x + yp_y}{r} = \hat r\cdot\vec p, \qquad P_\varphi = xp_y - yp_x = L_z .
> $$
>
> The momentum conjugate to the angle is the angular momentum, the generator of rotations ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-6|Theorem §B8.1.6]]). Since $p_x^2 + p_y^2 = P_r^2 + P_\varphi^2/r^2$, a particle in a central field has
>
> $$
> K = \frac{P_r^2}{2m} + \frac{P_\varphi^2}{2mr^2} + U(r),
> $$
>
> with $\varphi$ cyclic, so $P_\varphi = L_z$ is conserved ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-1|Theorem §B6.4.1]]), and the radial motion sees the effective potential ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]]).
>
> *A time-dependent case.* $F_2 = (q - vt)P$ gives $Q = q - vt$, $p = P$ and $K = H - vP$: the description from a frame moving at speed $v$. For a free particle $K = P^2/2m - vP$ and $\dot Q = \partial K/\partial P = P/m - v$, the relative velocity. Here $K \neq H$, and the difference is $\partial F_2/\partial t$.
>
> *Source: Series II §10.2 (point transformations from $F_2$) · H&S Example 11.8*

> [!example] Example §B8.2.3: The Oscillator as a Uniform Rotation
> For $H = p^2/2m + \tfrac12m\omega^2q^2$, try the type-1 generating function $F_1(q, Q) = \tfrac12 m\omega q^2\cot Q$. Its relations give
>
> $$
> p = \frac{\partial F_1}{\partial q} = m\omega q\cot Q, \qquad P = -\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial Q} = \frac{m\omega q^2}{2\sin^2 Q},
> $$
>
> which invert (on the branch $q\sin Q > 0$, continued smoothly) to
>
> $$
> q = \sqrt{\frac{2P}{m\omega}}\,\sin Q, \qquad p = \sqrt{2m\omega P}\,\cos Q .
> $$
>
> Substituting, $H = \omega P\cos^2Q + \omega P\sin^2Q = \omega P$. $F_1$ has no explicit time dependence, so $K = \omega P$: $Q$ is cyclic, and
>
> $$
> \dot P = -\frac{\partial K}{\partial Q} = 0, \qquad \dot Q = \frac{\partial K}{\partial P} = \omega \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P = \frac E\omega, \quad Q = \omega t + Q_0, \quad q(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m\omega^2}}\,\sin(\omega t + Q_0),
> $$
>
> the oscillation of [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|WO Theorem §A1.1.2]] with amplitude $A$ given by $E = \tfrac12m\omega^2A^2$ ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-1|WO Theorem §A1.2.1]]).
>
> Checks. The bracket test of [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]], done in the new variables (for one degree of freedom $\{q, p\}_{Q,P} = 1/\{Q, P\}_{q,p}$, since both are Jacobians of mutually inverse maps): $\{q, p\}_{Q,P} = \sqrt{2P/m\omega}\cos Q\cdot\sqrt{2m\omega}\cos Q/(2\sqrt P) + \sin Q\,\sqrt{2/m\omega}/(2\sqrt P)\cdot\sqrt{2m\omega P}\sin Q = \cos^2Q + \sin^2Q = 1$. Nondegeneracy: $\partial^2F_1/\partial q\,\partial Q = -m\omega q/\sin^2Q$, nonzero except at $q = 0$, where this chart degenerates.
>
> In the new variables the motion is a uniform drift of the phase angle $Q$ at constant $P$: the reference circle of level A ([[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^thm-a1-3-1|WO Theorem §A1.3.1]]), now as a canonical change of variables. The constant $P = E/\omega$ is the area of the orbit's ellipse divided by $2\pi$ (semi-axes $A$ and $m\omega A$), the **action variable**; making this construction systematic for any periodic motion gives action–angle variables (Classical Mechanics level C, planned). The generating function was a lucky guess; [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-1|Theorem §B8.3.1]] replaces the guess with an equation, and [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-2|Example §B8.3.2]] shows that this $F_1$ is, in form, the oscillator's own action.
>
> *Source: Series II §10.2, eqs. (173)–(178) · H&S Example 11.10 (same map, verified by brackets)*
