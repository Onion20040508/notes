A canonical transformation may change the Hamiltonian by the explicit time derivative of its generating function, $K = H + \partial F/\partial t$ ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]]). This section pushes that freedom to the limit, $K = 0$. Then every new variable is a constant of the motion, and the whole dynamics has been transformed away. The condition on the generating function is a single first-order partial differential equation, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, and Jacobi's theorem turns any sufficiently rich family of its solutions into all trajectories. For one degree of freedom the result is the energy quadrature of level A ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-3|Theorem §A4.7.3]]), seen from a new side. The solution that dynamics itself provides is the action of Hamilton's principle ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]]), evaluated along true paths; its differential is the integrand of the phase-space action of [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-3|Theorem §B7.1.3]]. Read as the phase of a wave, it shows classical mechanics as the short-wavelength limit of the Schrödinger equation.

## The equation and Jacobi's theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.3.1: The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation
> A type-2 generating function $S(q, P, t)$ ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^def-b8-2-2|Def. §B8.2.2]]) gives the new Hamiltonian $K \equiv 0$ if and only if it satisfies
>
> $$
> H\Bigl(q_1, \dots, q_n, \frac{\partial S}{\partial q_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial S}{\partial q_n}, t\Bigr) + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = 0,
> $$
>
> the **Hamilton–Jacobi equation**. In that case the new coordinates and momenta are all constants of the motion: $\dot Q_i = \partial K/\partial P_i = 0$, $\dot P_i = -\partial K/\partial Q_i = 0$. To write down the equation, take $H$ and replace each momentum $p_i$ by $\partial S/\partial q_i$.
>
> *Source: Series II §10.3, eqs. (179)–(181) · H&S §15.1, eqs. (15.1)–(15.4)*

^thm-b8-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]], $S$ generates a canonical transformation with $p_i = \partial S/\partial q_i$, $Q_i = \partial S/\partial P_i$ and $K = H(q, p, t) + \partial S/\partial t$. Eliminating $p$ with the first relation, $K = H(q, \partial S/\partial q, t) + \partial S/\partial t$, which vanishes identically exactly when the equation holds. With $K \equiv 0$, Hamilton's equations in the new variables make every $Q_i$ and $P_i$ constant.

^der-b8-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]], [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^def-b8-2-2|Def. §B8.2.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §B8.3.1: Complete Integral
> A **complete integral** of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation is a solution $S(q; \alpha, t)$ depending smoothly on $n$ parameters $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ such that, on its domain,
>
> $$
> \det\Bigl(\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial q_i\,\partial\alpha_j}\Bigr) \neq 0 .
> $$
>
> The parameters play the role of the constant new momenta, $P_i = \alpha_i$, and the condition is the nondegeneracy condition of a type-2 generating function. Since only derivatives of $S$ enter the equation, $S + c$ is a solution whenever $S$ is; an additive constant is always available and is not one of the $\alpha$'s. A parameter entering only additively makes a column of the matrix vanish, so the determinant condition excludes it automatically. Helliwell & Sahakian call a solution used this way **Hamilton's principal function**; the series reserves that name for the action function of [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-4|Theorem §B8.3.4]], which is one particular solution.
>
> *Source: Series II Def. 10.10 · H&S §15.1 (Example 15.1, counting of constants)*

^def-b8-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: One equation for 2n
> Hamilton's equations are $2n$ coupled ordinary differential equations; the Hamilton–Jacobi equation is one partial differential equation for one function of $n + 1$ variables, usually nonlinear (the momenta enter $H$ quadratically already for a free particle). The method never needs the general solution of that equation, only one $n$-parameter family with the determinant condition, found by any means, typically by a guess or by separating variables.
>
> *Source: Series II §10.3, Remark 10.13 · H&S §15.1*

^rem-b8-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.3.2: Jacobi's Theorem
> Let $H(q, p, t)$ be $C^1$ and $S(q; \alpha, t)$ a $C^2$ complete integral of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation. For constants $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ and $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n$, the relations
>
> $$
> \beta_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha_i}(q; \alpha, t), \qquad p_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i}(q; \alpha, t)
> $$
>
> determine, locally in $t$, a curve $(q(t), p(t))$ (the first set implicitly, the second by evaluation), and this curve solves Hamilton's equations. Conversely, every solution of Hamilton's equations that passes through the domain arises this way for suitable constants $(\alpha, \beta)$. So the $2n$ integration constants of the motion are the $n$ parameters $\alpha$ and the $n$ values $\beta$.
>
> *Source: Series II Thm. 10.11 · H&S §15.1, eqs. (15.2)–(15.4)*

^thm-b8-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Through the canonical machinery.* By [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-1|Theorem §B8.3.1]], $S$ generates a canonical transformation $(q, p) \mapsto (Q, P) = (\beta, \alpha)$ with $K = 0$, in which nothing moves. A trajectory is a choice of the $2n$ constants, and transforming back by solving the two relations for $(q, p)$ gives a solution of the old equations ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-2|Theorem §B8.2.2]], read in reverse).
>
> *Directly.* For fixed $(\alpha, \beta)$ the map $q \mapsto \partial S/\partial\alpha$ has Jacobian $\bigl(\partial^2S/\partial\alpha_i\,\partial q_j\bigr)$, invertible by the determinant condition, so $\beta = \partial S/\partial\alpha$ has a unique smooth local solution $q(t)$ ([[§13 The Inverse Function Theorem#^thm-13-2|452 Thm. §13.2]], with smooth dependence on $t$). Set $p_i(t) = \partial S/\partial q_i$ along it. Differentiate $\beta_i = \partial S/\partial\alpha_i(q(t); \alpha, t)$ in $t$, and separately differentiate the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, an identity in $(q, \alpha, t)$, with respect to $\alpha_i$; $H$ depends on $\alpha_i$ only through its momentum slots:
>
> $$
> 0 = \frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial t\,\partial\alpha_i} + \sum_j\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial q_j\,\partial\alpha_i}\,\dot q_j, \qquad 0 = \frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial\alpha_i\,\partial t} + \sum_j\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_j}\,\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial\alpha_i\,\partial q_j} .
> $$
>
> Subtracting, with mixed partials equal ([[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]), $\sum_j\bigl(\partial^2S/\partial\alpha_i\,\partial q_j\bigr)\bigl(\dot q_j - \partial H/\partial p_j\bigr) = 0$ for every $i$. The matrix is invertible, so $\dot q_j = \partial H/\partial p_j$. Next, $\dot p_i = \partial^2S/\partial t\,\partial q_i + \sum_j \bigl(\partial^2S/\partial q_j\,\partial q_i\bigr)\dot q_j$, while differentiating the Hamilton–Jacobi equation in $q_i$ (now $H$ depends on $q_i$ directly and through every momentum slot) gives
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial q_i\,\partial t} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} - \sum_j\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_j}\,\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial q_i\,\partial q_j} .
> $$
>
> Inserting this and $\dot q_j = \partial H/\partial p_j$, the two sums cancel and $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q_i$.
>
> *Converse.* Let a solution pass through $(q^0, p^0)$ at $t_0$. The equations $p^0_i = \partial S/\partial q_i(q^0; \alpha, t_0)$ have Jacobian in $\alpha$ equal to the transpose of the matrix in [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^def-b8-3-1|Def. §B8.3.1]], so they determine $\alpha$ locally; put $\beta_i = \partial S/\partial\alpha_i(q^0; \alpha, t_0)$. The curve built from this $(\alpha, \beta)$ solves Hamilton's equations and passes through the same point at the same time, so by uniqueness of solutions of the initial-value problem (stated; Mathematical Methods, planned) it is the given solution. The direct route shows where each hypothesis works: $C^2$ in the two exchanges of mixed partials, and the determinant condition three times.

## Time-independent Hamiltonians and separation of variables

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.3.3: Separating the Time; the Energy Quadrature
> Let $H(q, p)$ have no explicit time dependence. Then $S = W(q; \alpha) - Et$ solves the Hamilton–Jacobi equation if and only if $W$ solves the **time-independent (reduced) Hamilton–Jacobi equation**
>
> $$
> H\Bigl(q, \frac{\partial W}{\partial q}\Bigr) = E .
> $$
>
> $W$ is **Hamilton's characteristic function**, and the separation constant $E$, the conserved value of $H$, can serve as one of the parameters, $\alpha_1 = E$. Jacobi's theorem then reads
>
> $$
> t + \beta_1 = \frac{\partial W}{\partial E}, \qquad \beta_i = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_i}\quad(i \geq 2), \qquad p_i = \frac{\partial W}{\partial q_i}:
> $$
>
> the first equation gives the timing, the others the shape of the orbit, with no time in them. For one degree of freedom with $H = p^2/2m + U(q)$, on a stretch of motion toward $+q$,
>
> $$
> W(q; E) = \int^q \sqrt{2m\bigl(E - U(q')\bigr)}\,dq', \qquad t + \beta = \int^q \frac{dq'}{\sqrt{2\bigl(E - U(q')\bigr)/m}} ,
> $$
>
> which is the time-from-energy formula of [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-3|Theorem §A4.7.3]].
>
> *Source: Series II §10.5, eqs. (194)–(195) · H&S §15.2, eqs. (15.22)–(15.24)*

^thm-b8-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $S = W(q) - Et$, $\partial S/\partial t = -E$ and $\partial S/\partial q = \partial W/\partial q$, so the Hamilton–Jacobi equation becomes $H(q, \partial W/\partial q) - E = 0$; conversely a solution of the reduced equation gives one of the full equation. In Jacobi's relations, $\partial S/\partial E = \partial W/\partial E - t$ and $\partial S/\partial\alpha_i = \partial W/\partial\alpha_i$ for the other parameters, which gives the displayed forms. For one degree of freedom, $(W')^2/2m + U = E$ gives $W' = \sqrt{2m(E - U)}$ on the branch $p > 0$. Differentiating under the integral sign,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial W}{\partial E} = \int^q \frac{m\,dq'}{\sqrt{2m(E - U)}} = \int^q \frac{dq'}{\sqrt{2(E - U)/m}} ,
> $$
>
> and the lower limit, like $\beta$, only fixes the origin of time.

^der-b8-3-3

*Uses:* [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-1|Theorem §B8.3.1]], [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The level-A quadrature, one level up
> Level A obtained $t(x)$ by solving the energy equation for the speed and separating variables ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-3|Theorem §A4.7.3]]). Here the same integral is $\partial W/\partial E$: the constant conjugate to the energy is the time origin, $\beta = -t_0$. The method also says where it breaks down. The determinant condition is $\partial^2W/\partial q\,\partial E = m/p = 1/\dot q \neq 0$, which fails at the turning points ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^def-a4-7-1|Def. §A4.7.1]]). There the branch $p > 0$ must be exchanged for $p < 0$; [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-2|Example §B8.3.2]] shows that inverting the quadrature does this automatically.

^rem-b8-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Separation of variables, and what it means
> If in some coordinates the solution splits as $W = W_1(q_1; \alpha) + \dots + W_n(q_n; \alpha)$, each piece follows by a single integration, and $p_i = \partial W_i/\partial q_i$ draws the orbit's curve in each $(q_i, p_i)$ plane. The $n$ parameters $\alpha_i$ are then $n$ constants of the motion. As new canonical momenta they have zero mutual brackets ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-1|Theorem §B8.1.1]]): they are **in involution**, the mark of an integrable system. Integrable systems are rare, and even for them the separating coordinates may be hard to find. So the Hamilton–Jacobi equation is seldom the fastest route to a trajectory. Its value is structural.
>
> *Central field.* For $H = p_r^2/2m + p_\varphi^2/2mr^2 + U(r)$ in plane polar coordinates $(r, \varphi)$ (Helliwell & Sahakian take $U = \tfrac12kr^2$ and call the angle $\theta$), $W = p_\varphi\varphi + W_r(r)$ separates with the angular momentum $p_\varphi$ as separation constant, and
>
> $$
> W_r = \int^r\sqrt{2m\bigl(E - U(r')\bigr) - \frac{p_\varphi^2}{r'^2}}\;dr' .
> $$
>
> The radial momentum sees the effective potential $U + p_\varphi^2/2mr^2$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]], whose angular momentum is $p_\varphi$ here). Jacobi's relations give the orbit, $\beta_\varphi = \partial W/\partial p_\varphi = \varphi - \int^r p_\varphi\,dr'/\bigl(r'^2\sqrt{2m(E - U) - p_\varphi^2/r'^2}\bigr)$, and the timing, $t + \beta_E = \partial W/\partial E$: the two quadratures of [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-6|Theorem §B2.1.6]], now as Jacobi's theorem. In the language of H&S eq. (15.34), with new momenta $(E, p_\varphi)$ the conjugate coordinates are $\partial W/\partial E = t + t_1$, growing at unit rate, and $\partial W/\partial p_\varphi = $ constant; the two frequencies written there are $1$ and $0$.
>
> *Source: H&S §15.1, eqs. (15.20)–(15.21), and Example 15.2 · Series II Remark 10.15*

## The action as principal function

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.3.4: The Action Solves the Hamilton–Jacobi Equation
> Fix an initial event $(q^0, t_0)$ and suppose that for every endpoint $(q, t)$ in some region there is exactly one true trajectory from $(q^0, t_0)$ to $(q, t)$, depending smoothly on the endpoint. Define
>
> $$
> S(q, t;\ q^0, t_0) = \int_{t_0}^{t} L\bigl(\bar q(\tau), \dot{\bar q}(\tau), \tau\bigr)\,d\tau \qquad\text{along that trajectory } \bar q .
> $$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i} = p_i, \qquad \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = -H\Bigl(q, \frac{\partial S}{\partial q}, t\Bigr), \qquad \frac{\partial S}{\partial q^0_i} = -p^0_i ,
> $$
>
> with $p$ and $p^0$ the momenta of the trajectory at its end and at its start. So $dS = \sum_i p_i\,dq_i - H\,dt$, the integrand of the phase-space action ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^thm-b7-1-3|Theorem §B7.1.3]]) now read as a differential of the end point, and $S$ solves the Hamilton–Jacobi equation. Read as a function of the old coordinates $q$ and the "new coordinates" $q^0$, $S$ is a type-1 generating function ([[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]]) of the map $(q, p) \mapsto (q^0, p^0)$ that carries the present state back to its initial data, with $K = 0$. Time evolution is a canonical transformation, and its generating function is the action.
>
> *Source: Series II §10.6, eqs. (204)–(210) · H&S §15.1, eqs. (15.17)–(15.18)*

^thm-b8-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Moving the end point in space.* Change $q$ to $q + \delta q$ at fixed $t$. The true path changes by $\delta\bar q(\tau)$, with $\delta\bar q(t_0) = 0$ and $\delta\bar q(t) = \delta q$. Vary the integral as in the first variation formula ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]]), but keep the boundary term:
>
> $$
> \delta S = \int_{t_0}^{t}\sum_i\Bigl[\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} - \frac{d}{d\tau}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\Bigr]\delta\bar q_i\,d\tau + \Bigl[\sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\,\delta\bar q_i\Bigr]_{t_0}^{t} = \sum_i p_i\,\delta q_i .
> $$
>
> The integral vanishes because $\bar q$ obeys Lagrange's equations ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]), and the boundary term survives only at the upper end, where $\partial L/\partial\dot q_i = p_i$. In Hamilton's principle ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|Principle §B6.2.1]]) the ends were fixed and the path varied, so the integral gave the equations of motion; here the path is always a true one and the end moves, so the boundary term is the whole answer.
>
> *Moving the end point in time.* Extending the trajectory from $t$ to $t + dt$ adds $L\,dt$ to the integral, so $dS/dt = L$ along the trajectory. By the chain rule the left side is $\partial S/\partial t + \sum_i(\partial S/\partial q_i)\dot q_i = \partial S/\partial t + \sum_i p_i\dot q_i$. Hence $\partial S/\partial t = L - \sum_i p_i\dot q_i = -H$, the Legendre transform of [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]], with $p = \partial S/\partial q$.
>
> *Moving the starting point.* Varying $q^0$ at fixed $(q, t)$, the same computation leaves the boundary term only at the lower limit, with a minus sign: $\partial S/\partial q^0_i = -p^0_i$. The relations $p = \partial S/\partial q$, $p^0 = -\partial S/\partial q^0$ are the type-1 relations with $(Q, P) = (q^0, p^0)$, and $K = H + \partial S/\partial t = 0$, as it must be for the constants $q^0, p^0$.

^der-b8-3-4

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]], [[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]], [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: The hypothesis can fail
> The initial-value problem always has one solution, but the boundary-value problem "from $(q^0, t_0)$ to $(q, t)$" can have none or many. Where trajectories from one starting point refocus, at **conjugate points** (in optics, caustics), $S$ ceases to be a function of the endpoint and the type-1 reading fails, since $\det\bigl(\partial^2S/\partial q\,\partial q^0\bigr)$ must be nonzero. The harmonic oscillator refocuses after half a period ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-2|Example §B8.3.2]]). The systematic theory belongs to the second variation in the calculus of variations, not covered here.
>
> *Source: Series II §10.6 (footnote 1 and the discussion of Fig. 4)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.3.1: The Free Particle, Two Ways
> $H = p^2/2m$, so the Hamilton–Jacobi equation is $\frac{1}{2m}(\partial S/\partial q)^2 + \partial S/\partial t = 0$.
>
> *A complete integral.* Try $S = \alpha q - f(t)$: then $f'(t) = \alpha^2/2m$, and $S = \alpha q - \alpha^2t/2m$, with $\partial^2S/\partial q\,\partial\alpha = 1 \neq 0$. Jacobi's theorem gives $\beta = \partial S/\partial\alpha = q - \alpha t/m$ and $p = \partial S/\partial q = \alpha$: uniform motion $q = \beta + (\alpha/m)\,t$, with the constants already labelled as the momentum and the initial position.
>
> *The action.* The true path from $(q^0, t_0)$ to $(q, t)$ has constant velocity $(q - q^0)/(t - t_0)$, so
>
> $$
> S = \int_{t_0}^{t}\tfrac12m\dot{\bar q}^2\,d\tau = \frac{m(q - q^0)^2}{2(t - t_0)} .
> $$
>
> Check: $\partial S/\partial q = m(q - q^0)/(t - t_0) = p$ and $\partial S/\partial t = -m(q - q^0)^2/2(t - t_0)^2 = -\frac{1}{2m}(\partial S/\partial q)^2$. The cross-derivative $\partial^2S/\partial q\,\partial q^0 = -m/(t - t_0)$ never vanishes for $t > t_0$: free paths never refocus.
>
> *Source: Series II §10.5, eqs. (192)–(193), and §10.6, eqs. (211)–(212)*

^ex-b8-3-1

> [!example] Example §B8.3.2: The Harmonic Oscillator, Two Ways
> $H = p^2/2m + \tfrac12m\omega^2q^2$.
>
> *By separation.* The reduced equation $\frac{1}{2m}(W')^2 + \tfrac12m\omega^2q^2 = E$ gives $W' = \pm\sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2q^2}$, real for $|q| \leq A = \sqrt{2E/m\omega^2}$, the turning points. On the $+$ branch, [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-3|Theorem §B8.3.3]] needs only $\partial W/\partial E$, which is easier than $W$:
>
> $$
> t + \beta = \int^q\frac{m\,dq'}{\sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2q'^2}} = \frac1\omega\int^q\frac{dq'}{\sqrt{A^2 - q'^2}} = \frac1\omega\arcsin\frac qA ,
> $$
>
> so $q(t) = A\sin\omega(t + \beta)$. The arcsine covers one sweep from $-A$ to $A$, but the sine continues through the turning points and back, producing the $-$ branch on the return sweep without being asked: $p = m\dot q = m\omega A\cos\omega(t + \beta)$ agrees with $W' = m\omega\sqrt{A^2 - q^2}$ in magnitude, and the sign of the cosine picks the branch. The determinant condition, $\partial^2S/\partial q\,\partial E = m/\sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2q^2} = 1/\dot q$, holds for $|q| < A$ and fails exactly at the turning points. The energy is $E = \tfrac12m\omega^2A^2$, as in [[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-1|WO Theorem §A1.2.1]].
>
> *The action.* With $T = t - t_0$ and $\sin\omega T \neq 0$, the true path from $q^0$ to $q$ is $\bar q(\tau) = [q^0\sin\omega(t - \tau) + q\sin\omega(\tau - t_0)]/\sin\omega T$. In $S = \int\bigl(\tfrac12m\dot{\bar q}^2 - \tfrac12m\omega^2\bar q^2\bigr)d\tau$, integrate the kinetic term by parts; the remaining integrand is $-\tfrac12m\,\bar q\,(\ddot{\bar q} + \omega^2\bar q) = 0$, which leaves only the boundary terms, $S = \tfrac12m\bigl[q\,\dot{\bar q}(t) - q^0\,\dot{\bar q}(t_0)\bigr]$, and evaluating the velocities at the two ends gives
>
> $$
> S(q, t;\ q^0, t_0) = \frac{m\omega}{2\sin\omega T}\Bigl[\bigl(q^2 + (q^0)^2\bigr)\cos\omega T - 2qq^0\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Checks: $\partial S/\partial q = m\omega(q\cos\omega T - q^0)/\sin\omega T = m\dot{\bar q}(t)$, and as $\omega \to 0$ the formula tends to the free-particle action $m(q - q^0)^2/2T$. For $q^0 = 0$ it reduces to $\tfrac12m\omega q^2\cot\omega T$: the generating function guessed in [[§B8.2 Canonical Transformations and Generating Functions#^ex-b8-2-3|Example §B8.2.3]], evaluated at $Q = \omega T$, which is exactly the phase angle reached at time $t$ by an orbit that left $q = 0$ at $t_0$. The guess had the form of the oscillator's own action.
>
> The cross-derivative $\partial^2S/\partial q\,\partial q^0 = -m\omega/\sin\omega T$ blows up as $\omega T \to \pi$. There every path leaving $q^0 = 0$ returns to $q = 0$ whatever its amplitude, because the period does not depend on the amplitude. The endpoint $(0, t_0 + \pi/\omega)$ is reached by all of them and every other endpoint at that time by none: a conjugate point ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^cau-b8-3-1|Caution in §B8.3]]).
>
> *Source: Series II §10.5, eqs. (196)–(203), and §10.6, eqs. (213)–(217) · H&S Example 15.1*

^ex-b8-3-2

> [!example] Example §B8.3.3: A Beam of Projectiles
> A particle moves in a vertical plane in uniform gravity, $H = (p_x^2 + p_y^2)/2m + mgy$. Find a complete integral, the trajectories, and the surfaces $W = $ const.
>
> *Separation.* Put $W = \alpha x + Y(y)$: then $\alpha^2/2m + (Y')^2/2m + mgy = E$, so $Y' = \pm\sqrt{2m(E - mgy) - \alpha^2} = \pm m\sqrt{2g(h - y)}$ with the apex height $h = (E - \alpha^2/2m)/mg$. On the descending branch,
>
> $$
> W(x, y;\ \alpha, E) = \alpha x + \tfrac23\,m\sqrt{2g}\,(h - y)^{3/2} .
> $$
>
> *Jacobi's relations.* With parameters $(\alpha, E)$, using $\partial h/\partial\alpha = -\alpha/m^2g$ and $\partial h/\partial E = 1/mg$,
>
> $$
> \beta_\alpha = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha} = x - \frac\alpha m\sqrt{\frac{2(h - y)}{g}}, \qquad t + \beta_E = \frac{\partial W}{\partial E} = \sqrt{\frac{2(h - y)}{g}} .
> $$
>
> The first gives the orbit $y = h - g(x - x_0)^2/2v_x^2$ with $v_x = \alpha/m$ and $x_0 = \beta_\alpha$, a parabola with apex at $(x_0, h)$; the second gives the timing $h - y = \tfrac12g(t - t_0)^2$ with $t_0 = -\beta_E$. For the vertical motion alone ($\alpha = 0$) this is the falling ball of H&S Problem 15.1.
>
> *Wavefronts.* Fix $\alpha$ and $E$ and let $x_0$ vary: a beam of identical projectiles launched side by side. Every member has $\vec p = \nabla W$, and since $\vec p = m\vec v$, each trajectory crosses the surfaces $W = $ const at right angles, like rays crossing wavefronts. The reduced equation reads $|\nabla W|^2 = 2m(E - U)$. This is the eikonal equation of geometrical optics, $|\nabla\Sigma|^2 = n^2$ for the optical path length $\Sigma$ (H&S write $L$), with $\sqrt{2m(E - U)}$ in place of the refractive index. Along the beam $p_x = |\vec p|\sin\theta = \alpha$ is constant, $\theta$ being the angle from the vertical. That is Snell's law $n\sin\theta = $ const ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|WO Law §A4.2.4]]) for the "medium" $|\vec p|(y)$, exactly as Helliwell & Sahakian derive it for light in a layered atmosphere, and as Fermat's principle gives it in [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^ex-b5-1-2|Example §B5.1.2]].
>
> *Source: H&S Problem 15.1 (vertical fall; solved here and extended to two dimensions), §15.2 eq. (15.36), Example 15.5 (the optical counterpart)*

## Toward wave mechanics

> [!remark] Remark: The classical limit of the Schrödinger equation
> This is a bridge to quantum mechanics, not a result of classical mechanics. For a particle of mass $m$ with potential energy $U(\vec r, t)$, write the wave function as $\psi = \exp\bigl(A + iS/\hbar\bigr)$ with $A(\vec r, t)$ and $S(\vec r, t)$ real. The Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial\psi/\partial t = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + U\psi$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|QM Principle §A4.1.1]], in three dimensions) is equivalent to the pair
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} + \frac{(\nabla S)^2}{2m} + U = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Bigl(\nabla^2A + (\nabla A)^2\Bigr), \qquad \frac{\partial A}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2m}\Bigl(\nabla^2S + 2\nabla A\cdot\nabla S\Bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Why.* With $\Phi = A + iS/\hbar$, $\psi = e^\Phi$ gives $\partial_t\psi = \Phi_t\,\psi$ and $\nabla^2\psi = \bigl(\nabla^2\Phi + (\nabla\Phi)^2\bigr)\psi$, where $(\nabla\Phi)^2 = (\nabla A)^2 - (\nabla S)^2/\hbar^2 + (2i/\hbar)\nabla A\cdot\nabla S$ and $\nabla^2\Phi = \nabla^2A + (i/\hbar)\nabla^2S$. Dividing the Schrödinger equation by $\psi \neq 0$,
>
> $$
> i\hbar A_t - S_t = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\bigl(\nabla^2A + (\nabla A)^2\bigr) + \frac{(\nabla S)^2}{2m} - \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\bigl(\nabla^2S + 2\nabla A\cdot\nabla S\bigr) + U ,
> $$
>
> and since $A$, $S$, $U$ are real, the real parts give the first equation and the imaginary parts, divided by $\hbar$, the second; conversely the two real equations give back the complex one.
>
> *Reading.* When the right side of the first equation is negligible, the phase $S$ obeys the Hamilton–Jacobi equation ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-1|Theorem §B8.3.1]]). With $\rho = |\psi|^2 = e^{2A}$, $\partial_t\rho = 2A_t\,\rho$ and $\nabla\cdot(\rho\nabla S) = \rho\bigl(\nabla^2S + 2\nabla A\cdot\nabla S\bigr)$, so the second equation is the continuity equation $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot(\rho\,\nabla S/m) = 0$: the probability density is carried along with the velocity $\nabla S/m = \vec p/m$ ([[§B1.1 The Statistical Interpretation and Probability Current#^thm-b1-1-2|QM Theorem §B1.1.2]]). The neglect is justified when the amplitude varies little over a de Broglie wavelength $h/|\nabla S|$, so that $\hbar^2|\nabla^2A + (\nabla A)^2| \ll |\nabla S|^2$; this is a formal limit, made systematic by the WKB approximation ([[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-1|QM Theorem §B9.3.1]], [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^rem-b9-3-1|QM Remark: The expansion in powers of ħ, and the classical action]]). Classical mechanics is to wave mechanics as ray optics is to wave optics.
>
> - **Schrödinger's direction.** Helliwell & Sahakian retrace how Schrödinger went the other way: the time-independent Hamilton–Jacobi equation $(\nabla W)^2 = 2m(E - U)$ looks like the eikonal equation $(\nabla\Sigma)^2 = n^2$ of ray optics ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-3|Example §B8.3.3]]), which is the short-wavelength limit of a wave equation. Asking which wave equation has $(\nabla W)^2 = 2m(E - U)$ as its short-wavelength limit, with $\psi \propto e^{i(W - Et)/\hbar}$, leads to the Schrödinger equation.
> - **Matching pieces.** Separating the time, $S = W - Et$ ([[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-3|Theorem §B8.3.3]]), is the classical shadow of the stationary-state factor $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-4|QM Theorem §A4.1.4]]). For a free particle, $e^{iS/\hbar}$ with the complete integral $S = pq - p^2t/2m$ of [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-1|Example §B8.3.1]] is exactly the plane wave $e^{i(pq - Et)/\hbar}$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-3|QM Theorem §A4.1.3]]), and there the right side of the first equation above vanishes identically.
> - **The action as a phase.** In Feynman's path integral each path carries the phase $e^{iS/\hbar}$, and the classical path is the one of stationary phase; the two-point action of [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^thm-b8-3-4|Theorem §B8.3.4]] is the phase of the quantum propagator. Path integrals: Quantum Mechanics level C — [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-2|QM Theorem §C4.2.2]], [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-6|QM Theorem §C4.2.6]], [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-7|QM Theorem §C4.2.7]]; the free and oscillator propagators, whose phases are the actions of Examples §B8.3.1–§B8.3.2, [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-4|QM Theorem §C4.1.4]], [[§C4.1 Propagators#^thm-c4-1-5|QM Theorem §C4.1.5]]. WKB: [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^thm-b9-3-1|QM Theorem §B9.3.1]], [[§B9.3 The WKB Approximation and Bohr–Sommerfeld Quantization#^rem-b9-3-1|QM Remark: The expansion in powers of ħ, and the classical action]].
>
> *Source: H&S §15.6–15.7, eqs. (15.134)–(15.138) (time-independent form there) · Series II Remark 10.14 (one dimension, $\psi = e^{iS/\hbar}$)*

^rem-b8-3-4

> [!remark] Remark: How far the method reaches
> Jacobi's theorem is local. A complete integral with a global, single-valued $W$ exists only for integrable systems, with $n$ independent constants of the motion in involution (the Liouville–Arnold theorem, stated in the series without proof). The double pendulum at large amplitude is chaotic, and no choice of coordinates separates it. What holds for every system is the local construction and the identity $dS = \sum_i p_i\,dq_i - H\,dt$. For bounded periodic motion, using the action variable $\oint p\,dq/2\pi$ as the new momentum gives action–angle variables, the setting for adiabatic invariants and perturbation theory; these are Classical Mechanics level C (planned).
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 10.15, §11 · H&S §15.1*
