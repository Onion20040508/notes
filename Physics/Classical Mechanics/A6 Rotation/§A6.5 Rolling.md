A wheel on a road both translates and rotates, and its axis moves. When it rolls without slipping the two motions are locked together: the speed of the centre fixes the rate of turning. This section uses that constraint together with $\sum\vec F = m\vec a_{\text{cm}}$ ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]) and $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ ([[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]]), or with energy conservation, to find how round bodies roll, and why static friction makes them roll but takes no energy.

## Rolling without slipping

> [!definition] Definition §A6.5.1: Rolling without Slipping
> A round body of radius $R$ **rolls without slipping** on a surface when the point of the body in contact with the surface is momentarily at rest relative to the surface. Otherwise it **slips** (skids), and the contact point slides over the surface.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.1*

^def-a6-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.5.1: Rolling Constraint
> For a body of radius $R$ rolling without slipping on a fixed surface, the centre of mass (at the centre) and the rotation are linked by
>
> $$
> v_{\text{cm}} = R\omega, \qquad a_{\text{cm}} = R\alpha, \qquad d_{\text{cm}} = R\theta ,
> $$
>
> where $d_{\text{cm}}$ is the distance moved by the centre while the body turns through $\theta$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.1, eqs. (11.1)–(11.3)*

^thm-a6-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let the body move along $+x$ while it turns clockwise with angular speed $\omega$. Relative to the centre, the contact point $P$ (at the bottom) moves on a circle of radius $R$ with velocity $-R\omega\,\hat\imath$ ([[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^thm-a6-1-2|Theorem §A6.1.2]]). Its velocity relative to the surface is that plus the velocity of the centre ([[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|Theorem §A2.8.1]]):
>
> $$
> \vec v_P = v_{\text{cm}}\,\hat\imath - R\omega\,\hat\imath .
> $$
>
> Rolling without slipping means $\vec v_P = \vec 0$, so $v_{\text{cm}} = R\omega$. Differentiating gives $a_{\text{cm}} = R\alpha$, and integrating gives $d_{\text{cm}} = R\theta$: the arc $R\theta$ of the rim that has touched the ground is laid out along it.

^der-a6-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A6.5 Rolling#^def-a6-5-1|Def. §A6.5.1]], [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^thm-a6-1-2|Theorem §A6.1.2]], [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|Theorem §A2.8.1]] (addition of velocities)

> [!remark] Remark: How the points of a rolling wheel move
> Every point has velocity $\vec v_{\text{cm}}$ plus its velocity of rotation about the centre. At the top the two add, $v_{\text{top}} = v_{\text{cm}} + R\omega = 2v_{\text{cm}}$; at the bottom they cancel. At each instant the wheel is turning about its contact point, which is why photographs of rolling wheels show a sharp bottom and a blurred top.

^rem-a6-5-1

![[ph-cm-a6-5-2.svg]]

## Kinetic energy of a rolling body

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.5.2: Kinetic Energy of a Rolling Body
> The kinetic energy of a rigid body whose centre of mass moves with $\vec v_{\text{cm}}$ while it turns with $\omega$ about an axis through the centre of mass is
>
> $$
> K = \tfrac12 Mv_{\text{cm}}^2 + \tfrac12 I_{\text{cm}}\omega^2 .
> $$
>
> For rolling without slipping, $K = \tfrac12\bigl(M + I_{\text{cm}}/R^2\bigr)v_{\text{cm}}^2$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.1*

^thm-a6-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write the velocity of each mass element as $\vec v_j = \vec v_{\text{cm}} + \vec u_j$, with $\vec u_j$ its velocity relative to the centre of mass; relative to the centre of mass the body just turns, so $u_j = \rho_j\omega$ with $\rho_j$ the distance from the axis through the centre of mass. Then
>
> $$
> K = \sum_j\tfrac12 m_j\,\lvert\vec v_{\text{cm}} + \vec u_j\rvert^2 = \tfrac12 Mv_{\text{cm}}^2 + \vec v_{\text{cm}}\cdot\sum_j m_j\vec u_j + \tfrac12\Bigl(\sum_j m_j\rho_j^2\Bigr)\omega^2 .
> $$
>
> The middle sum is the total momentum relative to the centre of mass, $\sum_j m_j(\vec v_j - \vec v_{\text{cm}}) = M\vec v_{\text{cm}} - M\vec v_{\text{cm}} = \vec 0$ ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]]), and the last bracket is $I_{\text{cm}}$. With $\omega = v_{\text{cm}}/R$ ([[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-1|Theorem §A6.5.1]]) the second form follows.

^der-a6-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]], [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]] (centre of mass), [[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-1|Theorem §A6.5.1]], [[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-1|Definition 6.1]] (dot product)

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.5.3: Energy Conservation in Rolling without Slipping
> For a body rolling without slipping, the static friction force at the contact point does no work. If no other non-conservative force does work, the mechanical energy is conserved:
>
> $$
> \tfrac12 Mv_{\text{cm}}^2 + \tfrac12 I_{\text{cm}}\omega^2 + Mgh_{\text{cm}} = \text{constant}.
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.1*

^thm-a6-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A force does work at the rate $\vec F\cdot\vec v$, where $\vec v$ is the velocity of the material point it acts on. Static friction acts at the contact point, and in rolling without slipping that point has $\vec v_P = \vec 0$ (Definition §A6.5.1), so its power and its work are zero. The normal force acts at the same point and does no work either. Only gravity does work, and conservation of mechanical energy ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]) with the kinetic energy of [[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-2|Theorem §A6.5.2]] gives the result.

^der-a6-5-3

## Rolling down an incline

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.5.4: Rolling down an Incline
> A round body of mass $M$, radius $R$ and moment of inertia $I_{\text{cm}}$ about its axis rolls without slipping down an incline of angle $\theta$. Then
>
> $$
> a_{\text{cm}} = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + I_{\text{cm}}/(MR^2)}, \qquad f_s = \frac{Mg\sin\theta}{1 + MR^2/I_{\text{cm}}}\ \ (\text{up the incline}),
> $$
>
> and it can do so only if $\mu_s \geq \dfrac{\tan\theta}{1 + MR^2/I_{\text{cm}}}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.1, Example 11.1, eq. (11.4)*

^thm-a6-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take $x$ down the incline and $y$ perpendicular to it. The forces are the weight, the normal force $N$ and static friction $f_s$ up the incline at the contact point. Newton's second law for the centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]):
>
> $$
> Mg\sin\theta - f_s = Ma_{\text{cm}}, \qquad N - Mg\cos\theta = 0 .
> $$
>
> About the axis through the centre of mass, the weight and $N$ have no torque (the weight acts at the centre, $N$ points through it); friction has lever arm $R$, so $f_sR = I_{\text{cm}}\alpha$. (UP1 applies $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ about this moving axis without comment; it holds for an axis through the centre of mass, as is shown at level B.) With $\alpha = a_{\text{cm}}/R$ ([[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-1|Theorem §A6.5.1]]), $f_s = I_{\text{cm}}a_{\text{cm}}/R^2$. Substituting into the first equation, $Mg\sin\theta = (M + I_{\text{cm}}/R^2)a_{\text{cm}}$, which gives $a_{\text{cm}}$ and then $f_s$. Static friction can supply at most $\mu_sN = \mu_sMg\cos\theta$ ([[§A3.8 Friction#^law-a3-8-1|Law §A3.8.1]]); requiring $f_s \leq \mu_sMg\cos\theta$ gives the condition on $\mu_s$.

^der-a6-5-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]], [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]], [[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-1|Theorem §A6.5.1]], [[§A3.8 Friction#^law-a3-8-1|Law §A3.8.1]] (static friction)

![[ph-cm-a6-5-1.svg]]
*Free-body diagram of a body rolling down an incline. Weight and normal force act along lines through the centre and contact point; only static friction (red), up the slope at the contact point, has a torque about the centre, and it is what makes the body turn.*

> [!remark] Remark: The race down the slope
> Since $a_{\text{cm}} = g\sin\theta/(1 + I_{\text{cm}}/MR^2)$ depends only on the shape factor $I_{\text{cm}}/MR^2$, not on mass or size, round bodies released together reach the bottom in the order solid sphere ($\tfrac25$, $a = \tfrac57 g\sin\theta$), solid cylinder ($\tfrac12$, $a = \tfrac23 g\sin\theta$), hoop or thin-walled cylinder ($1$, $a = \tfrac12 g\sin\theta$). All are slower than a block sliding without friction ($a = g\sin\theta$): part of the released energy goes into rotation.

^rem-a6-5-2

> [!caution] Caution: Friction in rolling
> - Static friction in rolling is whatever the equations of motion require, up to $\mu_sN$; it is not $\mu_sN$. (UP1, p. 553, writes $(a_{\text{cm}})_x = g(\sin\theta - \mu_s\cos\theta)$ in Example 11.1; that would hold only at the slipping threshold, and the example's final answer does not use it.)
> - Friction is up the slope both when the body rolls down and when it rolls up, because $a_{\text{cm}}$ is the same down-slope acceleration in both cases.
> - With **slipping**, friction is kinetic, $f_k = \mu_kN$, and $v_{\text{cm}} \neq R\omega$, $a_{\text{cm}} \neq R\alpha$. For a solid cylinder slipping down an incline, $a_{\text{cm}} = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)$ and $\alpha = 2\mu_kg\cos\theta/R$; kinetic friction does work, and mechanical energy is not conserved.

^cau-a6-5-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.5.1: Solid Cylinder on an Incline
> For a solid cylinder, $I_{\text{cm}}/(MR^2) = \tfrac12$. [[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-4|Theorem §A6.5.4]] gives
>
> $$
> a_{\text{cm}} = \frac{g\sin\theta}{1 + \frac12} = \tfrac23 g\sin\theta, \qquad \alpha = \frac{a_{\text{cm}}}{R} = \frac{2g\sin\theta}{3R}, \qquad f_s = \frac{Mg\sin\theta}{1 + 2} = \tfrac13 Mg\sin\theta ,
> $$
>
> and rolling without slipping needs $\mu_s \geq \tfrac13\tan\theta$. On a steeper incline a larger $\mu_s$ is needed; on a frictionless incline the cylinder would slide without turning, since nothing else exerts a torque about its centre.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 11.1*

^ex-a6-5-1

> [!example] Example §A6.5.2: Ring and Disc down a Ramp
> A ring (thin hoop) of mass $m$ and radius $R$ starts from rest and rolls without slipping down a fixed ramp of height $h$ and angle $\varphi$.
> - Energy at the top: $mgh$. At the bottom: $\tfrac12 mV^2 + \tfrac12(mR^2)\omega^2$ with $\omega = V/R$, i.e. $\tfrac12 mV^2 + \tfrac12 mV^2$. For a hoop the translational and rotational kinetic energies are always equal.
> - By [[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-3|Theorem §A6.5.3]], $mgh = mV^2$, so $V = \sqrt{gh}$.
> - Angular acceleration: $a_{\text{cm}} = g\sin\varphi/(1 + 1) = \tfrac12 g\sin\varphi$ ([[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-4|Theorem §A6.5.4]]), so $\alpha = g\sin\varphi/(2R)$.
> - A solid disc of the same $m$, $R$: $mgh = \tfrac12 mV^2 + \tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac12 mR^2\bigr)(V/R)^2 = \tfrac34 mV^2$, so $V = \sqrt{4gh/3} > \sqrt{gh}$.
>
> The disc is faster because a smaller share of its kinetic energy goes into rotation. Neither answer depends on $m$, $R$ or the slope angle, only on the drop $h$.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, Exam 3 (F21), Question 4; Final practice problems (F21), MC33*

^ex-a6-5-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The split $K = \tfrac12 Mv_{\text{cm}}^2 + \tfrac12 I_{\text{cm}}\omega^2$ is the expansion of $\lvert u + v\rvert^2$ with the dot product ([[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-1|Definition 6.1]]) plus the defining property of the centre of mass, the same mechanism as the parallel-axis theorem ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-3|Theorem §A6.2.3]]).
> - Rolling without slipping is the standard example of a constraint between coordinates ($x = R\theta$); constraints are handled systematically by Lagrangian mechanics at level B (planned).
