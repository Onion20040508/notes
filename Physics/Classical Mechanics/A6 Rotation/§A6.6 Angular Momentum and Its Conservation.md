Linear momentum changes at the rate of the net force, and is conserved when no external force acts ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum|§A5.2]]). The rotational counterpart is angular momentum: it changes at the rate of the net external torque, and it is conserved when that torque vanishes. This explains why a skater spins faster with her arms pulled in, and it handles collisions with pivoted bodies, where linear momentum is not conserved because the pivot pushes back.

## Angular momentum of a particle

> [!definition] Definition §A6.6.1: Angular Momentum of a Particle
> A particle with momentum $\vec p = m\vec v$ at position $\vec r$ relative to a point $O$ has **angular momentum** about $O$
>
> $$
> \vec l = \vec r\times\vec p, \qquad l = rp\sin\theta = r_\perp p = r_\perp mv ,
> $$
>
> where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ and $r_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance from $O$ to the line along $\vec p$. The unit is $\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s}$. The particle need not move on a circle; but $\vec l$ depends on the point $O$, and is zero about any point on the particle's line of motion.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.2, eq. (11.5)*

^def-a6-6-1

![[ph-cm-a6-6-1.svg]]
*A particle moving in a straight line has angular momentum about any point $O$ off its line: $\vec l = \vec r\times\vec p$ (red, out of the page) has magnitude $r_\perp p$, the same for every position along the line as long as $\vec p$ is unchanged.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.6.1: Torque Changes Angular Momentum (Particle)
> About a point $O$ fixed in an inertial frame, the angular momentum of a particle changes at the rate of the net torque on it:
>
> $$
> \frac{d\vec l}{dt} = \sum\vec\tau .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.2, eq. (11.6)*

^thm-a6-6-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the product rule for the cross product,
>
> $$
> \frac{d\vec l}{dt} = \frac{d\vec r}{dt}\times\vec p + \vec r\times\frac{d\vec p}{dt} = \vec v\times m\vec v + \vec r\times\sum\vec F = \vec r\times\sum\vec F = \sum\vec\tau ,
> $$
>
> since a vector crossed with itself is zero and $d\vec p/dt = \sum\vec F$ by the second law in momentum form.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.2*

^der-a6-6-1

*Uses:* [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-1|Def. §A6.6.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-3|Principle §A3.3.3]], [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^def-a6-3-1|Def. §A6.3.1]], cross product ([[§A1.5 Products of Vectors|§A1.5]])

## Systems and rigid bodies

> [!definition] Definition §A6.6.2: Angular Momentum of a System
> The angular momentum of a system of particles about $O$ is the vector sum of the particles' angular momenta about the same point: $\vec L = \vec l_1 + \vec l_2 + \dots + \vec l_N$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.2, eq. (11.7)*

^def-a6-6-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.6.2: Torque Changes Angular Momentum (System)
> About a point fixed in an inertial frame, the total angular momentum of a system changes at the rate of the net **external** torque:
>
> $$
> \frac{d\vec L}{dt} = \sum\vec\tau_{\text{ext}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.2, eq. (11.8)*

^thm-a6-6-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Add [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-1|Theorem §A6.6.1]] over the particles: $d\vec L/dt$ is the sum of all torques, external and internal. Let $\vec F_{ij}$ be the force on particle $i$ from particle $j$. By the third law $\vec F_{ji} = -\vec F_{ij}$, so the torques of the pair add to
>
> $$
> \vec r_i\times\vec F_{ij} + \vec r_j\times\vec F_{ji} = (\vec r_i - \vec r_j)\times\vec F_{ij} = \vec 0
> $$
>
> provided the two forces act along the line joining the particles, which is true of gravitational, electrostatic and contact forces between particles. So the internal torques cancel in pairs. (UP1 states the result without discussing the internal torques.)
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.2; cancellation of internal torques written here*

^der-a6-6-2

*Uses:* [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-1|Theorem §A6.6.1]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-2|Def. §A6.6.2]], Newton's third law ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law|§A3.5]])

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.6.3: Angular Momentum of a Rotating Rigid Body
> A rigid body rotating with angular velocity $\omega$ about a fixed axis ($z$) has angular momentum component along the axis
>
> $$
> L_z = I\omega ,
> $$
>
> with $I$ the moment of inertia about the axis. If the body is symmetric about the axis, the perpendicular components cancel and $\vec L = I\vec\omega$, along the axis by the right-hand rule. Then [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]] gives $\sum\tau_z = dL_z/dt = I\alpha$, the second law for rotation again.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.2, eq. (11.9)*

^thm-a6-6-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the origin on the axis. A mass element $\Delta m_i$ at $\vec r_i$, at distance $R_i$ from the axis, moves on a circle with speed $v_i = R_i\omega$, perpendicular to $\vec r_i$; so $l_i = r_i\,\Delta m_i\,v_i$. Its direction is perpendicular to $\vec r_i$ and $\vec v_i$, at angle $90^\circ - \theta_i$ to the axis if $\vec r_i$ makes angle $\theta_i$ with it, so
>
> $$
> (l_i)_z = l_i\sin\theta_i = (r_i\sin\theta_i)\,\Delta m_i\,v_i = R_i\,\Delta m_i\,R_i\omega .
> $$
>
> Summing, $L_z = \bigl(\sum_i\Delta m_iR_i^2\bigr)\omega = I\omega$. For a body symmetric about the axis, each element has a partner on the opposite side whose perpendicular component of $\vec l$ is opposite, so only $L_z$ remains.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.2*

^der-a6-6-3

*Uses:* [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-1|Def. §A6.6.1]], [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^thm-a6-1-2|Theorem §A6.1.2]], [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]]

## Conservation of angular momentum

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.6.4: Conservation of Angular Momentum
> If the net external torque on a system about a point fixed in an inertial frame is zero, the total angular momentum about that point is constant:
>
> $$
> \sum\vec\tau_{\text{ext}} = \vec 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec L = \vec l_1 + \dots + \vec l_N = \text{constant}.
> $$
>
> The same holds component by component: if the external torques about an axis vanish, $L$ along that axis is constant. For a body turning about a fixed axis whose moment of inertia changes, $I_i\omega_i = I_f\omega_f$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.3, eqs. (11.10)–(11.11)*

^thm-a6-6-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]], $d\vec L/dt = \sum\vec\tau_{\text{ext}} = \vec 0$, so $\vec L$ is constant; taking the component along an axis gives the component statement. For a body about a fixed axis, $L = I\omega$ ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-3|Theorem §A6.6.3]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.3*

^der-a6-6-4

*Uses:* [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-3|Theorem §A6.6.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Law or theorem?
> UP1 calls this the *law* of conservation of angular momentum. Here it follows from Newton's laws, with internal forces along the lines joining the particles, so it is filed as a theorem, just as conservation of momentum follows from the third law ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum|§A5.2]]). Only the total is conserved: parts of the system can exchange angular momentum, as the helicopter body would spin opposite to its rotor without the tail rotor's torque. At the upper level conservation of angular momentum reappears as the consequence of rotational symmetry (level B, planned).
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.3, Ch. 11 introduction*

^rem-a6-6-1

> [!remark] Remark: Angular momentum is conserved, kinetic energy need not be
> With $I_f\omega_f = I_i\omega_i$,
>
> $$
> K_f = \tfrac12 I_f\omega_f^2 = \tfrac12 I_i\omega_i^2\,\frac{I_i}{I_f} = K_i\,\frac{I_i}{I_f} .
> $$
>
> A skater pulling in her arms ($I_f < I_i$) spins faster and gains kinetic energy: her muscles do positive work pulling the arms inward on their spiral path. Two flywheels that grab each other by friction ($I_f > I_i$ for the spinning part) lose kinetic energy to heat, like a perfectly inelastic collision. A collapsing gas cloud or star spins up for the same reason as the skater.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.3, Example 11.7*

^rem-a6-6-2

> [!caution] Caution: Same point, external torques, and the pivot
> - Every angular momentum and torque in one equation must be taken about the **same** point or axis.
> - A pivot or axle can exert a large impulsive force during a collision: linear momentum of the system is then not conserved, but the angular momentum about the axle is, since that force has no torque about it.
> - Conservation of $L$ does not mean $\omega$ is constant: $\omega$ changes whenever $I$ does.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.3, Example 11.9*

^cau-a6-6-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.6.1: Angular Momentum of a Falling Meteor
> An observer at the origin sees a meteor of mass $m = 15.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ at $\vec r = (25.0\,\hat\imath + 25.0\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm{km}$, moving straight down, $\vec v = -2.0\times10^3\ \mathrm{m/s}\,\hat\jmath$, with constant acceleration $2.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ along $-\hat\jmath$. Only the $x$-component of $\vec r$ is perpendicular to $\vec p$, so
>
> $$
> \vec l = \vec r\times m\vec v = x\,m v_y\,\hat k = (2.50\times10^4\ \mathrm m)(15.0\ \mathrm{kg})(-2.0\times10^3\ \mathrm{m/s})\,\hat k = -7.5\times10^8\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s}\,\hat k .
> $$
>
> As $v_y$ grows in magnitude, $d\vec l/dt = x\,m a_y\,\hat k = -7.5\times10^5\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}\,\hat k$, which is exactly the torque of the net force $\vec F = -30.0\ \mathrm N\,\hat\jmath$ with lever arm $x = 25.0\ \mathrm{km}$ ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-1|Theorem §A6.6.1]]). A body moving in a straight line has angular momentum about a point off its path; it would stay constant if the meteor did not accelerate.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 11.4*

^ex-a6-6-1

> [!example] Example §A6.6.2: Gymnast's Tuck
> An $80.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ gymnast leaves a high bar at full extension, modelled as a rod of length $1.8\ \mathrm m$ rotating about its centre at $1.0\ \mathrm{rev/s}$, and tucks into a rod of length $0.9\ \mathrm m$. In the air gravity acts at his centre of mass and has no torque about it, so his angular momentum about the centre of mass is conserved:
>
> $$
> I_0 = \tfrac1{12}(80.0)(1.8)^2 = 21.6\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}, \quad I_f = \tfrac1{12}(80.0)(0.9)^2 = 5.4\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}, \quad \omega_f = \frac{I_0}{I_f}\,\omega_0 = 4.0\ \mathrm{rev/s}.
> $$
>
> If he tucks at $3.0\ \mathrm m$ moving horizontally and opens at $1.8\ \mathrm m$, he falls $1.2\ \mathrm m$ in $t = \sqrt{2(1.2\ \mathrm m)/g} = 0.49\ \mathrm s$: about two revolutions. Halving his length quadruples his spin rate and, by the remark above, quadruples his rotational kinetic energy, which his muscles supply.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 11.8*

^ex-a6-6-2

> [!example] Example §A6.6.3: Bullet into a Pivoted Disc
> A bullet ($m = 2.0\ \mathrm g$, $v = 500\ \mathrm{m/s}$) moving along a line tangent to the rim embeds itself at the edge of a disc ($M = 3.2\ \mathrm{kg}$, $R = 0.50\ \mathrm m$) free to turn about its fixed central axis, initially at rest. The axle force has no torque about the axis, so the angular momentum about the axis is conserved, the bullet's initial $l = mvR$:
>
> $$
> mvR = \bigl(mR^2 + \tfrac12 MR^2\bigr)\omega \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega = \frac{mv}{(m + M/2)R} = \frac{(0.0020)(500)}{(1.602)(0.50)}\ \mathrm{rad/s} = 1.2\ \mathrm{rad/s}.
> $$
>
> Linear momentum is not conserved (the axle stops the centre from moving), and kinetic energy falls from $250\ \mathrm J$ to $\tfrac12 I\omega^2 \approx 0.31\ \mathrm J$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 11.9*

^ex-a6-6-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The derivations use the product rule for the cross product and its antisymmetry ($\vec v\times\vec v = \vec 0$), from [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors|§A1.5]].
> - Conservation of angular momentum about the Sun gives Kepler's second law ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws|§A8.3]]).
> - When the torque is perpendicular to $\vec L$ it turns $\vec L$ without changing its size: precession ([[§A6.7 Precession|§A6.7]]).
