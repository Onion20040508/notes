A rotating body has kinetic energy even when its centre stays put, but every point moves at a different speed, so $\tfrac12 mv^2$ cannot be applied to the body as a whole. Writing each speed as $r\omega$ ([[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^thm-a6-1-2|Theorem §A6.1.2]]) puts the energy in terms of the single angular velocity, and the price is a new property of the body, its moment of inertia, which plays the role of mass for rotation.

## Moment of inertia and rotational kinetic energy

> [!definition] Definition §A6.2.1: Moment of Inertia
> The **moment of inertia** of a system of point masses $m_j$ about a given axis is
>
> $$
> I = \sum_j m_j r_j^2 ,
> $$
>
> where $r_j$ is the perpendicular distance of $m_j$ from the axis. For a continuous body the sum becomes an integral over its mass elements, $I = \int r^2\,dm$. The unit is $\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$. $I$ depends on the axis as well as on the body.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.4–10.5, eqs. (10.17), (10.19)*

^def-a6-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.2.1: Rotational Kinetic Energy
> A rigid body turning with angular velocity $\omega$ about a fixed axis has kinetic energy
>
> $$
> K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 ,
> $$
>
> with $I$ the moment of inertia about that axis.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.4, eqs. (10.16), (10.18)*

^thm-a6-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Split the body into masses $m_j$ at distances $r_j$ from the axis. Each moves on a circle with speed $v_j = r_j\omega$, with the same $\omega$ for all. Adding their kinetic energies,
>
> $$
> K = \sum_j \tfrac12 m_j v_j^2 = \sum_j \tfrac12 m_j r_j^2\omega^2 = \tfrac12\Bigl(\sum_j m_j r_j^2\Bigr)\omega^2 = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 .
> $$

^der-a6-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^thm-a6-1-2|Theorem §A6.1.2]], [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]], [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]] (kinetic energy of a particle)

> [!remark] Remark: Rotational inertia
> - $I$ is to rotation what $m$ is to translation: $K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2$ against $K = \tfrac12 mv^2$, and in [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]] a larger $I$ means a smaller angular acceleration for a given torque. Unlike $m$, it is not fixed by the body: it depends on how the mass is placed relative to the chosen axis.
> - Mass far from the axis counts with weight $r^2$: one factor $r$ because it moves faster ($v = r\omega$), squared in the kinetic energy. So a hoop has more rotational inertia than a disc of equal mass and radius, and a skater who pulls in her arms lowers her $I$ ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^rem-a6-6-2|Remark in §A6.6]]).
> - Rotational kinetic energy is not a new form of energy: it is the ordinary kinetic energy of the body's points, written in terms of $\omega$.

^rem-a6-2-1

## Calculating moments of inertia

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.2.2: Thin Rod and Thin Disc
> For a uniform thin rod of mass $M$ and length $L$, about an axis perpendicular to it,
>
> $$
> I_{\text{centre}} = \tfrac1{12}ML^2, \qquad I_{\text{end}} = \tfrac13 ML^2 .
> $$
>
> For a uniform thin disc (or solid cylinder) of mass $M$ and radius $R$, about its symmetry axis, $I = \tfrac12 MR^2$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.5*

^thm-a6-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Rod.* Put the rod on the $x$-axis. Its linear mass density is $\lambda = M/L$, so a piece of length $dx$ has $dm = \lambda\,dx$ at distance $|x|$ from an axis through $x = 0$. With the axis at the centre the rod runs from $-L/2$ to $L/2$:
>
> $$
> I = \int_{-L/2}^{L/2} x^2\lambda\,dx = \frac{\lambda}{3}\Bigl[\bigl(\tfrac L2\bigr)^3 - \bigl(-\tfrac L2\bigr)^3\Bigr] = \frac{\lambda L^3}{12} = \frac{ML^2}{12};
> $$
>
> with the axis at one end it runs from $0$ to $L$, and $I = \lambda L^3/3 = ML^2/3$.
>
> *Disc.* The surface density is $\sigma = M/(\pi R^2)$. In polar coordinates the area element is $dA = r\,dr\,d\phi$, so a thin ring of radius $r$ and width $dr$ has area $2\pi r\,dr$ and all of it is at distance $r$ from the axis:
>
> $$
> I = \int_0^R r^2\,\sigma\,2\pi r\,dr = 2\pi\sigma\,\frac{R^4}{4} = 2\pi\,\frac{M}{\pi R^2}\,\frac{R^4}{4} = \tfrac12 MR^2 .
> $$
>
> A solid cylinder is a stack of such discs, each contributing $\tfrac12 R^2\,dm$, so it has the same $I = \tfrac12 MR^2$.

^der-a6-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-7|452 Thm. §15.7]] (integration in polar coordinates), [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: A short table
> | Body (uniform, mass $M$) | Axis | $I$ |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | thin rod, length $L$ | perpendicular, through centre | $\tfrac1{12}ML^2$ |
> | thin rod, length $L$ | perpendicular, through one end | $\tfrac13 ML^2$ |
> | thin hoop or thin-walled cylinder, radius $R$ | symmetry axis | $MR^2$ |
> | disc or solid cylinder, radius $R$ | symmetry axis | $\tfrac12 MR^2$ |
> | solid sphere, radius $R$ | through centre | $\tfrac25 MR^2$ |
> | rectangular slab, side $L$ across the axis | along one edge | $\tfrac13 ML^2$ |
> | rectangular slab, side $L$ across the axis | in its plane, through the centre | $\tfrac1{12}ML^2$ |
>
> The hoop value is immediate (all mass at distance $R$); the slab values are those of the rod, since a slab is a row of rods all at the same distances; the sphere value is quoted, not derived here.

^rem-a6-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.2.3: Parallel-Axis Theorem
> Let $I_{\text{cm}}$ be the moment of inertia of a body of mass $M$ about an axis through its centre of mass, and let a second axis be parallel to it at distance $d$. About the second axis,
>
> $$
> I = I_{\text{cm}} + Md^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.5, eq. (10.20)*

^thm-a6-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take both axes along $z$ and work with components in the $xy$-plane. Let $\vec\rho_j$ be the perpendicular position of $m_j$ relative to the axis through the centre of mass, and $\vec d$ the perpendicular vector from the new axis to the centre-of-mass axis, so that the perpendicular position of $m_j$ relative to the new axis is $\vec d + \vec\rho_j$. Then
>
> $$
> I = \sum_j m_j\,|\vec d + \vec\rho_j|^2 = \sum_j m_j\bigl(d^2 + 2\,\vec d\cdot\vec\rho_j + \rho_j^2\bigr) = Md^2 + 2\,\vec d\cdot\Bigl(\sum_j m_j\vec\rho_j\Bigr) + I_{\text{cm}} .
> $$
>
> The middle sum is $M$ times the perpendicular position of the centre of mass relative to its own axis, which is zero. So $I = I_{\text{cm}} + Md^2$.

^der-a6-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]], [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]] (centre of mass), [[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-1|LADR Def. 6.1]] (dot product, $\lvert u+v\rvert^2 = u\cdot u + 2u\cdot v + v\cdot v$)

![[ph-cm-a6-2-1.svg]]
*Cross-section perpendicular to the two parallel axes. The mass element $m_j$ sits at $\vec\rho_j$ from the centre-of-mass axis and at $\vec d + \vec\rho_j$ (red) from the new axis $P$; averaged over the body, the cross term $\vec d\cdot\vec\rho_j$ vanishes.*

> [!remark] Remark: Compound bodies
> Since $I$ is a sum over mass elements, the moment of inertia of a body made of parts is the sum of the parts' moments of inertia, **all about the same axis**: $I_{\text{total}} = \sum_i I_i$. A part whose own centre is off the axis enters through the parallel-axis theorem. For example, a rod of mass $m_r$ and length $L$ turning about one end, with a disc of mass $m_d$ and radius $R$ attached flat at its far end (disc centre at $L + R$), has $I = \tfrac13 m_r L^2 + \tfrac12 m_d R^2 + m_d(L+R)^2$.

^rem-a6-2-3

> [!caution] Caution: Which axis?
> - Every $I$ belongs to an axis; quote the axis with the number. Moving the axis changes $I$, and the centre-of-mass axis gives the smallest value among parallel axes ($Md^2 \geq 0$).
> - The parallel-axis theorem connects the centre-of-mass axis to another axis. Between two axes neither of which passes through the centre of mass, go through $I_{\text{cm}}$.
> - $r$ in $I = \sum m r^2$ is the distance to the **axis**, not to the origin.

^cau-a6-2-1

## Energy conservation with rotation

> [!remark] Remark: Mechanical energy including rotation
> When only conservative forces do work, conservation of mechanical energy ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]) holds with the rotational kinetic energy included: $K_{\text{trans}} + K_{\text{rot}} + U$ is constant. For a body turning about a fixed axis all its kinetic energy is $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$ about that axis, and gravitational potential energy ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-1|Theorem §A4.4.1]]) summed over the body is $\sum_j m_jgh_j = Mg\,h_{\text{cm}}$, with $h_{\text{cm}}$ the height of the centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]]). The torque does not have to be computed: this is often the quickest route to a speed.

^rem-a6-2-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.2.1: Solid or Hollow?
> A solid cylinder and a thin-walled hollow cylinder have the same mass $M$ and radius $R$. About their axes, $I_{\text{solid}} = \tfrac12 MR^2$ and $I_{\text{hollow}} = MR^2$: the hollow one has the larger moment of inertia, because all its mass is at the largest distance $R$. If both have the same rotational kinetic energy, then $\tfrac12 I_{\text{s}}\omega_{\text{s}}^2 = \tfrac12 I_{\text{h}}\omega_{\text{h}}^2$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{\omega_{\text{s}}}{\omega_{\text{h}}} = \sqrt{\frac{I_{\text{h}}}{I_{\text{s}}}} = \sqrt2 :
> $$
>
> the solid cylinder spins faster.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 23*

^ex-a6-2-1

> [!example] Example §A6.2.2: Rod and Sphere
> A rod of mass $m_r = 2.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ and length $L = 0.50\ \mathrm m$ carries a solid sphere of mass $m_s = 1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ and radius $R = 0.20\ \mathrm m$ at one end, the sphere's centre on the rod's line at $L + R$ from the free end. Both axes are perpendicular to the rod.
>
> (a) Axis through the free end of the rod. The rod contributes $\tfrac13 m_r L^2$; the sphere, by [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-3|Theorem §A6.2.3]], $\tfrac25 m_s R^2 + m_s(L+R)^2$:
>
> $$
> I = \bigl(0.167 + 0.016 + 0.490\bigr)\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2} = 0.673\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2} .
> $$
>
> (b) Axis where rod and sphere meet. The rod still turns about its end; the sphere's centre is now at distance $R$:
>
> $$
> I = \tfrac13 m_r L^2 + \tfrac25 m_s R^2 + m_s R^2 = \bigl(0.167 + 0.016 + 0.040\bigr)\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2} = 0.223\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2} .
> $$
>
> The same object is three times harder to spin in (a): the sphere, the heaviest concentrated part, sits far from that axis.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 10.12*

^ex-a6-2-2

> [!example] Example §A6.2.3: Falling Trapdoor
> A uniform square trapdoor of side $L = 1.00\ \mathrm m$ and mass $M = 10.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ is hinged along one edge and held horizontal. When released it swings down about the hinge; friction is negligible. About the hinge it is a slab about an edge, so
>
> $$
> I = \tfrac13 ML^2 = 3.33\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2} .
> $$
>
> When the door hangs vertically its centre of mass has dropped by $L/2$. Energy conservation with all kinetic energy rotational about the hinge:
>
> $$
> Mg\frac L2 = \tfrac12\Bigl(\tfrac13 ML^2\Bigr)\omega^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{3g}{L}} = 5.42\ \mathrm{rad/s}, \qquad v_{\text{cm}} = \omega\frac L2 = 2.71\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> This is less than the $\sqrt{gL} = 3.13\ \mathrm{m/s}$ of a point dropped through $L/2$: the door's points do not all move with its centre of mass, and the kinetic energy $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$ is larger than $\tfrac12 Mv_{\text{cm}}^2$. The mass cancels, as for the rod pendulum of UP1 Example 10.13.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, Final practice problems (F21), Question 2*

^ex-a6-2-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The disc integral is a polar-coordinate integral ([[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-7|452 Thm. §15.7]]); other shapes need the same multiple integrals in other coordinates.
> - The parallel-axis theorem is the expansion of $\lvert u + v\rvert^2$ with the dot product ([[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-1|LADR Def. 6.1]]) plus the defining property of the centre of mass.
