The work–energy theorem and power of [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem|§A4.2]] and [[§A4.3 Power|§A4.3]] were stated for forces moving through distances. For a body turning about a fixed axis it is more natural to measure the motion by the angle: a torque acting through an angle does work, that work changes $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$, and the rate of doing it is $\tau\omega$. This completes the dictionary between translation and fixed-axis rotation.

## Work done by a torque

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.4.1: Rotational Work
> When a rigid body turns about a fixed axis through $d\theta$, the external forces on it do work $dW = \bigl(\sum_i\tau_i\bigr)\,d\theta$, where the $\tau_i$ are their torques about the axis. Through a finite angle,
>
> $$
> W_{AB} = \int_{\theta_A}^{\theta_B}\Bigl(\sum_i\tau_i\Bigr)\,d\theta, \qquad W_{AB} = \tau\,(\theta_B - \theta_A) \ \text{ for a constant net torque } \tau .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.8, eqs. (10.27)–(10.28), (10.30)*

^thm-a6-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let a force $\vec F$ act at a point at $\vec r$ from the axis, and let $d\vec\theta$ be the vector of length $d\theta$ along the axis (right-hand rule). The point moves on its circle by $d\vec s = d\vec\theta\times\vec r$ ([[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^thm-a6-1-2|Theorem §A6.1.2]] with $\vec\omega\,dt = d\vec\theta$). Its work is, by the cyclic property of the scalar triple product,
>
> $$
> dW = \vec F\cdot\bigl(d\vec\theta\times\vec r\bigr) = d\vec\theta\cdot\bigl(\vec r\times\vec F\bigr) = \vec\tau\cdot d\vec\theta = \tau_z\,d\theta ,
> $$
>
> with $\tau_z$ the component of the torque along the axis. Add over all external forces and integrate. (Internal forces of a rigid body do no net work: each pair is equal and opposite along the line joining two points whose distance does not change.)
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.8; the remark on internal forces is added here*

^der-a6-4-1

*Uses:* work of a force ([[§A4.1 Work|§A4.1]]), [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^def-a6-3-1|Def. §A6.3.1]], [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^thm-a6-1-2|Theorem §A6.1.2]], scalar triple product ([[§A1.5 Products of Vectors|§A1.5]])

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.4.2: Work–Energy Theorem for Rotation
> For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, the work done by the net external torque from angular position $A$ to $B$ equals the change in rotational kinetic energy:
>
> $$
> W_{AB} = \tfrac12 I\omega_B^2 - \tfrac12 I\omega_A^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.8, eq. (10.29)*

^thm-a6-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]], $\sum\tau = I\alpha$. Along the motion $\theta$ and $\omega$ are functions of $t$, and by the chain rule $\alpha\,d\theta = \dfrac{d\omega}{dt}\,\omega\,dt = \omega\,d\omega$. So
>
> $$
> W_{AB} = \int_{\theta_A}^{\theta_B} I\alpha\,d\theta = \int_{\omega_A}^{\omega_B} I\omega\,d\omega = \tfrac12 I\omega_B^2 - \tfrac12 I\omega_A^2 .
> $$
>
> UP1 argues instead that the particle work–energy theorem holds for every particle of the body and therefore for their sum; both routes give the same result.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.8 (derivation via the second law written here)*

^der-a6-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^thm-a6-3-1|Theorem §A6.3.1]], [[§A6.4 Work and Power in Rotation#^thm-a6-4-1|Theorem §A6.4.1]], [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-1|Theorem §A6.2.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|Theorem §28.3]] (chain rule), [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|Theorem §34.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.4.3: Rotational Power
> The power delivered to a body rotating about a fixed axis by a torque $\tau$ about that axis is
>
> $$
> P = \tau\omega .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.8, eq. (10.31)*

^thm-a6-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Power is the rate of doing work ([[§A4.3 Power|§A4.3]]). By [[§A6.4 Work and Power in Rotation#^thm-a6-4-1|Theorem §A6.4.1]], $dW = \tau\,d\theta$, so $P = dW/dt = \tau\,d\theta/dt = \tau\omega$. (UP1 derives this for constant torque; the step holds at each instant for any torque.)
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.8*

^der-a6-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A6.4 Work and Power in Rotation#^thm-a6-4-1|Theorem §A6.4.1]], [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^def-a6-1-2|Def. §A6.1.2]], [[§A4.3 Power|§A4.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Radians make the units work
> $\tau\,\Delta\theta$ is in joules and $\tau\omega$ in watts only with $\theta$ in radians and $\omega$ in $\mathrm{rad/s}$; the radian, a ratio of lengths, then drops out of the units. A rope pulled through a length $d$ off a drum of radius $R$ turns it by $\theta = d/R$, so $W = (FR)\theta = Fd$: the torque picture and the force picture give the same work.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.8 (Examples 10.18–10.19)*

^cau-a6-4-1

## The dictionary

> [!remark] Remark: Translation and fixed-axis rotation side by side
> | Translation | Fixed-axis rotation |
> | --- | --- |
> | $x$, $v$, $a$ | $\theta$, $\omega$, $\alpha$ ([[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics\|§A6.1]]) |
> | $m$ | $I = \sum m_jr_j^2$ ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1\|Def. §A6.2.1]]) |
> | $K = \tfrac12 mv^2$ | $K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2$ ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-1\|Theorem §A6.2.1]]) |
> | $\sum\vec F = m\vec a$ | $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ ([[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^thm-a6-3-1\|Theorem §A6.3.1]]) |
> | $W = \int\vec F\cdot d\vec s$ | $W = \int\bigl(\sum\tau\bigr)\,d\theta$ |
> | $P = \vec F\cdot\vec v$ | $P = \tau\omega$ |
> | $\vec p = m\vec v$ | $L = I\omega$ ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation\|§A6.6]]) |
>
> The analogy is exact for rotation about a fixed axis, and it is a good way to remember the formulas. It is an analogy of form only: $I$ depends on the axis, and the rotational quantities are vectors along the axis rather than along the motion.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.8, Tables 10.5–10.7*

^rem-a6-4-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.4.1: Pulling a String off a Pulley
> A string wound on a pulley of radius $R = 0.10\ \mathrm m$ and moment of inertia $I = 2.5\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ is pulled with a constant force $F = 50\ \mathrm N$; the string does not slip and the pulley starts from rest. The bearing force and the pulley's weight act at the axle and do no work. After $d = 1.0\ \mathrm m$ of string has come off, the pulley has turned through $\theta = d/R$, and by [[§A6.4 Work and Power in Rotation#^thm-a6-4-2|Theorem §A6.4.2]]
>
> $$
> (FR)\,\theta = Fd = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{2Fd}{I}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(50\ \mathrm N)(1.0\ \mathrm m)}{2.5\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}}} = 200\ \mathrm{rad/s} .
> $$
>
> No angular acceleration or time was needed.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 10.18*

^ex-a6-4-1

> [!example] Example §A6.4.2: Stopping a Wheel
> A wheel of mass $M = 1.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, modelled as a thin hoop of radius $R = 1.0\ \mathrm m$ ($I = MR^2 = 1.0\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$), turns at $300\ \mathrm{rev/min}$ and must be stopped in $15.0\ \mathrm s$. Here $\omega_0 = 300\cdot 2\pi/60\ \mathrm{rad/s} = 31.4\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, so
>
> $$
> K_0 = \tfrac12 I\omega_0^2 = 493\ \mathrm J, \qquad W = 0 - K_0 = -493\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> The work does not depend on the stopping time. As a check with a constant braking torque: $\alpha = -\omega_0/(15.0\ \mathrm s) = -2.09\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}$, $\tau = I\alpha = -2.09\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}$, $\Delta\theta = \bar\omega t = \tfrac12(31.4)(15.0)\ \mathrm{rad} = 236\ \mathrm{rad}$, and $\tau\,\Delta\theta = -493\ \mathrm J$, as [[§A6.4 Work and Power in Rotation#^thm-a6-4-1|Theorem §A6.4.1]] requires. The brake's power $\tau\omega$ starts at $-65.8\ \mathrm W$ and falls to zero.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 23*

^ex-a6-4-2

> [!example] Example §A6.4.3: Torque on a Propeller Shaft
> A boat engine delivers $P = 9.0\times10^4\ \mathrm W$ while turning at $300\ \mathrm{rev/min} = 31.4\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. By [[§A6.4 Work and Power in Rotation#^thm-a6-4-3|Theorem §A6.4.3]] the torque on the shaft is
>
> $$
> \tau = \frac P\omega = \frac{9.0\times10^4\ \mathrm W}{31.4\ \mathrm{rad/s}} = 2.9\times10^3\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} .
> $$
>
> At a given power, a slowly turning shaft carries a large torque, which is why gearboxes trade speed for torque.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 10.19*

^ex-a6-4-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The step $\int I\alpha\,d\theta = \int I\omega\,d\omega$ is the same change of variables, by the chain rule ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|Theorem §28.3]]), that turns $\int ma\,dx$ into $\Delta(\tfrac12 mv^2)$ in [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem|§A4.2]].
> - With rolling ([[§A6.5 Rolling|§A6.5]]) the kinetic energy has both a translational and a rotational part, and energy conservation combines them.
