So far bodies have been points, which can only translate. A wheel, a fan or a door also turns, and its points move with different velocities. For a rigid body turning about a fixed axis, one angle describes where every point is, so rotation has its own kinematics: angular position, velocity and acceleration, which mirror $x$, $v$, $a$ of Chapter A2 and connect to the linear motion of each point through its distance from the axis.

## Rigid rotation about a fixed axis

> [!model] Model §A6.1.1: Rigid Body Rotating about a Fixed Axis
> A **rigid body** is a body whose points keep all their mutual distances: it does not deform. Here it turns about an axis that stays fixed in an inertial frame. Then
> - every point of the body moves on a circle centred on the axis, in a plane perpendicular to it;
> - in a given time all points turn through the same angle.
>
> So a single angle $\theta(t)$ fixes the position of the whole body.
>
> *Fails when:* the body deforms noticeably under the forces on it (elasticity, [[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli|§A7.2]]), for fluids, and when the axis itself moves; rolling ([[§A6.5 Rolling|§A6.5]]) and precession ([[§A6.7 Precession|§A6.7]]) extend the description to moving axes.
> *Source: UP1 Ch. 10 introduction, §12.3*

^mod-a6-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A6.1.1: Angular Position; Radian
> Fix a reference line perpendicular to the axis (the $x$-axis). The **angular position** $\theta$ of a point of the body is the angle from the reference line to the line from the axis to the point, counted positive counterclockwise. If the point sits at distance $r$ from the axis and has travelled the arc length $s$ along its circle from the reference line,
>
> $$
> \theta = \frac{s}{r} \qquad (\text{in radians}).
> $$
>
> The radian is a ratio of two lengths, so it is dimensionless; one revolution is $2\pi\ \mathrm{rad} = 360^\circ$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.1, eq. (10.1)*

^def-a6-1-1

## Angular velocity and angular acceleration

> [!definition] Definition §A6.1.2: Angular Velocity
> The **average angular velocity** over a time $\Delta t$ is $\bar\omega = \Delta\theta/\Delta t$; the (instantaneous) **angular velocity** is
>
> $$
> \omega = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\theta}{dt}, \qquad \text{unit } \mathrm{rad/s}.
> $$
>
> It is the same for every point of a rigid body. The **angular velocity vector** $\vec\omega$ lies along the axis, with the sense given by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of the right hand in the direction of rotation; the thumb points along $\vec\omega$. Counterclockwise rotation in the $xy$-plane has $\vec\omega$ along $+\hat k$ (and $\omega > 0$); clockwise rotation has $\vec\omega$ along $-\hat k$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.1, eq. (10.3)*

^def-a6-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §A6.1.3: Angular Acceleration
> The **average angular acceleration** is $\bar\alpha = \Delta\omega/\Delta t$; the (instantaneous) **angular acceleration** is
>
> $$
> \alpha = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\omega}{dt} = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}, \qquad \text{unit } \mathrm{rad/s^2}.
> $$
>
> The vector $\vec\alpha = d\vec\omega/dt$ also lies along the axis: parallel to $\vec\omega$ when the rotation speeds up, antiparallel when it slows down.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.1, eq. (10.6)*

^def-a6-1-3

> [!caution] Caution: Radians and signs
> - The relations below ($v_t = r\omega$, $a_t = r\alpha$, …) hold only with angles in radians. Convert first: $\omega = 2\pi \times (\text{rev/s})$, and $1\ \mathrm{rpm} = 2\pi/60\ \mathrm{rad/s}$.
> - A negative $\alpha$ does not mean "slowing down" by itself: it means $\vec\alpha$ points along $-\hat k$. The rotation slows down exactly when $\alpha$ and $\omega$ have opposite signs, as for $v$ and $a$ in one dimension.
> - $\theta$ keeps counting past $2\pi$: after $N$ turns, $\Delta\theta = 2\pi N$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.1–10.2*

^cau-a6-1-1

## Linear quantities of a point on the body

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.2: Tangential Speed and Tangential Acceleration
> A point of the body at distance $r$ from the axis has speed and tangential acceleration
>
> $$
> v_t = r\omega, \qquad a_t = r\alpha .
> $$
>
> In vector form, with $\vec r$ the position of the point measured from the centre of its circle,
>
> $$
> \vec v = \vec\omega \times \vec r, \qquad \vec a_t = \vec\alpha \times \vec r .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.1, eqs. (10.4), (10.5), (10.7), (10.8)*

^thm-a6-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The point stays at distance $r$, so its arc length is $s = r\theta$ with $r$ constant. Differentiating, $ds/dt = r\,d\theta/dt$; the left side is the speed along the circle, so $v_t = r\omega$. Differentiating once more, $a_t = dv_t/dt = r\,d\omega/dt = r\alpha$.
>
> For the vector form: $\vec\omega$ is along the axis and $\vec r$ lies in the plane of the circle, so they are perpendicular and $|\vec\omega\times\vec r| = \omega r = v_t$. The cross product is perpendicular to the axis and to $\vec r$, so it is tangent to the circle, and by the right-hand rule it points in the sense of rotation. The same argument with $\vec\alpha$ gives $\vec a_t$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.1*

^der-a6-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]], [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^def-a6-1-2|Def. §A6.1.2]], [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^def-a6-1-3|Def. §A6.1.3]], cross product ([[§A1.5 Products of Vectors|§A1.5]]), [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|Theorem §28.2]] (constant factor in a derivative)

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.3: Total Acceleration of a Point
> The acceleration of a point at distance $r$ from the axis is the sum of a tangential and a centripetal part,
>
> $$
> \vec a = \vec a_t + \vec a_c, \qquad \vec a_t = \vec\alpha \times \vec r, \qquad \vec a_c = -\omega^2\,\vec r ,
> $$
>
> with magnitudes $a_t = r\alpha$ and $a_c = r\omega^2 = v_t^2/r$. The two parts are perpendicular, so $|\vec a| = \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_t^2}$. The centripetal part changes the direction of $\vec v$; the tangential part changes its magnitude.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.3, eqs. (10.14)–(10.15)*

^thm-a6-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate $\vec v = \vec\omega \times \vec r$ with the product rule (valid for the cross product, which is bilinear):
>
> $$
> \vec a = \frac{d\vec\omega}{dt}\times\vec r + \vec\omega\times\frac{d\vec r}{dt} = \vec\alpha\times\vec r + \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r).
> $$
>
> By the identity $\vec A\times(\vec B\times\vec C) = \vec B(\vec A\cdot\vec C) - \vec C(\vec A\cdot\vec B)$, the last term is $\vec\omega(\vec\omega\cdot\vec r) - \vec r\,\omega^2 = -\omega^2\vec r$, because $\vec\omega \perp \vec r$. It points from the point toward the centre, with magnitude $r\omega^2 = v_t^2/r$: the centripetal acceleration of [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion|§A2.7]]. The first term is tangent to the circle, hence perpendicular to the second.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.3 states the result and leaves the vector derivation as an exercise; argument written here*

## Constant angular acceleration

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.4: Rotational Kinematic Equations
> If $\alpha$ is constant, with $\theta = \theta_0$ and $\omega = \omega_0$ at $t = 0$,
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \omega &= \omega_0 + \alpha t, &\qquad \theta &= \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2,\\
> \omega^2 &= \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha(\theta - \theta_0), &\qquad \theta &= \theta_0 + \bar\omega\, t, \quad \bar\omega = \tfrac12(\omega_0 + \omega).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.2, eqs. (10.9)–(10.13)*

^thm-a6-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Integrate $d\omega/dt = \alpha$ from $0$ to $t$; by the fundamental theorem of calculus $\omega(t) - \omega_0 = \int_0^t \alpha\,dt' = \alpha t$. Integrate $d\theta/dt = \omega_0 + \alpha t$ the same way: $\theta - \theta_0 = \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2$. Substituting $t = (\omega - \omega_0)/\alpha$ into this gives $\theta - \theta_0 = (\omega^2 - \omega_0^2)/(2\alpha)$. Finally $\omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2 = \tfrac12\bigl(\omega_0 + (\omega_0 + \alpha t)\bigr)t = \bar\omega\,t$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.2*

^der-a6-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^def-a6-1-2|Def. §A6.1.2]], [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^def-a6-1-3|Def. §A6.1.3]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|Theorem §34.1]] (fundamental theorem of calculus)

> [!remark] Remark: The translation dictionary
> The equations are those of [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall|§A2.3]] with $x \to \theta$, $v \to \omega$, $a \to \alpha$, and a problem is solved the same way: list the knowns, pick the equation that contains exactly one unknown. As in one dimension, the slope of an $\omega$–$t$ graph is $\alpha$ and the area under it is $\Delta\theta$; this holds for any $\alpha(t)$, not just constant. The two descriptions are linked point by point by $s = r\theta$, $v_t = r\omega$, $a_t = r\alpha$.
>
> | Linear | Angular | Link at radius $r$ |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | $x$ | $\theta$ | $s = r\theta$ |
> | $v = dx/dt$ | $\omega = d\theta/dt$ | $v_t = r\omega$ |
> | $a = dv/dt$ | $\alpha = d\omega/dt$ | $a_t = r\alpha$ |
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.2–10.3, Tables 10.2–10.3*

^rem-a6-1-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.1.1: A Point on a Turning Disc
> A person sits at the rim (radius $R$) of a disc that turns counterclockwise, seen from above, at constant angular velocity $\omega$.
> - Acceleration of the person: not zero. The speed $R\omega$ is constant but the direction of $\vec v$ turns, so $\vec a = \vec a_c$, of magnitude $\omega^2 R$, pointing to the centre ([[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]] with $\alpha = 0$).
> - Angular acceleration: zero, since $\vec\omega$ does not change.
> - Angular velocity: along the axis, pointing **up** (right-hand rule for counterclockwise rotation seen from above).
>
> Now the disc slows down at a constant rate to $\omega/2$ in a time $T$. Then $\alpha = (\omega/2 - \omega)/T = -\omega/(2T)$: magnitude $\omega/(2T)$, with $\vec\alpha$ pointing **down**, opposite to $\vec\omega$. The common slips are to answer "zero" for the acceleration at constant $\omega$ and to give $\vec\alpha$ a radial direction; both linear and angular quantities have to be kept apart.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, Exam 3 (F21), S5–S6*

^ex-a6-1-1

> [!example] Example §A6.1.2: Fishing Reel
> Fishing line unwinds from a reel at radius $r = 4.50\ \mathrm{cm}$. The reel starts from rest and has $\alpha = 110\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}$ for $t = 2.00\ \mathrm s$; counterclockwise is positive. By [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^thm-a6-1-4|Theorem §A6.1.4]],
>
> $$
> \omega = \alpha t = 220\ \mathrm{rad/s}\ (\approx 2100\ \mathrm{rpm}), \qquad \Delta\theta = \tfrac12\alpha t^2 = 220\ \mathrm{rad} = \frac{220}{2\pi}\ \mathrm{rev} = 35.0\ \mathrm{rev},
> $$
>
> and the line paid out is $s = r\Delta\theta = (0.0450\ \mathrm m)(220) = 9.90\ \mathrm m$. A brake then gives $\alpha = -300\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}$; the reel stops after
>
> $$
> t = \frac{0 - \omega_0}{\alpha} = \frac{-220\ \mathrm{rad/s}}{-300\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}} = 0.733\ \mathrm s .
> $$
>
> The negative $\alpha$ against a positive $\omega$ is what makes the reel slow down.
>
> *Source: UP1 Examples 10.4–10.5*

^ex-a6-1-2

> [!example] Example §A6.1.3: Acceleration of a Centrifuge Tip
> A centrifuge of radius $r = 0.200\ \mathrm m$ turning counterclockwise slows uniformly from $10{,}000\ \mathrm{rpm}$ to rest in $30.0\ \mathrm s$. Find the acceleration of the tip at $t = 29.0\ \mathrm s$. Here $\omega_0 = 10^4 \cdot 2\pi/60\ \mathrm{rad/s} = 1047\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, so
>
> $$
> \alpha = \frac{0 - \omega_0}{30.0\ \mathrm s} = -34.9\ \mathrm{rad/s^2}, \qquad \omega(29.0\ \mathrm s) = \omega_0 + \alpha t = \frac{\omega_0}{30} = 34.9\ \mathrm{rad/s}.
> $$
>
> By [[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]], $a_t = r\alpha = -6.98\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ (clockwise, against the rotation) and $a_c = r\omega^2 = (0.200)(34.9)^2\ \mathrm{m/s^2} = 244\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ (inward). Hence
>
> $$
> |\vec a| = \sqrt{a_c^2 + a_t^2} = 244\ \mathrm{m/s^2}, \qquad \text{at } \arctan\frac{6.98}{244} = 1.6^\circ \text{ from the inward radius, toward the clockwise side.}
> $$
>
> Even one second before stopping, the centripetal part dominates by a factor of 35.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 10.7*

^ex-a6-1-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The constant-$\alpha$ equations are the integrals of [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|Theorem §34.1]] applied twice, exactly as for linear motion in [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall|§A2.3]].
> - For a rigid body moving in any way, the velocity of each point is still $\vec v_{\text{ref}} + \vec\omega\times\vec r$ with one angular velocity for the whole body; rolling ([[§A6.5 Rolling|§A6.5]]) is the first case. General rigid-body kinematics is level B (planned).
