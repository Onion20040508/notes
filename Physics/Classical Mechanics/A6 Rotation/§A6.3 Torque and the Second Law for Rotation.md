[[§A6.1 Rotational Kinematics|§A6.1]] described rotation and [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy|§A6.2]] found the rotational counterpart of mass. What changes a rotation is not a force alone but where and in which direction it acts: a door does not open when pushed at its hinges or edge-on. That turning effect is the torque, and Newton's second law, applied to every particle of a rigid body, becomes $\sum\tau = I\alpha$.

## Torque

> [!definition] Definition §A6.3.1: Torque
> A force $\vec F$ applied at a point with position $\vec r$ relative to a point $O$ has **torque** about $O$
>
> $$
> \vec\tau = \vec r \times \vec F, \qquad |\vec\tau| = rF\sin\theta = r_\perp F ,
> $$
>
> where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec r$ and $\vec F$, and $r_\perp = r\sin\theta$, the **lever arm**, is the perpendicular distance from $O$ to the line of action of $\vec F$. The unit is $\mathrm{N\cdot m}$. For forces in the $xy$-plane, $\vec\tau$ is along $\pm\hat k$: positive (counterclockwise) if $\vec r\times\vec F$ points along $+\hat k$, negative (clockwise) if along $-\hat k$. The **net torque** is the sum of the torques of all forces about the same point, with their signs: $\tau_{\text{net}} = \sum_i \tau_i$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.6, eqs. (10.22)–(10.24)*

^def-a6-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: What makes a force turn things
> Pushing a door shows the three factors: a larger force, a point farther from the hinges, and a direction closer to perpendicular to the door all open it faster. All three sit in $r_\perp F$. A force whose line of action passes through the axis ($r_\perp = 0$), such as a push straight toward the hinges or the force of a bearing on its axle, has no torque about that axis however large it is.

^rem-a6-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Torques have signs, and a point
> - Add torques with their signs. (UP1 writes $\tau_{\text{net}} = \sum_i |\tau_i|$, eq. (10.24); the absolute values are a misprint, since its own examples add signed values.)
> - A torque is always about a stated point or axis; the same force has different torques about different points.
> - $\mathrm{N\cdot m}$ for torque is not a joule: torque is not work, and it is a vector.

^cau-a6-3-1

## Newton's second law for rotation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.3.1: Second Law for Rotation
> For a rigid body turning about a fixed axis, the net external torque about the axis equals the moment of inertia about that axis times the angular acceleration:
>
> $$
> \sum_i \tau_i = I\alpha .
> $$
>
> In vector form the axial component of $\sum\vec\tau$ equals $I\alpha$: the net torque along the axis and $\vec\alpha$ point the same way.
>
> *Source: UP1 §10.7, eqs. (10.25)–(10.26)*

^thm-a6-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the axis along $z$ and a particle $m_j$ at perpendicular distance $r_j$ from it. Only the tangential component $F_{j,t}$ of the total force on $m_j$ has a torque about the axis (the radial component points through the axis, the $z$-component is parallel to it), and $r_jF_{j,t}$ is that torque. By Newton's second law in the tangential direction and $a_t = r\alpha$,
>
> $$
> F_{j,t} = m_j a_{j,t} = m_j r_j\alpha \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_jF_{j,t} = m_j r_j^2\,\alpha .
> $$
>
> All particles share $\alpha$. Summing over the body, $\sum_j r_jF_{j,t} = \bigl(\sum_j m_jr_j^2\bigr)\alpha = I\alpha$. The forces on $m_j$ are external forces plus forces from the other particles. Those internal forces come in third-law pairs, equal and opposite and (assumed) along the line joining the two particles, so the two torques of a pair have the same lever arm and opposite signs and cancel. Only the external torques survive.

^der-a6-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Solving rotational dynamics problems
> - Draw the free-body diagram of each body with each force at its **point of application**: for an extended body the point matters.
> - Choose the axis, and use the same axis for $\tau$, $I$ and $\alpha$.
> - Choose a positive sense of rotation, and choose the linear positive directions to match it, so that a rope that does not slip gives $a = R\alpha$ with no stray signs.
> - For several bodies, write $\sum\vec F = m\vec a$ for those that translate, $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ for those that rotate, and the constraints that tie them together; then solve.
> - The weight of an extended body acts, for the purpose of torques, at its centre of mass ([[§A7.1 Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-a7-1-3|Theorem §A7.1.3]]).

^rem-a6-3-2

> [!caution] Caution: A massive pulley has two tensions
> With a frictionless massless pulley the string tension is the same on both sides ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^mod-a3-6-2|Model §A3.6.2]]). A pulley with moment of inertia $I$ can be angularly accelerated only by a net torque, $(T_1 - T_2)R = I\alpha$, so the tensions on the two sides differ. And the tension in a string holding an accelerating weight is not $mg$.

^cau-a6-3-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.3.1: Torque as a Cross Product
> A force $\vec F = (4\hat\imath - \hat\jmath)\ \mathrm N$ acts at $\vec r = (2\hat\imath + 3\hat k)\ \mathrm m$ from the origin. Distribute the product and use $\hat\imath\times\hat\imath = \vec 0$, $\hat\imath\times\hat\jmath = \hat k$, $\hat k\times\hat\imath = \hat\jmath$, $\hat k\times\hat\jmath = -\hat\imath$:
>
> $$
> \vec\tau = (2\hat\imath + 3\hat k)\times(4\hat\imath - \hat\jmath) = 8(\hat\imath\times\hat\imath) - 2\hat k + 12\hat\jmath + 3\hat\imath = (3\hat\imath + 12\hat\jmath - 2\hat k)\ \mathrm{N\cdot m},
> $$
>
> of magnitude $\sqrt{157}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} = 12.5\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}$. Check: $\vec\tau\cdot\vec F = 12 - 12 = 0$ and $\vec\tau\cdot\vec r = 6 - 6 = 0$, as a cross product must be perpendicular to both factors.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 26*

^ex-a6-3-1

> [!example] Example §A6.3.2: The Trapdoor's First Instant
> The trapdoor of [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^ex-a6-2-3|Example §A6.2.3]] (side $L = 1.00\ \mathrm m$, mass $M = 10.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, hinged on one edge) is released from horizontal. Take the hinge as axis. The hinge force has no torque; the weight acts at the centre, lever arm $L/2$. With $I = \tfrac13 ML^2$,
>
> $$
> Mg\,\frac L2 = \tfrac13 ML^2\,\alpha \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = \frac{3g}{2L} = 14.7\ \mathrm{rad/s^2} .
> $$
>
> The free edge starts down with $a_t = L\alpha = \tfrac32 g$, faster than free fall: a coin lying on that edge would be left behind. The centre falls with only $\tfrac L2\alpha = \tfrac34 g$, because the hinge pushes up on the door with $M(g - \tfrac34 g) = \tfrac14 Mg$.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, Final practice problems (F21), Question 2*

^ex-a6-3-2

> [!example] Example §A6.3.3: Weight on a Wheel
> A light wire wrapped around a uniform disc of radius $R$ (axle fixed, frictionless) carries a mass $m$. Released from rest, $m$ falls a distance $L$ in a time $T$. Find the disc's mass $M$. Take "down" positive for $m$ and the matching sense of rotation positive for the disc; let $F_T$ be the tension. Constant acceleration from rest gives $a = 2L/T^2$, and the wire does not slip, so $\alpha = a/R$.
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \text{mass:}&\quad mg - F_T = ma,\\
> \text{disc:}&\quad F_T R = \tfrac12 MR^2\,\frac aR \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_T = \tfrac12 Ma .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> Adding, $mg = (m + \tfrac12 M)a$, so
>
> $$
> M = \frac{2m(g - a)}{a} = m\Bigl(\frac{gT^2}{L} - 2\Bigr).
> $$
>
> The disc acts like an extra inertial mass $\tfrac12 M$ that is not pulled by gravity. If the disc were massless, $a = g$ and $T^2 = 2L/g$, which makes $M = 0$, as it should.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 23*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The torque is a cross product ([[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]), and the rule $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ is the fixed-axis case of $d\vec L/dt = \sum\vec\tau$ ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-3|Theorem §A6.6.3]]).
> - Setting $\sum\tau = 0$ together with $\sum\vec F = \vec 0$ gives the conditions of static equilibrium ([[§A7.1 Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-a7-1-1|Theorem §A7.1.1]]).
