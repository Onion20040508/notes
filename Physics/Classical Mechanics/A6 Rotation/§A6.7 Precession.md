A top that is not spinning falls over; a spinning top, tilted just the same, does not fall but swings its axis slowly around the vertical. Both follow from $d\vec L/dt = \sum\vec\tau$ ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]]). For the resting top the torque of gravity builds up angular momentum from zero, about a horizontal axis. For the spinning top the same torque is added to a large $\vec L$ perpendicular to it, and so it turns $\vec L$ instead of lengthening it.

## Gyroscopes and precession

> [!definition] Definition §A6.7.1: Gyroscope; Precession
> A **gyroscope** is a spinning disc whose axis is free to take any orientation. **Precession** is the motion in which the spin axis of a spinning body sweeps around another axis, here the vertical, because of a torque perpendicular to its angular momentum. The **precession angular velocity** $\omega_P = d\varphi/dt$ is the rate at which the vertical plane containing the spin axis turns.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.4*

^def-a6-7-1

> [!model] Model §A6.7.1: Fast-Spinning Top
> A symmetric top of mass $M$ spins with angular velocity $\omega$ about its own axis, with moment of inertia $I$ about that axis. Its tip rests on a fixed pivot and its centre of mass is on the axis at distance $r$ from the pivot. Assume:
> - the angular momentum lies along the spin axis with $L = I\omega$, the contribution of the slow precession motion being neglected;
> - friction at the pivot is negligible.
>
> *Valid when:* $\omega_P \ll \omega$. *Fails when:* the spin is slow; then the precession adds a noticeable vertical component to $\vec L$, the axis bobs up and down (nutation), and eventually the top falls.
> *Source: UP1 §11.4*

^mod-a6-7-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.7.2: Precession Rate of a Top
> In the fast-top model, a top whose axis makes angle $\theta$ with the vertical precesses about the vertical with angular velocity
>
> $$
> \omega_P = \frac{rMg}{L} = \frac{rMg}{I\omega},
> $$
>
> independent of $\theta$, in the sense of the gravitational torque.
>
> *Source: UP1 §11.4, eq. (11.12)*

^thm-a6-7-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The weight acts at the centre of mass, at $\vec r$ from the pivot along the spin axis. The pivot force has no torque about the pivot, so the net torque is $\vec\tau = \vec r\times M\vec g$, horizontal and perpendicular to the vertical plane containing the axis, of magnitude $rMg\sin\theta$. By [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-2|Theorem §A6.6.2]], in time $dt$
>
> $$
> d\vec L = \vec\tau\,dt ,
> $$
>
> which is perpendicular to $\vec L$ (along the axis). So $\vec L$ keeps its length and its angle $\theta$ with the vertical, and only its horizontal part, of length $L\sin\theta$, turns, through the angle
>
> $$
> d\varphi = \frac{dL}{L\sin\theta} = \frac{rMg\sin\theta\,dt}{L\sin\theta} = \frac{rMg}{L}\,dt .
> $$
>
> Dividing by $dt$ and using $L = I\omega$ gives $\omega_P$.

^der-a6-7-2

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the formula
> - The faster the spin, the slower the precession ($\omega_P \propto 1/\omega$). As friction slows a real top, its precession speeds up until the fast-top model fails and the top falls.
> - $\omega_P$ is proportional to $g$ and does not depend on the tilt: a larger tilt increases the torque ($\propto\sin\theta$) and, in the same proportion, the circle of radius $L\sin\theta$ that the horizontal part of $\vec L$ has to go around.
> - Holding a spinning bicycle wheel by its axle and trying to tilt it, one feels the wheel swing sideways, in the direction of the applied torque, not in the direction of the push. A spinning disc in a box makes the box easy to translate but hard to turn about an axis perpendicular to the spin: that is why gyroscopes hold their direction in spacecraft and other navigation systems.
> - Earth is a gyroscope too: its spin axis, now pointing near Polaris, precesses once in about $26{,}000$ years under the torques of the Sun and Moon on its equatorial bulge.

^rem-a6-7-1

> [!caution] Caution: The top does move, just not down
> A spinning top is not in equilibrium: the net torque on it is not zero. The torque goes into turning $\vec L$, so the axis moves sideways. Setting $\sum\tau = 0$, or using $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ about the pivot, gives nonsense here; the vector equation $d\vec L/dt = \sum\vec\tau$ is needed.

^cau-a6-7-1

## Example

> [!example] Example §A6.7.1: Period of a Precessing Gyroscope
> A gyroscope's disc has mass $M = 0.30\ \mathrm{kg}$ and radius $5.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ and spins at $20\ \mathrm{rev/s}$; its centre of mass is $r = 5.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ from the pivot. Treat the disc as a uniform disc:
>
> $$
> I = \tfrac12 MR^2 = 3.75\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}, \qquad \omega = 20(2\pi)\ \mathrm{rad/s} = 125.7\ \mathrm{rad/s}.
> $$
>
> By [[§A6.7 Precession#^thm-a6-7-2|Theorem §A6.7.2]],
>
> $$
> \omega_P = \frac{rMg}{I\omega} = \frac{(0.050)(0.30)(9.8)}{(3.75\times10^{-4})(125.7)}\ \mathrm{rad/s} = 3.1\ \mathrm{rad/s}, \qquad T_P = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_P} = 2.0\ \mathrm s .
> $$
>
> Since $\omega_P/\omega \approx 0.025$, the fast-top model is justified.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 11.10*
