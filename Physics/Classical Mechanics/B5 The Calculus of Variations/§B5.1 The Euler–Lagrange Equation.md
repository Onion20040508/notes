*Sources: Morin, Introduction to Classical Mechanics, §6.2, §6.8 · Helliwell & Sahakian, Modern Classical Mechanics, §3.1–3.5 · Series Part II (the user's notes), §1, §2.1–2.3, App. A–B.*

Ordinary calculus finds where a function of finitely many variables is stationary by setting its gradient to zero ([[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-1|452 Thm. §14.1]]). Many questions of physics ask instead for a whole curve: the path a light ray takes through a medium of varying index, whose discrete version is Snell's law ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|WO Law §A4.2.4]]); the shape of the fastest wire; and, in Chapter B6, the motion of a mechanical system. This section makes "stationary among curves" precise, proves that it is equivalent to a second-order differential equation, the Euler–Lagrange equation, and derives two first integrals. At level A, energy conservation was a first integral of Newton's law found case by case ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]); here first integrals follow from the form of the integrand alone.

## Functionals and their variation

> [!definition] Definition §B5.1.1: Functional; Admissible Curves
> A **functional** assigns a real number to each function of a given class. The functionals of this chapter have the form
>
> $$
> J[y] = \int_a^b F\bigl(x, y(x), y'(x)\bigr)\,dx ,
> $$
>
> where the **integrand** $F(x, u, w)$ is a $C^2$ function of three real variables and $y$ runs over the **admissible curves** with fixed endpoints,
>
> $$
> \mathcal C = \bigl\{\, y \in C^2([a, b]) : y(a) = y_A,\ y(b) = y_B \,\bigr\} .
> $$
>
> Square brackets, $J[y]$, mark that the argument is a whole function and not a number. With several unknown functions $y_1, \dots, y_n$ the integrand is $F(x, y_1, \dots, y_n, y_1', \dots, y_n')$ and each $y_i$ has fixed end values.
>
> *Source: Series II Convention 1.1, eq. (7) · H&S §3.2, eq. (3.15) · Morin §6.2*

^def-b5-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: A function of infinitely many variables
> Sample $y$ at the points $x_k = a + k\epsilon$ and replace $y'$ by a difference quotient and the integral by a sum: $J \approx \sum_k F\bigl(x_k, y_k, (y_k - y_{k-1})/\epsilon\bigr)\,\epsilon$. This is an ordinary function of the finitely many numbers $y_1, y_2, \dots$, stationary where all its partial derivatives vanish. A functional is the limit $\epsilon \to 0$ of such functions, and the Euler–Lagrange equation below is the limit of the conditions $\partial J/\partial y_k = 0$. The picture is a guide; the proof below works directly with curves.
>
> *Source: H&S §3.2, eqs. (3.18)–(3.21)*

^rem-b5-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B5.1.2: Variation; First Variation; Stationary Curve
> A **test function** is an $\eta \in C^2([a, b])$ with $\eta(a) = \eta(b) = 0$; for every real $\varepsilon$ the curve $y + \varepsilon\eta$ is again admissible. The **first variation** of $J$ at $y$ in the direction $\eta$ is the ordinary derivative
>
> $$
> \delta J[y; \eta] = \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Big|_{\varepsilon = 0} J[y + \varepsilon\eta] .
> $$
>
> An admissible $y$ is **stationary** (an **extremal** of $J$) if $\delta J[y; \eta] = 0$ for every test function $\eta$. In physics notation $\delta y$ stands for $\varepsilon\eta$ to first order, and "$\delta J = 0$" means $\delta J[y; \eta] = 0$ for all $\eta$.
>
> A local minimum or maximum of $J$ among admissible curves is stationary, but a stationary curve may be neither (a saddle); which case occurs is decided by the second variation and is a separate question.
>
> *Source: Series II Conventions 1.2–1.3 · H&S §3.2, eqs. (3.16)–(3.17) · Morin §6.2, eq. (6.16)*

^def-b5-1-2

![[ph-cm-b5-1-1.svg]]
*A stationary curve $y(x)$ between the fixed points $A$ and $B$, and the family $y + \varepsilon\eta$ obtained from one test function $\eta$ (below). Every member of the family has the same endpoints. Along the family, $J[y + \varepsilon\eta]$ is an ordinary function of $\varepsilon$, and stationarity says that its derivative vanishes at $\varepsilon = 0$, for every choice of $\eta$.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.1: First Variation Formula
> Let $y \in C^2([a, b])$ and $\eta \in C^2([a, b])$ (not necessarily zero at the ends). Then
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Big|_{\varepsilon = 0} J[y + \varepsilon\eta] = \int_a^b \Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\Bigr)\,\eta\,dx \;+\; \Bigl[\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\,\eta\Bigr]_a^b .
> $$
>
> Here $\partial F/\partial y$ and $\partial F/\partial y'$ are the partial derivatives of $F(x, u, w)$ in its second and third slots, evaluated at $(x, y(x), y'(x))$, and $d/dx$ is the derivative along the curve. For a test function the boundary term vanishes.
>
> *Source: Series II Theorem 2.1, proof (Moves 1–3) · Morin §6.2, eqs. (6.17)–(6.21) · H&S §3.2, eqs. (3.22)–(3.24)*

^thm-b5-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Reduce to one variable.* Put $\Phi(\varepsilon) = J[y + \varepsilon\eta] = \int_a^b f(x, \varepsilon)\,dx$ with $f(x, \varepsilon) = F(x, y + \varepsilon\eta, y' + \varepsilon\eta')$.
>
> *Differentiate under the integral.* For fixed $x$, the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]) with $u = y + \varepsilon\eta$, $w = y' + \varepsilon\eta'$ gives
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial f}{\partial\varepsilon} = F_u(x, u, w)\,\eta(x) + F_w(x, u, w)\,\eta'(x) .
> $$
>
> Since $F$ is $C^2$ and $y, \eta$ are $C^2$, $f$ and $\partial f/\partial\varepsilon$ are continuous on the compact rectangle $[a, b] \times [-1, 1]$, which is what the Leibniz rule for differentiating under the integral sign needs (Series II, App. A: by the mean value theorem in $\varepsilon$ and uniform continuity); so $\Phi'(\varepsilon) = \int_a^b \partial f/\partial\varepsilon\,dx$. At $\varepsilon = 0$,
>
> $$
> \Phi'(0) = \int_a^b \Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}\,\eta + \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\,\eta'\Bigr)\,dx .
> $$
>
> *Integrate by parts.* Along the curve, $x \mapsto \partial F/\partial y'\bigl(x, y(x), y'(x)\bigr)$ is $C^1$: its derivative is $F_{wx} + F_{wu}\,y' + F_{ww}\,y''$, each term continuous. Integration by parts ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]) gives
>
> $$
> \int_a^b \frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\,\eta'\,dx = \Bigl[\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\,\eta\Bigr]_a^b - \int_a^b \Bigl(\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\Bigr)\eta\,dx ,
> $$
>
> and substituting proves the formula. The boundary term is the only place where the endpoint conditions enter.

^der-b5-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^def-b5-1-1|Def. §B5.1.1]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]; differentiation under the integral sign: Mathematical Methods, planned

> [!theorem] Lemma §B5.1.2: Fundamental Lemma of the Calculus of Variations
> If $g$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $\int_a^b g\,\eta\,dx = 0$ for every test function $\eta$, then $g \equiv 0$ on $[a, b]$.
>
> *Source: Series II §2.2 (Move 4) and App. B, Lemma B.1 · H&S §3.2*

^thm-b5-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Suppose $g(x_0) \ne 0$; replacing $g$ by $-g$ if necessary, $g(x_0) > 0$. By continuity there is an interval $[\alpha, \beta] \subset (a, b)$, $\alpha < \beta$, on which $g \ge c > 0$ (if $x_0$ is an endpoint, pick the interval just inside it). Take the bump
>
> $$
> \eta(x) = \begin{cases} (x - \alpha)^3(\beta - x)^3, & \alpha \le x \le \beta, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Each factor vanishes to third order at its end of the interval, so $\eta$, $\eta'$ and $\eta''$ all tend to $0$ at $\alpha$ and $\beta$ from inside and match the zero function outside: $\eta$ is $C^2$, vanishes at $a$ and $b$, and is positive on $(\alpha, \beta)$. Then $\int_a^b g\,\eta\,dx \ge c\int_\alpha^\beta \eta\,dx > 0$, a contradiction. Test functions are probes: a probe concentrated where $g \ne 0$ detects it.

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]]

## The Euler–Lagrange equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.3: Euler–Lagrange Equation
> An admissible curve $y \in \mathcal C$ is stationary for $J[y] = \int_a^b F(x, y, y')\,dx$ if and only if
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = 0 \qquad \text{for all } x \in [a, b] .
> $$
>
> Written out with the chain rule, this is the second-order differential equation
>
> $$
> F_{y'y'}\,y'' + F_{y'y}\,y' + F_{y'x} - F_y = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Series II Theorem 2.1 and Remark 2.2, eqs. (8), (16) · H&S §3.2, eq. (3.25) ("Euler's equation") · Morin §6.2, Theorem 6.1*

^thm-b5-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $E(x) = \partial F/\partial y - \frac{d}{dx}\,\partial F/\partial y'$ along the curve; the derivation of [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]] showed that $E$ is continuous. For a test function the first variation formula reads $\delta J[y; \eta] = \int_a^b E\,\eta\,dx$.
>
> If $E \equiv 0$, every first variation vanishes and $y$ is stationary. Conversely, if $y$ is stationary then $\int_a^b E\,\eta\,dx = 0$ for every test function, and [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|Lemma §B5.1.2]] gives $E \equiv 0$. The expanded form is the chain rule applied to $x \mapsto F_{y'}(x, y(x), y'(x))$.

^der-b5-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|Lemma §B5.1.2]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: A global question with a local answer
> Stationarity is a condition on the whole curve, posed by comparing integrals over $[a, b]$; the answer is a differential equation that holds at each point separately. The equation is a **boundary-value problem**: its two constants of integration are fixed by the two end values $y(a) = y_A$, $y(b) = y_B$, not by a starting value and slope. Such a problem may have one solution, several, or none ([[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^ex-b5-2-3|Example §B5.2.3]]). And the equation is only a necessary condition for a minimum: a solution is stationary, nothing more.
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 2.2 · Morin §6.8, footnote 11*

^rem-b5-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Partial and total derivatives
> - In $\partial F/\partial y$ and $\partial F/\partial y'$ the three slots of $F(x, u, w)$ are treated as independent; only afterwards is the curve substituted. For $F = x\,y\,y'^2$: $\partial F/\partial y = x\,y'^2$ and $\partial F/\partial y' = 2xyy'$, and the equation is $x\,y'^2 - \frac{d}{dx}(2xyy') = -2yy' - x\,y'^2 - 2xyy'' = 0$.
> - The $d/dx$ is total: it differentiates every appearance of $x$, including those through $y(x)$ and $y'(x)$.
> - Nothing assumes that $y$ and $y'$ vary independently. In the derivation they vary together, $\delta y = \varepsilon\eta$ and $\delta y' = \varepsilon\eta'$; the integration by parts is what converts the second into the first.
>
> *Source: H&S §3.2, eq. (3.30) · Morin §6.2, footnote 7*

^cau-b5-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.4: Euler–Lagrange Equations for Several Functions
> Let $J[y_1, \dots, y_n] = \int_a^b F(x, y_1, \dots, y_n, y_1', \dots, y_n')\,dx$ with $F$ of class $C^2$ and each $y_i \in C^2([a, b])$ with fixed end values. Then $(y_1, \dots, y_n)$ is stationary under all variations $y_i \to y_i + \varepsilon\eta_i$ (test functions $\eta_i$) if and only if
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial F}{\partial y_i} - \frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_i'} = 0, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n .
> $$
>
> For arbitrary $\eta_i$ the first variation is $\sum_i \int_a^b E_i\,\eta_i\,dx + \bigl[\sum_i (\partial F/\partial y_i')\,\eta_i\bigr]_a^b$, with $E_i$ the left-hand sides.
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 2.3, eq. (17) · H&S §3.5, eqs. (3.62)–(3.68) · Morin §6.2*

^thm-b5-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The derivation of [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]] runs unchanged, with the chain rule now summing over all $2n$ slots $y_i$, $y_i'$; this gives the first-variation formula. If all $E_i$ vanish, so does every first variation. Conversely, choose all test functions zero except $\eta_i$: the first variation becomes $\int_a^b E_i\,\eta_i\,dx = 0$ for every $\eta_i$, and [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|Lemma §B5.1.2]] gives $E_i \equiv 0$. Doing this for each $i$ isolates one equation at a time.

^der-b5-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|Lemma §B5.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: The variations must be independent
> The step "choose all $\eta_j$ zero except one" needs the $y_i$ to be free of relations among themselves. If the unknowns are tied by a constraint, such as a curve forced to lie on a surface, the $\eta_i$ cannot be chosen separately and the equations above do not follow. Constraints need multipliers ([[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]]; for mechanics, [[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers]]) or a choice of independent coordinates ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations]]).
>
> *Source: H&S §3.5 (after eq. (3.68)) and §4.3*

^cau-b5-1-2

> [!example] Example §B5.1.1: The Shortest Path in a Plane
> The length of a graph from $(a, y_A)$ to $(b, y_B)$ is $J[y] = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx$. Here $\partial F/\partial y = 0$ and $\partial F/\partial y' = y'/\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$, so the Euler–Lagrange equation says $y'/\sqrt{1 + y'^2} = c$, a constant. Solving, $y'^2 = c^2/(1 - c^2)$: the slope is constant and the extremal is the straight segment through the two points.
>
> In space, a path $(y(x), z(x))$ has $F = \sqrt{1 + y'^2 + z'^2}$ and [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]] gives $y'/\sqrt{1 + y'^2 + z'^2} = k_1$ and $z'/\sqrt{1 + y'^2 + z'^2} = k_2$. Squaring and adding shows that the common denominator is constant, so $y'$ and $z'$ are constant: again a straight line. The machinery returns the known answer, the usual calibration check before harder problems.
>
> *Source: Series II Example 2.4 · H&S Examples 3.3 and 3.6 · Morin Problem 6.20*

## First integrals

The Euler–Lagrange equation is of second order. When the integrand lacks one of its arguments, one integration comes for free.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.5: Cyclic Variable
> If $F$ does not depend on $y_i$ ($\partial F/\partial y_i \equiv 0$), then along every extremal
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial F}{\partial y_i'} = \text{const} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Series II Proposition 2.5 · H&S §3.2 (Example 3.3) · Morin §6.8 (second solution)*

^thm-b5-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\partial F/\partial y_i = 0$, the $i$-th equation of [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]] reads $\frac{d}{dx}\,\partial F/\partial y_i' = 0$.

^der-b5-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.6: Beltrami Identity
> For any $C^2$ curve $(y_1, \dots, y_n)$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl(F - \sum_i y_i'\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_i'}\Bigr) = \frac{\partial F}{\partial x} + \sum_i y_i'\Bigl(\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_i} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_i'}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Hence, if $F$ does not depend explicitly on $x$, then along every extremal
>
> $$
> F - \sum_i y_i'\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_i'} = \text{const} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Series II Proposition 2.6, eqs. (20)–(23) · Morin §6.8, eqs. (6.84), (6.92) · H&S Problem 3.13*

^thm-b5-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the chain rule along the curve, $\dfrac{dF}{dx} = \dfrac{\partial F}{\partial x} + \sum_i \dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y_i}\,y_i' + \sum_i \dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y_i'}\,y_i''$, and by the product rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}\Bigl(y_i'\,\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y_i'}\Bigr) = y_i''\,\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y_i'} + y_i'\,\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y_i'}$. Subtracting, the $y_i''$ terms cancel and the identity remains. On an extremal each bracket vanishes ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]), so the derivative equals $\partial F/\partial x$, which is zero by hypothesis.

^der-b5-1-6

*Uses:* [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]]

> [!caution] Caution: A first integral can admit false solutions
> For one function the identity says $\frac{d}{dx}(F - y'F_{y'}) = F_x + y'E$. A curve that satisfies the first integral with $F_x = 0$ therefore has $y'E = 0$, which allows $E \ne 0$ wherever $y' = 0$. Horizontal lines are the typical impostors. For $F = \sqrt{1 + y'^2}/\sqrt y$ (the brachistochrone of [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]) every line $y = \text{const}$ satisfies $F - y'F_{y'} = 1/\sqrt y = \text{const}$, but $E = F_y = -\tfrac12 y^{-3/2} \ne 0$: it is not an extremal. Solutions of a first integral with stretches of $y' = 0$ must be checked against the Euler–Lagrange equation.

^cau-b5-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: The mechanical meaning
> In Chapter B6 the independent variable is time $t$ and the integrand is the Lagrangian $L(q, \dot q, t)$. A cyclic variable becomes a cyclic coordinate whose generalized momentum $\partial L/\partial\dot q_i$ is conserved ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-1|Theorem §B6.4.1]]), and the Beltrami identity becomes the conservation of the energy function $\sum_i \dot q_i\,\partial L/\partial\dot q_i - L$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^def-b6-4-3|Def. §B6.4.3]]) when $L$ has no explicit $t$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]]). The level-A conservation of mechanical energy ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]) is the simplest case.
>
> *Source: Series II §2.3 (after eq. (23)) and §3.3 · Morin §6.8*

^rem-b5-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Choose the independent variable
> A curve in the plane can be written as $y(x)$ or as $x(y)$, with $ds = \sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx = \sqrt{1 + x'^2}\,dy$. The choice decides which first integral is visible. If the integrand depends on $y$ but not on $x$, then in the form $x(y)$ the unknown $x$ is cyclic and [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-5|Theorem §B5.1.5]] applies directly; in the form $y(x)$ the same information comes from [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-6|Theorem §B5.1.6]]. The form $x(y)$ also handles curves that turn back in $x$, as long as they are locally graphs.
>
> *Source: H&S §3.3–3.4 · Morin §6.8 (second solution)*

^rem-b5-1-4

> [!example] Example §B5.1.2: Light in a Layered Medium
> Take Fermat's principle as given: a light ray between two points takes a path that makes the travel time $\frac1c\int n\,ds$ stationary. In a medium whose index depends only on height, $n = n(y)$, write the ray as $x(y)$:
>
> $$
> t[x] = \frac1c\int n(y)\sqrt{1 + x'^2}\,dy .
> $$
>
> The unknown $x$ is cyclic, so by [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-5|Theorem §B5.1.5]]
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial F}{\partial x'} = \frac{n(y)\,x'}{\sqrt{1 + x'^2}} = n(y)\sin\theta(y) = \text{const},
> $$
>
> where $\theta$ is the angle of the ray to the vertical (the normal to the layers), since $x' = \tan\theta$. This is Snell's law ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|WO Law §A4.2.4]]) in continuous form: a stack of thin uniform layers obeys $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2 = \cdots$ at each interface, and the stationarity condition is its limit. The angle at the ground follows from the indices at the two ends alone, $n_a\sin\theta_a = n_b\sin\theta_b$, whatever the profile $n(y)$ in between: this is how atmospheric refraction displaces the apparent position of a star.
>
> *Source: H&S §3.1 and Example 3.5 · Series II §2.1*

^ex-b5-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - A stationary curve is the infinite-dimensional analogue of a critical point, $\nabla f = \mathbf 0$ ([[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-1|452 Thm. §14.1]]); the first variation is the directional derivative of $J$ along $\eta$.
> - The derivation uses the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]) and integration by parts ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]); differentiation under the integral sign and the fundamental lemma are proved in Series II, App. A–B (Mathematical Methods, planned).
> - With constraints: the isoperimetric multiplier rule, [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]], the analogue of [[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-2|452 Thm. §14.2]].
> - In mechanics, with $x \to t$ and $F \to L$: Hamilton's principle, [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action]].
