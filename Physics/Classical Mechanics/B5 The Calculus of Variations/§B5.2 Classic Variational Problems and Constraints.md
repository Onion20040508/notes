The two problems that started the calculus of variations, the fastest wire (brachistochrone) and the shape of a hanging chain (catenary), are solved here with the tools of [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation]]. Both need a level-A ingredient: the bead's speed comes from energy conservation ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]), and the chain hangs at a minimum of its potential energy ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-1|Theorem §A4.4.1]]). The chain adds something new, a constraint that is itself an integral (fixed length); it is handled by a Lagrange multiplier, as in finite dimensions ([[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-2|452 Thm. §14.2]]), and the multiplier turns out to be a force. Along the way: geodesics, and the soap film between two rings.

## Integrands built on arc length

All the problems of this section minimise an integral of the form $\int f\,ds$ along a curve, with a weight $f$ that depends on height only: travel time ($f = 1/v$), potential energy of a chain ($f \propto y$), area of a surface of revolution ($f \propto y$), optical path ($f = n$).

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.1: Extremals of a Height-Weighted Length
> Let $f$ be a $C^2$ function and $J[y] = \int_a^b f(y)\sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx$. Every extremal satisfies
>
> $$
> \frac{f(y)}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = k \quad\text{for a constant } k, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad 1 + y'^2 = B\,f(y)^2 ,\ B = 1/k^2 .
> $$
>
> Conversely, a $C^2$ solution of this first-order equation with $y' \ne 0$ except at isolated points is an extremal.
>
> *Source: Morin §6.8, Lemma 6.5, eq. (6.86) · Series II §2.4–2.5*

^thm-b5-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The integrand $F = f(y)\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$ has no explicit $x$, and $\partial F/\partial y' = f\,y'/\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$, so the Beltrami quantity is
>
> $$
> F - y'\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = f\sqrt{1 + y'^2} - \frac{f\,y'^2}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = \frac{f}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} ,
> $$
>
> constant on extremals by [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-6|Theorem §B5.1.6]]. For the converse, the same theorem gives $y'E = 0$ for the Euler–Lagrange expression $E$; so $E = 0$ wherever $y' \ne 0$, and by continuity at the isolated points too ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^cau-b5-1-3|Caution: A first integral can admit false solutions]] shows why the proviso is needed).

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-6|Theorem §B5.1.6]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]

## The brachistochrone

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.2: The Brachistochrone Is a Cycloid
> A bead slides without friction along a wire from $A$ to a lower point $B$ under uniform gravity $g$, released from rest at $A$. Put $A$ at the origin, measure $y$ downward, and let $B = (x_B, y_B)$ with $x_B, y_B > 0$. The travel time along a wire $y(x)$ is
>
> $$
> T[y] = \int_0^{x_B} \frac{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}}{\sqrt{2gy}}\,dx ,
> $$
>
> and its extremal through $B$ is the cycloid
>
> $$
> x = a(\theta - \sin\theta), \qquad y = a(1 - \cos\theta), \qquad 0 \le \theta \le \theta_B ,
> $$
>
> traced by a point on the rim of a circle of radius $a$ rolling along the underside of the line $y = 0$. The parameters are fixed by $B$: $\theta_B$ is the unique root in $(0, 2\pi)$ of $(\theta - \sin\theta)/(1 - \cos\theta) = x_B/y_B$, and $a = y_B/(1 - \cos\theta_B)$. The descent time is
>
> $$
> T = \theta_B\sqrt{a/g} .
> $$
>
> The bead starts straight down (the cycloid has a vertical tangent at $A$); if $x_B/y_B > \pi/2$, then $\theta_B > \pi$ and the bead dips below $B$ and rises to it.
>
> *Source: Series II §2.1, eq. (5); §2.4, eqs. (24)–(30); Remark 2.7(i) · Morin Problem 6.24 · H&S §3.4, eqs. (3.43)–(3.55)*

^thm-b5-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The functional.* The wire is frictionless and its normal force is perpendicular to the motion, so it does no work; mechanical energy is conserved ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]) and, from rest at depth $0$, $\tfrac12 mv^2 = mgy$ ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-1|Theorem §A4.4.1]]), so $v = \sqrt{2gy}$. Time is $\int ds/v$ with $ds = \sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx$.
>
> *First integral.* This is [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]] with $f = 1/\sqrt{2gy}$, so $\sqrt y\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$ is constant; call the constant $\sqrt{2a}$:
>
> $$
> y\,(1 + y'^2) = 2a .
> $$
>
> *Integration.* Since $y \le 2a$, substitute $y = a(1 - \cos\theta) = 2a\sin^2(\theta/2)$. Then $y'^2 = (2a - y)/y = \cot^2(\theta/2)$, and on the descending part ($0 < \theta < \pi$) $y' = \cot(\theta/2)$. With $dy = a\sin\theta\,d\theta$,
>
> $$
> dx = \frac{dy}{y'} = a\sin\theta\,\tan\tfrac\theta2\,d\theta = 2a\sin^2\tfrac\theta2\,d\theta = a(1 - \cos\theta)\,d\theta ,
> $$
>
> and $x = a(\theta - \sin\theta)$, the constant fixed by $x = 0$ at $\theta = 0$. The same parametrisation continues through $\theta = \pi$ (lowest point) to the rising branch, where $y' = \cot(\theta/2) < 0$; one checks directly that it satisfies $y(1 + y'^2) = 2a$ for all $0 < \theta < 2\pi$, with $y' = \sin\theta/(1 - \cos\theta)$. Since $y' \ne 0$ except at $\theta = \pi$, the converse in [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]] makes it an extremal.
>
> *Fitting $B$.* The ratio $h(\theta) = (\theta - \sin\theta)/(1 - \cos\theta)$ rises from $0$ (as $\theta \to 0$, $h \approx \theta/3$) to $\infty$ (as $\theta \to 2\pi$), and it is strictly increasing: the numerator of $h'$ is $2 - 2\cos\theta - \theta\sin\theta = 4\sin\tfrac\theta2\bigl(\sin\tfrac\theta2 - \tfrac\theta2\cos\tfrac\theta2\bigr) > 0$ on $(0, 2\pi)$. So $h(\theta_B) = x_B/y_B$ has exactly one root, and $h(\pi) = \pi/2$ separates the two cases.
>
> *Time.* Along the cycloid $ds = a\sqrt{(1 - \cos\theta)^2 + \sin^2\theta}\,d\theta = 2a\sin\tfrac\theta2\,d\theta$ and $v = \sqrt{2ga(1 - \cos\theta)} = 2\sqrt{ga}\,\sin\tfrac\theta2$, so $dT = ds/v = \sqrt{a/g}\,d\theta$: equal times for equal steps of the rolling angle. At $A$, $dx/dy = \tan(\theta/2) \to 0$: the tangent is vertical.

^der-b5-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]], [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-1|Theorem §A4.4.1]], [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Bernoulli's solution by optics
> Johann Bernoulli solved the problem in 1697, before the Euler–Lagrange equation existed, by a translation into optics. The bead's speed $\sqrt{2gy}$ depends only on depth, like light in a layered medium with index $n \propto 1/v$. So the fastest wire is the path of a light ray in that medium, and the continuous Snell law $n\sin\theta = \text{const}$ ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^ex-b5-1-2|Example §B5.1.2]]) is a ready-made first integral. With $\sin\theta = 1/\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$ for the angle to the vertical, it reads $\sqrt{y(1 + y'^2)} = \text{const}$, the first integral above. In the language of this chapter the optical and the mechanical problems are one functional.
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 2.8 · H&S §3.4 (footnote on the history)*

^rem-b5-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.3: The Cycloid Is a Tautochrone
> Turn the cycloid of [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]] into a frictionless track, cusps up, with its lowest point at $\theta = \pi$. A bead released from rest anywhere on it reaches the bottom after the same time $\pi\sqrt{a/g}$, and it oscillates about the bottom with period $4\pi\sqrt{a/g}$ whatever the amplitude.
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 2.7(ii) (stated without proof) · H&S Problem 3.15*

^thm-b5-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Measure arc length $s$ along the track from the bottom. With $ds = 2a\sin(\theta/2)\,d\theta$ (derivation of [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]), $s = \int_\theta^\pi 2a\sin(\varphi/2)\,d\varphi = 4a\cos(\theta/2)$. The height above the bottom is $2a - y = a(1 + \cos\theta) = 2a\cos^2(\theta/2) = s^2/8a$. Energy conservation ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]) gives $\tfrac12 m\dot s^2 + mg\,s^2/8a = \text{const}$, and differentiating, $\ddot s = -(g/4a)\,s$. This is simple harmonic motion in $s$ ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|WO Theorem §A1.1.2]]) with $\omega = \sqrt{g/4a}$ for every amplitude. From rest the bead reaches $s = 0$ after a quarter period, $\pi/2\omega = \pi\sqrt{a/g}$. Released at the cusp ($\theta = 0$) this is the brachistochrone time with $\theta_B = \pi$, as it must be.

^der-b5-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]], [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]], [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|WO Theorem §A1.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why it matters for clocks
> A simple pendulum is only approximately isochronous: its period grows with amplitude. Huygens built pendulum clocks whose bob was made to move along a cycloid, which by [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]] keeps time at any amplitude. The potential energy along the cycloid is exactly quadratic in arc length, $mg\,s^2/8a$; on a circle it is quadratic only for small swings.
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 2.7(ii) · Morin Problem 6.29 (cycloidal pendulum)*

^rem-b5-2-2

> [!example] Example §B5.2.1: Cycloid, Straight Line and Circle
> Take $B = (\pi a, 2a)$, so $x_B/y_B = \pi/2$ and $\theta_B = \pi$: $B$ is the lowest point of the cycloid.
> - *Cycloid:* $T = \pi\sqrt{a/g} = 3.142\sqrt{a/g}$, along a path of length $\int_0^\pi 2a\sin(\theta/2)\,d\theta = 4a$.
> - *Straight line:* length $\ell = a\sqrt{\pi^2 + 4} = 3.724a$, constant acceleration $g\,(2a/\ell)$ along it from rest, so $\ell = \tfrac12 g(2a/\ell)T^2$ and $T = \ell/\sqrt{ga} = \sqrt{\pi^2 + 4}\,\sqrt{a/g} = 3.724\sqrt{a/g}$, a factor $\sqrt{\pi^2 + 4}/\pi = 1.185$ slower.
> - *Circular arc* through $A$ and $B$ with a vertical tangent at $A$ (centre on the line $y = 0$, radius $R = a(\pi^2 + 4)/2\pi = 2.207a$): numerical integration of $\int ds/\sqrt{2gy}$ gives $T = 3.221\sqrt{a/g}$.
>
> The straight line is shortest and slowest: it wastes the start, where the bead is slow. The circle starts vertically too, but overshoots in depth and must climb back. The cycloid trades length for early speed in exactly the right proportion.
>
> *Source: Series II §2.4, Fig. 1 (same endpoints; times recomputed here) · H&S Problem 3.19*

## Geodesics and minimal surfaces

> [!example] Example §B5.2.2: Geodesics on a Sphere
> On a sphere of radius $R$ with polar angle $\theta$ and azimuth $\varphi$, $ds^2 = R^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$. Describe a path by $\varphi(\theta)$; its length is $R\int\sqrt{1 + \sin^2\theta\,\varphi'^2}\,d\theta$, in which $\varphi$ is cyclic. By [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-5|Theorem §B5.1.5]]
>
> $$
> \frac{\sin^2\theta\,\varphi'}{\sqrt{1 + \sin^2\theta\,\varphi'^2}} = k \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \varphi' = \frac{k}{\sin\theta\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - k^2}} .
> $$
>
> With $u = \cot\theta$ ($du = -d\theta/\sin^2\theta$) and $\sin^2\theta - k^2 = \sin^2\theta\,\bigl(1 - k^2 - k^2u^2\bigr)$, this becomes $d\varphi = -du/\sqrt{\alpha^2 - u^2}$ with $\alpha^2 = (1 - k^2)/k^2$, so $\cot\theta = \alpha\cos(\varphi - \varphi_0)$. Multiplying by $R\sin\theta$ and using $x = R\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, $y = R\sin\theta\sin\varphi$, $z = R\cos\theta$,
>
> $$
> z = \alpha\,(x\cos\varphi_0 + y\sin\varphi_0) ,
> $$
>
> a plane through the centre. The extremals are arcs of **great circles**. (The choice $\varphi(\theta)$ misses the meridians $\varphi = \text{const}$, which are great circles too: the case $k = 0$.) Through two points that are not antipodal there pass two great-circle arcs. The shorter is a minimum; the longer is stationary but a saddle: wiggling it lengthens it, while pulling it sideways off the great circle shortens it. A cylinder can be unrolled into a plane without changing lengths, so its geodesics are the images of straight lines: helices, circles and generators.
>
> *Source: H&S §3.3, Example 3.4, Fig. 3.6; Problems 3.11, 3.18*

^ex-b5-2-2

> [!example] Example §B5.2.3: A Soap Film between Two Rings
> A soap film spans two coaxial rings of radius $r$ a distance $2\ell$ apart, and its surface tension minimises its area. For a surface of revolution generated by $y(x)$, the area is $\int 2\pi y\sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx$, and [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]] with $f = y$ gives $1 + y'^2 = b^2y^2$, solved by
>
> $$
> y = \frac1b\cosh\bigl(b(x - x_0)\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> a **catenoid**. By symmetry $x_0 = 0$ (rings at $x = \pm\ell$), and the boundary condition is $r = \cosh(b\ell)/b$. With $z = b\ell$ this reads $r/\ell = \cosh z/z$. The function $\cosh z/z$ has a minimum $1.509$ at $z\tanh z = 1$, $z = 1.200$. Hence:
> - for $\ell/r < 1/1.509 = 0.663$ there are two catenoids; the shallower one is a local minimum of the area, the deeper one a saddle;
> - for $\ell/r > 0.663$ there is none: pulled that far apart, the film collapses towards two flat disks joined by a line, which is not a smooth surface of revolution.
>
> At the limit the waist radius is $y(0) = r/\cosh z = 0.55\,r$. This is the boundary-value problem of [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^rem-b5-1-2|Remark: A global question with a local answer]] failing to have a solution.
>
> *Source: Morin §6.8 (minimal surface of revolution), Problem 6.23 and its solution*

## Constraints and the catenary

> [!definition] Definition §B5.2.1: Isoperimetric Problem
> An **isoperimetric problem** asks for the stationary curves of $J[y] = \int_a^b F(x, y, y')\,dx$ among admissible curves ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^def-b5-1-1|Def. §B5.1.1]]) that also satisfy a constraint of the same kind,
>
> $$
> \Lambda[y] = \int_a^b G(x, y, y')\,dx = \ell ,
> $$
>
> with $G$ of class $C^2$ and $\ell$ a given number. A curve $y_*$ with $\Lambda[y_*] = \ell$ is **stationary under the constraint** if $\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}J[y_\varepsilon] = 0$ at $\varepsilon = 0$ for every one-parameter family of admissible curves $y_\varepsilon$ through $y_\varepsilon|_{\varepsilon = 0} = y_*$ that keeps $\Lambda[y_\varepsilon] = \ell$ and depends smoothly on $\varepsilon$.
>
> *Source: Series II §2.5 (opening) and Theorem 2.9*

^def-b5-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.4: Isoperimetric Multiplier Rule
> Let $y_*$ be stationary for $J$ under the constraint $\Lambda[y] = \ell$, and suppose $y_*$ is not an extremal of $\Lambda$ itself. Then there is a constant $\lambda$ such that $y_*$ satisfies the Euler–Lagrange equation of the combined integrand
>
> $$
> \tilde F = F + \lambda G : \qquad \frac{\partial\tilde F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial\tilde F}{\partial y'} = 0 .
> $$
>
> The solution of this equation, with its two integration constants and $\lambda$, is fixed by the two end values and the constraint $\Lambda[y] = \ell$.
>
> *Source: Series II Theorem 2.9 and App. D*

^thm-b5-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $A[\eta] = \delta J[y_*; \eta]$ and $B[\eta] = \delta\Lambda[y_*; \eta]$; by [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]] both are integrals $\int_a^b E_F\,\eta\,dx$ and $\int_a^b E_G\,\eta\,dx$ of the Euler–Lagrange expressions of $F$ and $G$ against $\eta$.
>
> Since $y_*$ is not an extremal of $\Lambda$, $E_G \not\equiv 0$, and by [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|Lemma §B5.1.2]] there is a test function $\eta_2$ with $B[\eta_2] \ne 0$; scale it so that $B[\eta_2] = 1$. Fix any test function $\eta_1$ and consider the two-parameter family $y_* + \varepsilon_1\eta_1 + \varepsilon_2\eta_2$ and
>
> $$
> \psi(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2) = \Lambda[y_* + \varepsilon_1\eta_1 + \varepsilon_2\eta_2] - \ell .
> $$
>
> Then $\psi(0, 0) = 0$, $\psi$ has continuous partial derivatives (differentiation under the integral, as in [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]]), $\partial\psi/\partial\varepsilon_1(0, 0) = B[\eta_1]$ and $\partial\psi/\partial\varepsilon_2(0, 0) = 1 \ne 0$. By the implicit function theorem ([[§12 The Implicit Function Theorem#^thm-12-1|452 Thm. §12.1]]) there is a differentiable $e(\varepsilon_1)$ with $e(0) = 0$, $\psi(\varepsilon_1, e(\varepsilon_1)) = 0$ and $e'(0) = -B[\eta_1]$. So $\varepsilon_1 \mapsto y_* + \varepsilon_1\eta_1 + e(\varepsilon_1)\eta_2$ is a family that respects the constraint, and stationarity with the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]) gives
>
> $$
> 0 = A[\eta_1] + A[\eta_2]\,e'(0) = A[\eta_1] - A[\eta_2]\,B[\eta_1] .
> $$
>
> Put $\lambda = -A[\eta_2]$, a single number fixed by $\eta_2$ and independent of $\eta_1$. Then $A[\eta_1] + \lambda B[\eta_1] = \int_a^b (E_F + \lambda E_G)\,\eta_1\,dx = 0$ for every test function $\eta_1$, and [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|Lemma §B5.1.2]] gives $E_F + \lambda E_G = 0$, which is the Euler–Lagrange equation of $F + \lambda G$.

^der-b5-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-1|Theorem §B5.1.1]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-2|Lemma §B5.1.2]], [[§12 The Implicit Function Theorem#^thm-12-1|452 Thm. §12.1]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The finite-dimensional picture
> On a constraint surface $g = 0$, a constrained extremum of $f$ has $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$ ([[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-2|452 Thm. §14.2]]): $f$ cannot change to first order along any direction that keeps $g$ fixed, so $\nabla f$ is normal to the surface, like $\nabla g$. The proof above is the same argument in function space, with $\eta_2$ the direction that repairs the constraint. The hypothesis that $y_*$ is not an extremal of $\Lambda$ is the analogue of $\nabla g \ne 0$.
>
> *Source: Series II §2.5 (after Theorem 2.9)*

^rem-b5-2-3

> [!caution] Caution: The degenerate case
> If the chain below is exactly as long as the distance between its supports, the only admissible curve is the taut straight segment. That segment is an extremal of the length $\Lambda$, the hypothesis of [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]] fails, and indeed no catenary passes through the supports with that length. A constraint set with no room to move gives no multiplier equation.

^cau-b5-2-1

> [!model] Model §B5.2.5: Hanging Chain
> A chain of length $\ell$ hangs at rest between two supports at $x = a$ and $x = b$ (with $\ell$ greater than the distance between them), in uniform gravity $g$, with height $y(x)$ measured upward. Assumptions:
> - the chain is uniform, with mass $\mu$ per unit length, perfectly flexible and inextensible;
> - its shape is a graph $y(x)$ of class $C^2$;
> - it rests in the shape of least gravitational potential energy compatible with its length (a stable equilibrium sits at a minimum of the potential energy, [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|Theorem §A4.7.2]]).
>
> So the shape makes $U[y] = \mu g\int_a^b y\sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx$ stationary ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-1|Theorem §A4.4.1]] for each element $dm = \mu\,ds$) under the constraint $\int_a^b\sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx = \ell$.
>
> *Source: Series II §2.1, eq. (6); §2.5*

^mod-b5-2-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.6: The Catenary
> The equilibrium shape of the hanging chain ([[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^mod-b5-2-5|Model §B5.2.5]]) is a **catenary**,
>
> $$
> y(x) = C\cosh\frac{x - x_0}{C} - \lambda ,
> $$
>
> with the three constants $C > 0$, $x_0$ and $\lambda$ fixed by the two support points and the length $\ell$. Its lowest point is at $x_0$, at height $C - \lambda$.
>
> *Source: Series II §2.5, eqs. (33)–(37) · Morin §6.8, eq. (6.78) (same curve for the soap film)*

^thm-b5-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The chain is not straight (it is longer than the distance between the supports), so it is not an extremal of the length, and [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]] applies: for some constant $\lambda'$ the shape is an extremal of $\int(\mu g\,y + \lambda')\sqrt{1 + y'^2}\,dx$. Divide by $\mu g$ and write $\lambda = \lambda'/\mu g$: the integrand is $(y + \lambda)\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$, of the form of [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-1|Theorem §B5.2.1]] with $f = y + \lambda$. So
>
> $$
> \frac{y + \lambda}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = C , \qquad y' = \pm\sqrt{\frac{(y + \lambda)^2}{C^2} - 1} .
> $$
>
> ($C > 0$, since $y + \lambda > 0$ along a hanging chain, as the tension remark below shows; the horizontal line $y + \lambda = C$ solves the first integral but not the Euler–Lagrange equation, [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^cau-b5-1-3|Caution: A first integral can admit false solutions]].) Substitute $y + \lambda = C\cosh u$: then $y' = \sinh u$ (sign absorbed into the sign of $u$) and $dy = C\sinh u\,du$, so $C\sinh u\,du/dx = \sinh u$, $du/dx = 1/C$ and $u = (x - x_0)/C$.

> [!remark] Remark: The multiplier is a tension
> Compare with statics. The chain's tension $T$ acts along it; on an element between $x$ and $x + dx$ there are no horizontal external forces, so the horizontal component $H = T\cos\alpha$ is the same everywhere, and the vertical balance ([[§A7.1 Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-a7-1-1|Theorem §A7.1.1]]) is $d(H\,y') = \mu g\,ds$, that is $H\,y'' = \mu g\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$. The catenary $y + \lambda = C\cosh((x - x_0)/C)$ has $y'' = \cosh((x - x_0)/C)/C = \sqrt{1 + y'^2}/C$, so statics and the variational solution agree exactly when
>
> $$
> C = \frac{H}{\mu g} .
> $$
>
> The tension is then $T = H/\cos\alpha = H\sqrt{1 + y'^2} = \mu g\,(y + \lambda)$: at each point it equals the weight of a piece of chain as long as the height of that point above the line $y = -\lambda$. In particular the multiplier $\lambda' = \mu g\lambda$ is the tension the chain has, or would have, at height $y = 0$. The multiplier, introduced as bookkeeping for the length constraint, is the force that enforces it, a first instance of the general rule of [[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers]].
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 2.10 (C = H/μg; the tension formula is derived here)*

> [!remark] Remark: Galileo's parabola
> Galileo guessed that a hanging chain forms a parabola. Near its lowest point the catenary is $y - y_{\min} = C(\cosh(x/C) - 1) = x^2/2C + x^4/24C^3 + \cdots$ (with $x_0 = 0$), so it agrees to second order with the parabola $x^2/2C$ of the same curvature, but that parabola misses the supports. The parabola through the same lowest point and the same supports is more sharply curved at the bottom and lies above the catenary in between (figure). For a shallow chain, $ds \approx dx$, the load is nearly uniform per horizontal length, and $H\,y'' = \mu g$ gives exactly a parabola: Galileo's guess is the small-sag limit.
>
> *Source: Series II §2.5, Fig. 2 (redrawn and corrected here)*

> [!remark] Remark: Constraints that hold at every point
> The length of a chain is one number, so it brings one constant multiplier. A constraint that must hold at every point of the curve, such as a path forced to lie on a surface $g(x, y, z) = 0$, is a separate condition for each $x$, and it brings a multiplier function $\lambda(x)$: the Euler–Lagrange equations of $F$ acquire terms $\lambda(x)\,\partial g/\partial y_i$. In mechanics the curve is a motion, the constraint holds at every instant, and $\lambda(t)$ is the constraint force: [[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers]].
>
