Choosing coordinates that respect the constraints ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations]]) makes the constraint forces disappear, and with them any way to compute them. Often they are wanted: when does a sliding particle leave a curved surface, where the normal force ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-1|Def. §A3.6.1]]) vanishes? Does a string stay taut, with positive tension ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-2|Def. §A3.6.2]])? At level A such forces were found case by case from the components of Newton's law perpendicular to the motion, as in circular motion ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^thm-a3-9-1|Theorem §A3.9.1]]). Here they come systematically: keep more coordinates than there are degrees of freedom, and impose the constraints with Lagrange multipliers. The multiplier of [[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]] was a constant, the tension of a chain; a constraint that holds at every instant brings a multiplier function $\lambda(t)$, and it is again a force.

## Multipliers are constraint forces

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.1: Lagrange's Equations with Multipliers
> Describe a system of particles by $N$ coordinates $q_1, \dots, q_N$, $\vec r_a = \vec r_a(q, t)$, which do not yet respect $m$ holonomic constraints
>
> $$
> f_\alpha(q, t) = 0, \qquad \alpha = 1, \dots, m ,
> $$
>
> whose gradients $\partial f_\alpha/\partial q$ are linearly independent. Suppose the constraints are ideal ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^mod-b6-1-1|Model §B6.1.1]]) and the applied forces come from a potential, with Lagrangian $L = T - V$ written in all $N$ coordinates. Then along every Newtonian motion there are functions $\lambda_1(t), \dots, \lambda_m(t)$ with
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = \sum_{\alpha = 1}^m \lambda_\alpha\,\frac{\partial f_\alpha}{\partial q_i}, \quad i = 1, \dots, N; \qquad f_\alpha(q, t) = 0, \quad \alpha = 1, \dots, m .
> $$
>
> These are $N + m$ equations for the $N + m$ unknown functions $q_i(t)$ and $\lambda_\alpha(t)$. The right-hand sides are the **generalized constraint forces** $Q_i^{\text{c}} = \sum_a \vec C_a\cdot\partial\vec r_a/\partial q_i$; when the $q$'s are Cartesian coordinates of the particles, the constraint force on particle $a$ is
>
> $$
> \vec C_a = \sum_\alpha \lambda_\alpha\,\nabla_a f_\alpha .
> $$
>
> *Source: H&S §6.1, eqs. (6.1)–(6.14) · Morin §6.3, Remark 2, eq. (6.36) · Series II §3.2 (Recipe)*

^thm-b6-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The constraint force is a combination of gradients.* Work first in Cartesian coordinates, $X = (\vec r_1, \dots, \vec r_N) \in \mathbb R^{3N}$, and stack the constraint forces into $C = (\vec C_1, \dots, \vec C_N)$. At each instant the virtual displacements are the vectors tangent to the constraint set, the null space of the $m \times 3N$ matrix $Df$ whose rows are the gradients $\nabla f_\alpha$. The model says that $C$ is orthogonal to every virtual displacement: $C \in (\operatorname{null} Df)^\perp = \operatorname{range}(Df)^{\mathsf T}$ ([[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-6|LADR Thm. 7.6]](b)), the span of the gradients. So $C = \sum_\alpha \lambda_\alpha\nabla f_\alpha$, with unique coefficients because the gradients are independent.
>
> *Projection onto any coordinates.* For general coordinates $q$ the kinematic identity in the derivation of [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]], $\sum_a m_a\ddot{\vec r}_a\cdot\partial\vec r_a/\partial q_i = \frac{d}{dt}\,\partial T/\partial\dot q_i - \partial T/\partial q_i$, uses only [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]] and holds whether or not the $q$'s respect the constraints. Dot Newton's law $m_a\ddot{\vec r}_a = -\nabla_aV + \vec C_a$ with $\partial\vec r_a/\partial q_i$ and sum: the potential gives $-\partial V/\partial q_i$, and the constraint forces give, by the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]),
>
> $$
> \sum_a \vec C_a\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_i} = \sum_\alpha \lambda_\alpha\sum_a \nabla_af_\alpha\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_i} = \sum_\alpha \lambda_\alpha\,\frac{\partial f_\alpha}{\partial q_i} .
> $$
>
> Collecting terms gives the equations, and the motion satisfies the constraints by assumption.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.3.2: Multipliers from Hamilton's Principle
> The equations of [[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]] are the Euler–Lagrange equations of the extended Lagrangian
>
> $$
> L' = L + \sum_\alpha \lambda_\alpha(t)\,f_\alpha(q, t)
> $$
>
> in the $N + m$ coordinates $(q_1, \dots, q_N, \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m)$, all varied independently: the $\lambda_\alpha$ equations return the constraints, the $q_i$ equations the force balance.
>
> *Source: H&S §6.1, eqs. (6.4)–(6.9) · Morin §6.3, Remark 2 ("basically the method of Lagrange multipliers")*

^thm-b6-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $L'$ contains no $\dot\lambda_\alpha$, so the $\lambda_\alpha$ equation of [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-2|Theorem §B6.2.2]] is $-\partial L'/\partial\lambda_\alpha = -f_\alpha = 0$. The added terms contain no $\dot q$, so the $q_i$ equation is $\frac{d}{dt}\,\partial L/\partial\dot q_i - \partial L/\partial q_i - \sum_\alpha \lambda_\alpha\,\partial f_\alpha/\partial q_i = 0$.

^der-b6-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-2|Theorem §B6.2.2]], [[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Three views of one multiplier
> - *Geometric.* At each instant the constraint force is normal to the allowed set, so it lies in the span of the gradients $\nabla f_\alpha$, exactly like $\nabla f = \sum\lambda_\alpha\nabla g_\alpha$ at a constrained extremum ([[§14 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-14-3|452 Thm. §14.3]]). The multipliers are the coefficients.
> - *Variational.* A constraint that holds at every instant is an infinite family of conditions, one per $t$, so it needs a multiplier function $\lambda(t)$, where the single length condition of the catenary needed one constant ([[§B5.2 Classic Variational Problems and Constraints#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]]).
> - *Physical.* A real surface yields a little: the particle sinks in until the surface pushes back. Model this by a very steep potential $V_{\text{c}}(\eta)$ of the distance $\eta$ from the ideal surface. Lagrange's equations with $V_{\text{c}}$ included are those of the theorem with $\lambda = -V_{\text{c}}'(\eta)$, the restoring force; letting the potential become infinitely steep fixes $\eta = 0$ while $-V_{\text{c}}'$ stays finite. The multiplier is the limit of an ordinary force.
>
> *Source: Morin §6.3 (eqs. (6.29)–(6.32)), Remarks 2–3 · H&S §6.1 · Series II §3.2*

^rem-b6-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Scale of the constraint function
> The force $\sum_\alpha \lambda_\alpha\nabla f_\alpha$ does not depend on how a constraint is written: replacing $f$ by $c f$ replaces $\lambda$ by $\lambda/c$. But $\lambda$ alone is the magnitude of the force only when $|\nabla f| = 1$, that is, when $f$ measures the distance from the allowed surface, as $f = r - R$ does. Writing the hemisphere constraint as $7(r - R) = 0$ makes $\lambda$ one seventh of the normal force, and writing it as $y - \sqrt{R^2 - x^2} = 0$ gives a gradient of length $R/\sqrt{R^2 - x^2} \ne 1$. Choose one coordinate to be the distance from where the particle should be, or compute $\lambda\nabla f$.
>
> *Source: Morin §6.3, Remark 3*

^cau-b6-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: One-sided constraints
> A surface can push but not pull; a string can pull but not push. The equations assume the constraint holds; they return a force of either sign. The answer is physical only while the force has the allowed sign. Where the computed normal force or tension passes through zero, the constraint stops acting, the system gains a degree of freedom, and the equations must be set up anew without it.
>
> *Source: Morin §6.3 (after eq. (6.32)) · H&S Example 6.2*

^cau-b6-3-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.3.1: Leaving a Hemisphere
> A particle slides from rest near the top of a fixed frictionless hemisphere of radius $R$. Where does it leave the surface?
>
> Keep both polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$, $\theta$ from the vertical, and the constraint $f = r - R = 0$, a distance, so $\lambda$ is the outward normal force $N$. With $L = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2) - mgr\cos\theta$, [[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]] gives
>
> $$
> m\ddot r - mr\dot\theta^2 + mg\cos\theta = \lambda, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(mr^2\dot\theta\bigr) - mgr\sin\theta = 0 .
> $$
>
> With $r = R$ and $\ddot r = 0$, the second is the tangential equation $\ddot\theta = (g/R)\sin\theta$, and the first gives the normal force
>
> $$
> N = \lambda = mg\cos\theta - mR\dot\theta^2 ,
> $$
>
> the radial Newton equation with its centripetal term. Energy conservation from rest at the top ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]) gives $R\dot\theta^2 = 2g(1 - \cos\theta)$, so $N = mg(3\cos\theta - 2)$. It vanishes at $\cos\theta = 2/3$, $\theta = 48.2^\circ$, where the particle leaves the surface ([[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers#^cau-b6-3-2|Caution: One-sided constraints]]).
>
> *Source: Morin §6.3, eqs. (6.27)–(6.32) (the leaving angle computed here)*

^ex-b6-3-1

> [!example] Example §B6.3.2: Tension in the String through the Table
> Return to the mass $m_1$ on the table tied through a hole to the hanging mass $m_2$ ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^ex-b6-1-1|Example §B6.1.1]]) and find the tension.
>
> Keep $r$, $\theta$ and the depth $z$ of $m_2$ (positive downward) as coordinates, with the string constraint $f = r + z - \ell = 0$:
>
> $$
> L = \tfrac12 m_1(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2) + \tfrac12 m_2\dot z^2 + m_2gz .
> $$
>
> The multiplier equations are
>
> $$
> m_1\ddot r - m_1r\dot\theta^2 = \lambda, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(m_1r^2\dot\theta\bigr) = 0, \qquad m_2\ddot z - m_2g = \lambda, \qquad \ddot r + \ddot z = 0 .
> $$
>
> Here $\lambda\,\partial f/\partial r = \lambda$ is the radial force on $m_1$ (outward if positive) and $\lambda\,\partial f/\partial z = \lambda$ the vertical force on $m_2$ (downward if positive). The string pulls $m_1$ toward the hole and $m_2$ upward, so $\lambda = -T$. Eliminating $\ddot r = r\dot\theta^2 + \lambda/m_1$ and $\ddot z = g + \lambda/m_2$ with the constraint,
>
> $$
> T = -\lambda = \frac{m_1m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,\bigl(g + r\dot\theta^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> It is always positive, so the string stays taut, as assumed. *Checks.* Starting from rest ($\dot\theta = 0$): $T = m_1m_2g/(m_1 + m_2)$, less than the weight $m_2g$, since $m_2$ accelerates downward. Uniform circular motion ($\ddot r = \ddot z = 0$, $m_1r\dot\theta^2 = m_2g$): $T = \frac{m_1m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\bigl(g + \frac{m_2g}{m_1}\bigr) = m_2g$, as the hanging mass is then in equilibrium, and $T = m_1r\dot\theta^2$ supplies the centripetal force ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^thm-a3-9-1|Theorem §A3.9.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 421, Final (Fall 2021), Problem 1(d), (f) (solved here)*

^ex-b6-3-2

> [!example] Example §B6.3.3: A Hoop Rolling off a Fixed Cylinder
> A thin hoop of radius $a$ and mass $m$ (moment of inertia $ma^2$ about its axis, [[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^def-a6-2-1|Def. §A6.2.1]]) starts from rest on top of a fixed horizontal cylinder of radius $R$ and rolls off without slipping. Where does it lose contact?
>
> Coordinates: the polar position $(r, \theta)$ of the hoop's centre about the cylinder's axis, $\theta$ from the vertical, and the hoop's rotation angle $\psi$ measured from a fixed direction. Two constraints act:
> - *rolling* (holonomic: the contact point is at rest, so the centre's speed $(R + a)\dot\theta$ equals $a\dot\psi$, which integrates to $a\psi = (R + a)\theta + \text{const}$). Its force, static friction, is not wanted: eliminate $\psi$;
> - *contact*, $f = r - (R + a) = 0$, a distance: keep it with a multiplier, $\lambda = N$.
>
> With $\psi$ eliminated, $T = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2) + \tfrac12 m(R + a)^2\dot\theta^2$ and $V = mgr\cos\theta$. The $r$ equation at $r = R + a$ gives $N = mg\cos\theta - m(R + a)\dot\theta^2$, and energy conservation from rest at the top ([[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-3|Theorem §A6.5.3]]), $m(R + a)^2\dot\theta^2 = mg(R + a)(1 - \cos\theta)$, gives
>
> $$
> N = mg\,(2\cos\theta - 1) ,
> $$
>
> so the hoop leaves at $\cos\theta = 1/2$, $\theta = 60^\circ$, whatever the radii. Half its kinetic energy is rotational, so it moves more slowly than a sliding particle and stays on longer (the particle leaves at $\cos\theta = 2/3$, [[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers#^ex-b6-3-1|Example §B6.3.1]]). For a body with moment of inertia $kma^2$ the same steps give $\cos\theta = 2/(3 + k)$.
>
> The rolling condition must use the *absolute* rotation of the hoop. Measured relative to the turning line of centres, the rotation angle $\phi = \psi - \theta$ satisfies $a\dot\phi = R\dot\theta$; using that relative rate in the rotational kinetic energy is a slip that changes the answer.
>
> *Source: H&S Example 6.2 (set-up; result corrected here)*

## Beyond holonomic constraints

> [!remark] Remark: Non-holonomic constraints
> Some constraints restrict velocities in a way that cannot be integrated to a condition on positions. A wheel rolling upright on a plane without slipping or skidding must move along the direction it faces: with position $(x, y)$ and heading $\theta$, $\dot x\sin\theta - \dot y\cos\theta = 0$. If the heading is steered, this cannot be integrated to a relation $g(x, y, \theta) = 0$: the wheel can reach any position with any heading, so no coordinate can be eliminated, although the velocities are restricted. A constraint linear in the velocities, $\sum_i a_{\alpha i}(q, t)\dot q_i + a_{\alpha t}(q, t) = 0$, is holonomic when its coefficients are, possibly after multiplication by a common factor, the partial derivatives of one function $f_\alpha(q, t)$; then the cross-derivative conditions $\partial a_{\alpha i}/\partial t = \partial a_{\alpha t}/\partial q_i$ and $\partial a_{\alpha i}/\partial q_j = \partial a_{\alpha j}/\partial q_i$ hold, because mixed partials of $f_\alpha$ commute. For workless constraints of this linear kind, Newton's laws give equations of the same shape as [[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers#^thm-b6-3-1|Theorem §B6.3.1]], with $a_{\alpha i}$ in place of $\partial f_\alpha/\partial q_i$, solved together with the velocity conditions. Hamilton's principle in the form of [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|Principle §B6.2.1]] does not cover them. Inequalities, such as a particle confined to a box, are also non-holonomic. These notes treat non-holonomic systems no further.
>
> *Source: H&S §4.2 (box), §6.1 (eqs. (6.16)–(6.23)), Example 6.4 · Series II §11 (Outlook: non-holonomic constraints "genuinely require more")*
