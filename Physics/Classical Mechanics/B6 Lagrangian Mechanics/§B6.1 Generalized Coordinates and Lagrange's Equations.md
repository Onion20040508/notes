Newton's second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]) is a statement about Cartesian vectors, one for each particle, and it must carry every force, including normal forces and tensions ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-1|Def. §A3.6.1]], [[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-2|Def. §A3.6.2]]) that are unknown until the motion is known. This section derives from it Lagrange's equations: one equation per independent coordinate, in whatever coordinates fit the constraints, with the constraint forces gone. The inputs are level A: the second law, kinetic energy ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]]) and conservative forces as minus a gradient ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]]). New here: generalized coordinates, configuration space and the Lagrangian. In [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action]] the same equations reappear as the Euler–Lagrange equations of [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]].

*Notation.* As in the series and in Morin, $T$ is the kinetic energy and $V$ the potential energy (level A writes $K$ and $U$). The system has $N$ particles with masses $m_a$ and positions $\vec r_a$, $a = 1, \dots, N$.

## Constraints and generalized coordinates

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.1: Holonomic Constraint; Degrees of Freedom
> A **holonomic constraint** is a condition on the positions of the particles, and possibly the time, of the form
>
> $$
> f(\vec r_1, \dots, \vec r_N, t) = 0 .
> $$
>
> It is **scleronomic** if $f$ does not depend on $t$ explicitly and **rheonomic** if it does. If $m$ holonomic constraints with linearly independent gradients (in $\mathbb R^{3N}$) act, the allowed positions at each instant form a surface of dimension $n = 3N - m$ in $\mathbb R^{3N}$, and $n$ is the number of **degrees of freedom**. Constraints of other kinds, inequalities or conditions on velocities that cannot be integrated to conditions on positions, are **non-holonomic** ([[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers#^rem-b6-3-4|Remark: Non-holonomic constraints]]).
>
> Examples: a pendulum bob on a rod of length $\ell$ ($|\vec r| - \ell = 0$); a bead on a hoop that is turned at a prescribed rate (rheonomic); the fixed distances between the particles of a rigid body; a wheel rolling without slipping along a line, $x - R\theta = 0$ ([[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-1|Theorem §A6.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: Series II §3.2 (Setup) · H&S §4.2, §6.1 · Landau §1, §5*

^def-b6-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.2: Generalized Coordinates; Configuration Space
> **Generalized coordinates** for a system with $n$ degrees of freedom are $n$ numbers $q_1, \dots, q_n$ that label the allowed configurations, through $C^2$ maps
>
> $$
> \vec r_a = \vec r_a(q_1, \dots, q_n, t), \qquad a = 1, \dots, N ,
> $$
>
> that satisfy all the constraints identically and whose $n$ partial-derivative vectors $(\partial\vec r_1/\partial q_i, \dots, \partial\vec r_N/\partial q_i) \in \mathbb R^{3N}$ are linearly independent. Their time derivatives $\dot q_i$ are the **generalized velocities**. The set of allowed configurations is the **configuration space**; a motion is a curve $q(t)$ in it. The $q_i$ need not be lengths (angles are common), and $\dot q_i$ need not have the units of a velocity.
>
> Experience shows that the coordinates and velocities at one instant determine the subsequent motion. So the state of the system is the pair $(q, \dot q)$, and the equations of motion are of second order in time.
>
> *Source: Landau §1 · H&S §4.2 · Series II §3.2*

^def-b6-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Configuration space as a manifold
> For scleronomic constraints the configuration space is a level set $\{f_1 = \dots = f_m = 0\}$ in $\mathbb R^{3N}$ with independent gradients, hence a smooth manifold of dimension $n$ ([[§9 Manifolds in Euclidean Space#^prop-9-2|591 Prop. §9.2]]); generalized coordinates are local charts on it. One chart need not cover all of it: the angles $(\theta, \varphi)$ of a spherical pendulum fail at the poles. The state $(q, \dot q)$ is a point of its tangent bundle ([[§17 The Tangent Bundle#^def-17-1|591 Def. §17.1]]), and the Lagrangian below is a function on the tangent bundle.

^rem-b6-1-1

> [!model] Model §B6.1.1: Ideal Holonomic Constraints
> The system is subject to holonomic constraints, enforced by **constraint forces** $\vec C_a$, so that Newton's second law reads
>
> $$
> m_a\ddot{\vec r}_a = \vec F_a + \vec C_a ,
> $$
>
> with $\vec F_a$ the **applied** forces. The constraints are **ideal**: the constraint forces do no work in any **virtual displacement**, a displacement that respects the constraints at frozen time,
>
> $$
> \sum_{a=1}^N \vec C_a\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_i} = 0, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n .
> $$
>
> Realised (to good approximation) by frictionless surfaces and wires, rigid rods and links of negligible mass, inextensible massless strings over frictionless pulleys ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^mod-a3-6-2|Model §A3.6.2]]: the tensions at the two ends of a string do equal and opposite work), and rolling without slipping (the static friction acts at a point at rest, [[§A6.5 Rolling#^thm-a6-5-3|Theorem §A6.5.3]]).
>
> *Valid when:* sliding friction at the constraints is negligible and the constraining elements have negligible mass. Sliding friction is not ideal and must be kept among the applied forces.
>
> *Source: Series II Postulate 3.4, eq. (45) · H&S §4.3 (point 2), Examples 4.1, 4.6 · Landau §5 (end)*

^mod-b6-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Virtual is not actual
> For a rheonomic constraint the actual displacement $d\vec r_a = \sum_i (\partial\vec r_a/\partial q_i)\,dq_i + (\partial\vec r_a/\partial t)\,dt$ has a part along $\partial\vec r_a/\partial t$, on which a constraint force can do work. A bead on a frictionless hoop that is spun at a prescribed rate is pushed around by the hoop, which does real work on it, although the normal force is perpendicular to the hoop at every instant and does no virtual work. The model asks only for the virtual statement; Lagrange's equations then hold, but $T + V$ need not be conserved ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem]]).

^cau-b6-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.3: Generalized Force
> The **generalized force** conjugate to $q_i$ is
>
> $$
> Q_i = \sum_{a=1}^N \vec F_a\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_i} ,
> $$
>
> so that the work of the applied forces in a virtual displacement is $\sum_a \vec F_a\cdot\delta\vec r_a = \sum_i Q_i\,\delta q_i$. The product $Q_i q_i$ has the units of energy: for an angle, $Q_i$ is a torque. If the applied forces come from a potential energy, $\vec F_a = -\nabla_a V(\vec r_1, \dots, \vec r_N, t)$, then by the chain rule $Q_i = -\partial V/\partial q_i$, with $V$ written in the $q$'s.
>
> *Source: Series II eq. (46) and §3.2 (before eq. (50))*

^def-b6-1-3

## Lagrange's equations from Newton's laws

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.2: Two Velocity Identities
> With $\vec r_a = \vec r_a(q, t)$ as in [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]], the velocity
>
> $$
> \dot{\vec r}_a = \sum_j \frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_j}\,\dot q_j + \frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial t}
> $$
>
> is a function of $(q, \dot q, t)$, and
>
> $$
> \text{(i)}\quad \frac{\partial\dot{\vec r}_a}{\partial\dot q_i} = \frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_i}, \qquad\qquad \text{(ii)}\quad \frac{d}{dt}\,\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_i} = \frac{\partial\dot{\vec r}_a}{\partial q_i} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Series II eq. (47) · Morin §6.4, eqs. (6.43)–(6.46)*

^thm-b6-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The velocity formula is the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]). (i): $\dot q_i$ enters $\dot{\vec r}_a$ linearly, with coefficient $\partial\vec r_a/\partial q_i$, which depends on $(q, t)$ only. (ii): along a motion, $\frac{d}{dt}\,\partial\vec r_a/\partial q_i = \sum_j \frac{\partial^2\vec r_a}{\partial q_j\,\partial q_i}\,\dot q_j + \frac{\partial^2\vec r_a}{\partial t\,\partial q_i}$, while differentiating the velocity formula by $q_i$ gives the same sum with the order of the partial derivatives reversed. They agree because mixed partials of a $C^2$ map commute ([[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]).

^der-b6-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]], [[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|452 Thm. §5.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.3: Lagrange's Equations with Generalized Forces
> Under [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^mod-b6-1-1|Model §B6.1.1]], every motion that obeys Newton's second law satisfies
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_i} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_i} = Q_i, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n ,
> $$
>
> where $T = \sum_a \tfrac12 m_a\,|\dot{\vec r}_a|^2$ is written as a function of $(q, \dot q, t)$ and $Q_i$ is the generalized force ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-3|Def. §B6.1.3]]). The constraint forces do not appear.
>
> *Source: Series II §3.2, eqs. (44)–(49)*

^thm-b6-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the dot product of $m_a\ddot{\vec r}_a = \vec F_a + \vec C_a$ ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]) with $\partial\vec r_a/\partial q_i$ and sum over $a$. By the model the constraint forces drop out, and the right side is $Q_i$. On the left, the product rule and then the identities (i) and (ii) of [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]] give
>
> $$
> \ddot{\vec r}_a\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_i} = \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(\dot{\vec r}_a\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_i}\Bigr) - \dot{\vec r}_a\cdot\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_i} = \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(\dot{\vec r}_a\cdot\frac{\partial\dot{\vec r}_a}{\partial\dot q_i}\Bigr) - \dot{\vec r}_a\cdot\frac{\partial\dot{\vec r}_a}{\partial q_i} .
> $$
>
> Since $\dot{\vec r}_a\cdot\partial\dot{\vec r}_a/\partial\dot q_i = \partial\bigl(\tfrac12|\dot{\vec r}_a|^2\bigr)/\partial\dot q_i$, and likewise for $q_i$, multiplying by $m_a$ and summing gives $\sum_a m_a\ddot{\vec r}_a\cdot\partial\vec r_a/\partial q_i = \frac{d}{dt}\,\partial T/\partial\dot q_i - \partial T/\partial q_i$.

^der-b6-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^mod-b6-1-1|Model §B6.1.1]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-3|Def. §B6.1.3]], [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.4: Lagrangian
> If the applied forces come from a potential energy, $\vec F_a = -\nabla_a V(\vec r_1, \dots, \vec r_N, t)$ ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]]; $V$ may depend on $t$ explicitly, as in a prescribed external field), the **Lagrangian** of the system is
>
> $$
> L(q, \dot q, t) = T - V ,
> $$
>
> kinetic minus potential energy, both written in the generalized coordinates and velocities. The kinetic energy must be that seen from an inertial frame ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Series II Definition 3.1, eq. (38) · Morin §6.1, eq. (6.1) · H&S §4.3, eq. (4.1) · Landau §5, eqs. (5.1), (5.6)*

^def-b6-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.4: Lagrange's Equations
> Under [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^mod-b6-1-1|Model §B6.1.1]], with applied forces from a potential energy $V(\vec r_1, \dots, \vec r_N, t)$, the generalized coordinates of every Newtonian motion satisfy
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = 0, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n ,
> $$
>
> with $L = T - V$. These are $n$ second-order equations for the $n$ functions $q_i(t)$; the constraints are built into the coordinates and the constraint forces never appear.
>
> *Source: Series II Theorem 3.5, eq. (50) · Morin §6.1, §6.4 · H&S §4.3, eq. (4.5) · Landau §2, eq. (2.6); §5*

^thm-b6-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-3|Def. §B6.1.3]], $Q_i = -\partial V/\partial q_i$. Since $V$ depends on $(q, t)$ but not on $\dot q$, $\partial V/\partial\dot q_i = 0$, and [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]] becomes $\frac{d}{dt}\,\partial(T - V)/\partial\dot q_i - \partial(T - V)/\partial q_i = 0$.

^der-b6-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-3|Def. §B6.1.3]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-4|Def. §B6.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: What was proved, and the layer
> - *Newton in, Lagrange out.* Lagrange's equations are here a **theorem**: a consequence of Newton's second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]) for each particle together with the model of ideal constraints. They are the components of Newton's law along the directions $\partial\vec r/\partial q_i$ in which the system is free to move; the components perpendicular to the constraint surface carry the constraint forces and are dropped ([[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers]] recovers them).
> - *Newton is contained in it.* For one unconstrained particle in Cartesian coordinates, $L = \tfrac12 m(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 + \dot z^2) - V(x, y, z)$, and the three equations are $m\ddot x = -\partial V/\partial x$, and so on: $m\ddot{\vec r} = -\nabla V = \vec F$.
> - *The other direction.* [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action]] takes a variational principle as the starting point and obtains the same equations as its Euler–Lagrange equations. Within the conditions of the model the two routes are equivalent; the level-A principles keep their place.
>
> *Source: Series II §3.1 (eqs. (41)–(43)) · Morin §6.1, eqs. (6.5)–(6.8) · H&S §3.8, §4.3*

^rem-b6-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Forces without a potential
> [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-3|Theorem §B6.1.3]] does not need a potential. If some applied forces are conservative and others (sliding friction, drag) are not, put the conservative part into $L$ and keep the rest as generalized forces: $\frac{d}{dt}\,\partial L/\partial\dot q_i - \partial L/\partial q_i = Q_i^{\text{nc}}$. The method then loses much of its elegance, and for problems dominated by friction $\vec F = m\vec a$ is often simpler.
>
> *Source: Morin §6.2, Remark 8 · H&S §4.1*

^rem-b6-1-3

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.5: Generalized Momentum
> The **generalized momentum** (or **canonical momentum**) conjugate to $q_i$ is
>
> $$
> p_i = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i} .
> $$
>
> Lagrange's equations read $\dot p_i = \partial L/\partial q_i$. For a Cartesian coordinate $x$ of a particle in a potential, $p_x = m\dot x$; for the polar angle $\theta$ of a particle moving in a plane, $L = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2) - V$ gives $p_\theta = mr^2\dot\theta$, the angular momentum about the axis ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-1|Def. §A6.6.1]]). When $L$ does not contain $q_i$, $p_i$ is conserved: [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem]].
>
> *Source: Series II eq. (51) · H&S §4.4, eqs. (4.15)–(4.20) · Morin §6.5.1*

^def-b6-1-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.5: Kinetic Energy in Generalized Coordinates
> In generalized coordinates
>
> $$
> T = \tfrac12\sum_{i,j} M_{ij}(q, t)\,\dot q_i\dot q_j + \sum_i b_i(q, t)\,\dot q_i + T_0(q, t),
> $$
>
> $$
> M_{ij} = \sum_a m_a\,\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_i}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_j}, \qquad b_i = \sum_a m_a\,\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial q_i}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial t}, \qquad T_0 = \sum_a \tfrac12 m_a\Bigl|\frac{\partial\vec r_a}{\partial t}\Bigr|^2 .
> $$
>
> The matrix $\mathsf M(q, t)$ is symmetric and positive definite. For scleronomic constraints (and $\vec r_a(q)$ without explicit $t$), $b_i = T_0 = 0$ and $T = \tfrac12\sum_{i,j} M_{ij}(q)\,\dot q_i\dot q_j$ is a quadratic form in the velocities whose coefficients depend on the coordinates.
>
> *Source: Landau §5, eq. (5.5) · Series II §3.2*

^thm-b6-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert the velocity formula of [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]] into $\tfrac12 m_a|\dot{\vec r}_a|^2$ and expand the square; collecting the terms of second, first and zeroth order in $\dot q$ gives the three pieces. $\mathsf M$ is symmetric because the dot product is. For $\dot q \ne 0$, $\sum_{i,j} M_{ij}\dot q_i\dot q_j = \sum_a m_a\bigl|\sum_i (\partial\vec r_a/\partial q_i)\,\dot q_i\bigr|^2 \ge 0$, and it vanishes only if $\sum_i \dot q_i\,(\partial\vec r_1/\partial q_i, \dots, \partial\vec r_N/\partial q_i) = 0$ in $\mathbb R^{3N}$, which the linear independence in [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]] forbids.

^der-b6-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Kinetic energy from the line element
> For one particle, $T = \tfrac12 m\,(d\ell/dt)^2$, so it is enough to know the squared line element in the chosen coordinates: $d\ell^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\varphi^2 + dz^2$ in cylindrical coordinates gives $T = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2 + \dot z^2)$, and $d\ell^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$ in spherical coordinates gives $T = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2)$. Lagrange's equations then produce the centripetal and Coriolis-type terms of Newton's law in curvilinear coordinates ([[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form]]) without any differentiation of unit vectors. The matrix $\mathsf M$ is the mass matrix of small oscillations ([[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium]]; [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^def-b2-1-1|WO Def. §B2.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: Landau §4, eqs. (4.3)–(4.6) · H&S Example 4.4*

^rem-b6-1-4

> [!caution] Caution: Start from Cartesian velocities in an inertial frame
> Write each particle's position in Cartesian components in an inertial frame, express it through the $q$'s, and only then differentiate and square. Kinetic energies guessed directly in generalized velocities miss cross terms such as the $\dot X\dot D\cos\alpha$ of [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^ex-b6-1-2|Example §B6.1.2]], and kinetic energies measured relative to an accelerating body (such as a sliding wedge) are wrong.
>
> *Source: H&S §4.3 (point 4) and Example 4.7*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.1.1: A Mass on a Table Tied to a Hanging Mass
> A mass $m_1$ slides on a frictionless horizontal table. A massless inextensible string of length $\ell$ runs from it through a frictionless hole in the table to a mass $m_2$ that hangs below and moves only vertically; the string stays taut. Find the Lagrangian, the equations of motion and the conserved quantities, and solve for the motion that starts from rest with $m_2$ just below the table.
>
> *Coordinates.* Put the origin at the hole. Six Cartesian coordinates, four constraints ($m_1$ on the table; $m_2$ on the vertical line through the hole, two conditions; string length $r + z = \ell$, with $r$ the distance of $m_1$ from the hole and $z$ the depth of $m_2$) leave **two degrees of freedom**, taken as the polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ of $m_1$. Then $z = \ell - r$ and $\dot z = -\dot r$.
>
> *Lagrangian.* $T = \tfrac12 m_1(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2) + \tfrac12 m_2\dot z^2$ and $V = -m_2gz = m_2g\,r + \text{const}$, so
>
> $$
> L = \tfrac12(m_1 + m_2)\,\dot r^2 + \tfrac12 m_1r^2\dot\theta^2 - m_2g\,r .
> $$
>
> *Equations of motion.* By [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]],
>
> $$
> (m_1 + m_2)\,\ddot r = m_1r\dot\theta^2 - m_2g, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(m_1r^2\dot\theta\bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>
> The first is the radial equation for the whole string-and-masses system, with the centripetal term $m_1r\dot\theta^2$ appearing on its own; the tension never enters.
>
> *Conserved quantities.* $\theta$ does not appear in $L$, so $p_\theta = m_1r^2\dot\theta$, the angular momentum of $m_1$ about the vertical through the hole, is constant; and $L$ has no explicit $t$, so the energy $\tfrac12(m_1 + m_2)\dot r^2 + \tfrac12 m_1r^2\dot\theta^2 + m_2gr$ is constant ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem]]). Eliminating $\dot\theta$, $(m_1 + m_2)\ddot r = p_\theta^2/(m_1r^3) - m_2g$: a one-dimensional motion in an effective potential, as for central forces ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential]]).
>
> *From rest.* Initially $r = \ell$, $\dot r = 0$, $\dot\theta = 0$, so $p_\theta = 0$, $\theta$ stays constant and $\ddot r = -m_2g/(m_1 + m_2)$:
>
> $$
> r(t) = \ell - \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,\frac{gt^2}{2}, \qquad z(t) = \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,\frac{gt^2}{2} ,
> $$
>
> until $m_1$ reaches the hole at $t = \sqrt{2\ell(m_1 + m_2)/m_2g}$. The weight of $m_2$ alone accelerates the total mass $m_1 + m_2$.
>
> *Uniform circular motion.* If $m_1$ circles at constant angular velocity $\omega$, then $\ddot r = 0$ and the radial equation fixes the radius, $m_1r_0\omega^2 = m_2g$. The tension in both cases is found in [[§B6.3 Constraints and Lagrange Multipliers#^ex-b6-3-2|Example §B6.3.2]].
>

> [!example] Example §B6.1.2: A Block on a Sliding Wedge
> A block of mass $m$ slides on the frictionless face of a wedge of mass $M$ and angle $\alpha$, which itself slides on a frictionless floor. Find the accelerations.
>
> Two degrees of freedom: the wedge's position $X$ and the block's distance $D$ down the face from its top. With the origin at the top of the face at $t = 0$, the block is at $x = X + D\cos\alpha$, $y = -D\sin\alpha$. From the Cartesian velocities ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^cau-b6-1-2|Caution: Start from Cartesian velocities in an inertial frame]]),
>
> $$
> T = \tfrac12 M\dot X^2 + \tfrac12 m\bigl[(\dot X + \dot D\cos\alpha)^2 + \dot D^2\sin^2\alpha\bigr] = \tfrac12(M + m)\dot X^2 + \tfrac12 m\dot D^2 + m\dot X\dot D\cos\alpha ,
> $$
>
> and $V = mgy = -mgD\sin\alpha$. Lagrange's equations are
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\bigl[(M + m)\dot X + m\dot D\cos\alpha\bigr] = 0, \qquad m\ddot D + m\ddot X\cos\alpha = mg\sin\alpha ,
> $$
>
> with the solution
>
> $$
> \ddot D = \frac{(M + m)\,g\sin\alpha}{M + m\sin^2\alpha}, \qquad \ddot X = -\frac{m\,g\sin\alpha\cos\alpha}{M + m\sin^2\alpha} .
> $$
>
> *Checks.* As $M \to \infty$, $\ddot X \to 0$ and $\ddot D \to g\sin\alpha$, the fixed incline ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^thm-a3-6-1|Theorem §A3.6.1]]). As $M \to 0$, the block's accelerations are $\ddot x = \ddot X + \ddot D\cos\alpha = 0$ and $\ddot y = -\ddot D\sin\alpha = -g$: free fall. The first equation says that $p_X = (M + m)\dot X + m\dot D\cos\alpha$, the total horizontal momentum, is conserved, because $X$ does not appear in $L$. No normal force appeared anywhere; with Newton's laws there are three (block on wedge, wedge on block, floor on wedge).
>
> *Source: H&S Example 4.7 · Morin Problem 6.1*
