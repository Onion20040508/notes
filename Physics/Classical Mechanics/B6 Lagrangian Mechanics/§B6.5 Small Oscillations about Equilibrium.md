Near a stable equilibrium a one-dimensional system oscillates harmonically with $\omega^2 = U''(x_0)/m$ ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-3|WO Theorem §A1.2.3]], [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|Theorem §A4.7.2]]), and a network of point masses and springs oscillates in normal modes, found from $\det(\mathsf K - \omega^2\mathsf M) = 0$ ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-3|WO Theorem §B2.1.3]]). This section derives the small-oscillation equations for *any* holonomic system from its Lagrangian: expand $L$ to quadratic order about an equilibrium, then vary. What is new compared with the spring networks of Oscillations, Waves and Optics B2 is that the mass matrix comes from the kinetic energy in generalized coordinates and need not be diagonal, as the double pendulum shows; the normal-mode theory itself is the one of WO B2, extended to such a mass matrix. As in [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations]], $T$ is the kinetic energy and $U$ the potential energy (Morin and the series write $V$).

## The quadratic approximation

> [!model] Model §B6.5.1: Small-Oscillation (Quadratic) Approximation
> A system with $n$ generalized coordinates, time-independent constraints and a velocity-independent potential has
>
> $$
> L = \tfrac12\,\dot q^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M(q)\,\dot q - U(q)
> $$
>
> with $\mathsf M(q)$ symmetric and positive definite ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]); its conserved energy function is $h = T + U$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]]). Let $q_0$ be an equilibrium, $\partial U/\partial q_i(q_0) = 0$, and write $x = q - q_0$. The **small-oscillation approximation** replaces $L$ by its quadratic part
>
> $$
> L_2 = \tfrac12\,\dot x^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\,\dot x - \tfrac12\,x^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K\,x, \qquad \mathsf M = \mathsf M(q_0), \qquad K_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial q_i\,\partial q_j}\Big|_{q_0},
> $$
>
> both matrices constant, real and symmetric; constant terms are dropped. Assumptions: $U$ and $\mathsf M$ are smooth near $q_0$, and the displacements and velocities are small enough that the cubic terms of $L$ are negligible next to the quadratic ones. For oscillation about the equilibrium, $\mathsf K$ is positive definite (a strict, nondegenerate minimum of $U$).
>
> *Source: Series II §5.3, eqs. (81)–(83) · H&S §13.2, eqs. (13.66)–(13.69) · Morin §6.7*

^mod-b6-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.5.2: Equilibria and Linearized Equations
> For $L = \tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M(q)\dot q - U(q)$:
> 1. the constant curve $q(t) = q_0$ solves Lagrange's equations if and only if $\nabla U(q_0) = 0$;
> 2. the Lagrange equations of the quadratic Lagrangian $L_2$ are
>
> $$
> \mathsf M\,\ddot x = -\mathsf K\,x ,
> $$
>
> and they coincide with the linearization of the exact Lagrange equations about $q_0$: expanding and then varying gives the same result as varying and then expanding.
>
> *Source: Series II §5.3, Lemma 5.1 · H&S §13.2, eq. (13.71) · Morin §6.7*

^thm-b6-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Since $T$ is quadratic in $\dot q$ with $\partial T/\partial\dot q_i = \sum_j M_{ij}\dot q_j$, Lagrange's equations ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]) read
>
> $$
> \sum_j M_{ij}(q)\,\ddot q_j + \sum_{j,k}\Bigl(\frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial q_k} - \frac12\frac{\partial M_{jk}}{\partial q_i}\Bigr)\dot q_j\dot q_k + \frac{\partial U}{\partial q_i} = 0 .
> $$
>
> (1) For $q \equiv q_0$ the first two terms vanish, leaving $\partial U/\partial q_i(q_0) = 0$; conversely, if $\nabla U(q_0) = 0$ the constant curve satisfies every equation.
>
> (2) By Taylor's theorem ([[§11 Taylor's Theorem for Multivariable Functions#^thm-11-2|452 Thm. §11.2]]), $U(q_0 + x) = U(q_0) + 0 + \tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K x + O(|x|^3)$, the linear term vanishing at the equilibrium; and $\mathsf M(q_0 + x) = \mathsf M + O(|x|)$, whose correction multiplies $\dot x\dot x$ and is cubic. So $L = L_2 + (\text{terms of degree} \ge 3 \text{ in } x, \dot x)$. Lagrange's equations take one derivative of $L$ with respect to $x$ or $\dot x$ (then possibly $d/dt$, which preserves the degree): a term of degree $d$ in $L$ yields terms of degree $d - 1$ in the equations. So the terms of degree $\ge 3$ in $L$ give exactly the terms of degree $\ge 2$ in the equations, which linearization discards, and every quadratic term of $L$ survives as a linear term. For $L_2$, $\partial L_2/\partial\dot x = \mathsf M\dot x$ and $\partial L_2/\partial x = -\mathsf K x$ (using the symmetry of $\mathsf M$ and $\mathsf K$), so $\mathsf M\ddot x + \mathsf K x = 0$. In the exact equation above, the middle term is quadratic in $\dot x$ and $M_{ij}(q) \ddot q_j = M_{ij}\ddot x_j + O(|x|\,|\ddot x|)$, which confirms the result.

> [!remark] Remark: Why the equilibrium is stable when U has a minimum
> If $q_0$ is a strict local minimum of $U$, the conserved energy $h = T + U$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]]) keeps a motion that starts close to $q_0$ with small velocity close to $q_0$: since $T \ge 0$, $U(q(t)) \le h$ for all $t$, and for $h$ slightly above $U(q_0)$ the set $\{U \le h\}$ near $q_0$ is a small neighbourhood. This is the energy-diagram argument of [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|Theorem §A4.7.2]] in $n$ dimensions. The symmetric matrices $\mathsf M$ and $\mathsf K$ are the second-order Taylor coefficients of $T$ and $U$; an antisymmetric part would drop out of the quadratic forms anyway (H&S §13.2).

^rem-b6-5-1

> [!caution] Caution: Expand to the right order
> - Velocities are already first-order small, so the coefficients of the kinetic energy are needed only at the equilibrium: in the double pendulum, $\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2 \to \dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2$, the correction being of fourth order.
> - The potential must be kept to *second* order: $\cos\theta \to 1 - \tfrac12\theta^2$. Replacing $\cos\theta$ by $1$ throws away the restoring force altogether.
> - Expanding $L$ once and then differentiating a polynomial is less error-prone than linearizing long nonlinear equations of motion term by term, and [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^thm-b6-5-2|Theorem §B6.5.2]] guarantees the two agree.

## Normal modes

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.5.3: Normal Modes of a Quadratic Lagrangian
> Let $\mathsf M$ be real, symmetric and positive definite, and $\mathsf K$ real and symmetric. Then:
> 1. $x(t) = v\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ with $v \ne 0$ solves $\mathsf M\ddot x = -\mathsf K x$ if and only if $\mathsf K v = \omega^2\mathsf M v$, the **generalized eigenvalue problem**; the squared frequencies are the roots of
>
> $$
> \det\bigl(\mathsf K - \omega^2\mathsf M\bigr) = 0 ;
> $$
>
> 2. there are $n$ real roots $\omega_1^2 \le \dots \le \omega_n^2$, all positive if $\mathsf K$ is positive definite, with real mode shapes $v_1, \dots, v_n$ that form a basis and are orthonormal in the $\mathsf M$ inner product, $v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M v_s = \delta_{rs}$;
> 3. in the normal coordinates $\xi_r = v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M x$, so that $x = \sum_r\xi_r v_r$, the system decouples into $n$ independent oscillators, $\ddot\xi_r = -\omega_r^2\xi_r$, and every small motion is a superposition of normal modes.
>
> *Normalisation.* These are the normal coordinates of [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^def-b2-2-1|WO Def. §B2.2.1]], $\xi_r = v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M x/v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M v_r$, with the mode shapes scaled to $v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M v_r = 1$; WO keeps unscaled shapes such as $(1, 1)$, and so does [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^ex-b6-5-1|Example §B6.5.1]]. The letter $\xi$ replaces WO's $q_n$, which here are the generalized coordinates. With this scaling the energy is $\sum_r \tfrac12(\dot\xi_r^2 + \omega_r^2\xi_r^2)$, unit-mass oscillators.
>
> This is the normal-mode theory of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^def-b2-1-2|WO Def. §B2.1.2]], [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-3|WO Theorem §B2.1.3]] and [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|WO Theorem §B2.1.5]], with normal coordinates and the general solution as in [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-1|WO Theorem §B2.2.1]] and [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-3|WO Theorem §B2.2.3]]; there it was proved for point masses on springs, $\mathsf M = \operatorname{diag}(m_1, \dots, m_n)$. What is new here is a mass matrix with off-diagonal entries, which generalized coordinates produce.
>
> *Source: Series II §5.4, Theorem 5.2 and Appendix E · H&S §13.2, eqs. (13.72)–(13.82)*

^thm-b6-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Part 1 is the computation of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^der-b2-1-3|the derivation of WO Theorem §B2.1.3]] word for word: substituting the mode gives $(-\omega^2\mathsf M v + \mathsf K v)\cos(\omega t - \phi) = 0$, and a nonzero $v$ with $(\mathsf K - \omega^2\mathsf M)v = 0$ exists exactly when the determinant vanishes ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-50|LADR Thm. 9.50]]).
>
> For part 2, the WO proof put $y = \mathsf M^{1/2}x$ with $\mathsf M^{1/2} = \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{m_i})$. For a non-diagonal $\mathsf M$, take instead the unique positive square root: $\mathsf M$ is positive, so it has a unique positive (in particular symmetric) square root $\mathsf M^{1/2}$ ([[§25 Positive Operators#^ladr-7-39|LADR Thm. 7.39]]), invertible because $\mathsf M$ is. Concretely, diagonalize $\mathsf M = \mathsf O^{\mathsf T}\mathsf D\mathsf O$ with $\mathsf O$ orthogonal and $\mathsf D = \operatorname{diag}(d_i)$, $d_i > 0$ ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]), and set $\mathsf M^{1/2} = \mathsf O^{\mathsf T}\mathsf D^{1/2}\mathsf O$. With $w = \mathsf M^{1/2}v$ the problem becomes
>
> $$
> \mathsf S\,w = \omega^2 w, \qquad \mathsf S = \mathsf M^{-1/2}\,\mathsf K\,\mathsf M^{-1/2},
> $$
>
> and $\mathsf S$ is symmetric. From here the WO proof applies unchanged: an orthonormal eigenbasis $w_r$ with real eigenvalues $\omega_r^2$ (real spectral theorem), $v_r = \mathsf M^{-1/2}w_r$ is a basis with $v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M v_s = w_r^{\mathsf T}w_s = \delta_{rs}$, and $\omega_r^2 = v_r^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K v_r > 0$ when $\mathsf K$ is positive definite.
>
> Part 3 is [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-1|WO Theorem §B2.2.1]] and [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-3|WO Theorem §B2.2.3]], whose proofs use only $\mathsf K v_r = \omega_r^2\mathsf M v_r$ and the $\mathsf M$-orthogonality of the shapes, so they hold unchanged for a non-diagonal $\mathsf M$; multiplying $x = \sum_r\xi_r v_r$ by $v_s^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M$ gives $\xi_s = v_s^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M x$.

^der-b6-5-3

*Uses:* [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^thm-b6-5-2|Theorem §B6.5.2]], [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-3|WO Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|WO Theorem §B2.1.5]], [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-1|WO Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-3|WO Theorem §B2.2.3]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-50|LADR Thm. 9.50]], [[§25 Positive Operators#^ladr-7-39|LADR Thm. 7.39]], [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]], [[§23 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-12|LADR Thm. 7.12]]

> [!remark] Remark: Zero and negative eigenvalues
> - If $\mathsf K$ is only positive semidefinite, some $\omega_r = 0$: no restoring force along $v_r$, and $\xi_r = a + bt$ drifts. A linear CO$_2$ model (oxygen masses $M$ at the ends, carbon $m$ in the middle, springs $k$) has $\omega_1 = 0$ with $v_1 = (1, 1, 1)$, the free translation, besides $\omega_2^2 = k/M$, $v_2 = (1, 0, -1)$ (carbon at rest) and $\omega_3^2 = (k/M)(1 + 2M/m)$, $v_3 = (1, -2M/m, 1)$ (carbon against the oxygens).
> - If $\mathsf K$ has a negative eigenvalue, the equilibrium is not a minimum of $U$; along that direction $\omega_r^2 < 0$ and $\xi_r \propto e^{\pm|\omega_r|t}$ grows: the equilibrium is unstable.
>
> H&S §13.2 state that all $\omega^2$ are "real and positive" because $T \ge 0$; positivity of $T$ (of $\mathsf M$) makes them real, but positive values need $\mathsf K$ positive definite, as their own CO$_2$ example with $\omega_1 = 0$ shows.
>
> *Source: H&S §13.2, Example 13.5 · Morin Problem 6.11 (solution)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.5.1: The Double Pendulum at Small Angles
> Bob 1 (mass $m_1$) hangs from a fixed pivot on a light rigid rod of length $\ell_1$; bob 2 (mass $m_2$) hangs from bob 1 on a rod of length $\ell_2$; the motion is in a vertical plane, and $\theta_1, \theta_2$ are the angles of the rods from the downward vertical. Find the normal modes.
>
> *Lagrangian.* With $x_1 = \ell_1\sin\theta_1$, $y_1 = -\ell_1\cos\theta_1$, $x_2 = x_1 + \ell_2\sin\theta_2$, $y_2 = y_1 - \ell_2\cos\theta_2$, the speed of bob 2 contains the cross term $2\ell_1\ell_2\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2$, and
>
> $$
> T = \tfrac12(m_1 + m_2)\ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2\ell_2^2\dot\theta_2^2 + m_2\ell_1\ell_2\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2, \qquad U = -(m_1 + m_2)g\ell_1\cos\theta_1 - m_2g\ell_2\cos\theta_2 .
> $$
>
> Neither rod tension appears: the constraints are ideal and cost nothing ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^mod-b6-1-1|Model §B6.1.1]]). The hanging position $\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0$ is an equilibrium, and [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^mod-b6-5-1|Model §B6.5.1]] (see [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^cau-b6-5-1|Caution: Expand to the right order]]) gives
>
> $$
> \mathsf M = \begin{pmatrix} (m_1 + m_2)\ell_1^2 & m_2\ell_1\ell_2 \\ m_2\ell_1\ell_2 & m_2\ell_2^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathsf K = \begin{pmatrix} (m_1 + m_2)g\ell_1 & 0 \\ 0 & m_2g\ell_2 \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> $\mathsf M$ has the off-diagonal entry $m_2\ell_1\ell_2$ (the coordinates are coupled through their velocities) and $\det\mathsf M = m_1m_2\ell_1^2\ell_2^2 > 0$; $\mathsf K$ is diagonal and positive definite.
>
> *Equal lengths*, $\ell_1 = \ell_2 = \ell$. With $\lambda = \omega^2\ell/g$ and $s = \sqrt{m_2/(m_1 + m_2)}$, $\det(\mathsf K - \omega^2\mathsf M) = g^2\ell^2\bigl[(m_1 + m_2)m_2(1 - \lambda)^2 - m_2^2\lambda^2\bigr] = 0$ gives $1 - \lambda = \pm s\lambda$, and the first row of $(\mathsf K - \omega^2\mathsf M)v = 0$, $(m_1 + m_2)(1 - \lambda)v_1 = m_2\lambda v_2$, gives the shape:
>
> $$
> \omega_\mp^2 = \frac{g/\ell}{1 \pm s}, \qquad v_\mp = \Bigl(1,\ \pm\sqrt{\frac{m_1 + m_2}{m_2}}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> In the slow mode $\omega_-$ the rods swing to the same side, in the fast mode $\omega_+$ to opposite sides. Limits: for $m_2 \ll m_1$ ($s \approx \sqrt{m_2/m_1}$) both frequencies approach $\sqrt{g/\ell}$, $\omega_\mp \approx (1 \mp s/2)\sqrt{g/\ell}$, and $|v_2/v_1| \gg 1$: the heavy upper bob hardly moves and the light lower one swings as a pendulum of length $\ell$. For $m_1 \ll m_2$ ($s \to 1$), $\omega_- \to \sqrt{g/2\ell}$ with $v_- = (1, 1)$, a straight pendulum of length $2\ell$, while $\omega_+^2 \approx 2(m_2/m_1)\,g/\ell$ with $v_+ = (1, -1)$: the heavy lower bob stays put ($x_2 = \ell(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = 0$) and the light upper bob vibrates fast between two taut rods.
>
> *Equal masses and lengths.* Then $s = 1/\sqrt2$ and
>
> $$
> \omega_\mp^2 = \bigl(2 \mp \sqrt2\bigr)\frac g\ell \approx 0.586\,\frac g\ell,\ 3.414\,\frac g\ell, \qquad v_\mp = \bigl(1,\ \pm\sqrt2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The frequencies bracket the single pendulum's $g/\ell$: the in-phase mode acts like a longer pendulum, the scissoring mode like a stiffer one. The shapes are $\mathsf M$-orthogonal, $v_-^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M v_+ = m\ell^2(1, \sqrt2)\cdot(2 - \sqrt2,\ 1 - \sqrt2) = 0$, but not orthogonal, $(1, \sqrt2)\cdot(1, -\sqrt2) = -1$: with a non-diagonal mass matrix the $\mathsf M$ inner product is not decoration.
>
> *Release from rest* with $\theta(0) = (\alpha, 0)$, $\dot\theta(0) = 0$. Since $(\alpha, 0) = \tfrac\alpha2(1, \sqrt2) + \tfrac\alpha2(1, -\sqrt2)$, each mode starts from rest with amplitude $\alpha/2$ ([[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^thm-b6-5-3|Theorem §B6.5.3]]):
>
> $$
> \theta_1(t) = \frac\alpha2\bigl(\cos\omega_-t + \cos\omega_+t\bigr), \qquad \theta_2(t) = \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt2}\bigl(\cos\omega_-t - \cos\omega_+t\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Check: $\ddot\theta(0) = \bigl(-\tfrac\alpha2(\omega_-^2 + \omega_+^2),\ -\tfrac{\alpha}{\sqrt2}(\omega_-^2 - \omega_+^2)\bigr) = (-2\alpha g/\ell,\ 2\alpha g/\ell)$, which equals $-\mathsf M^{-1}\mathsf K\theta(0)$ evaluated directly. The frequency ratio $\omega_+/\omega_- = 1 + \sqrt2$ is irrational, so the motion never repeats exactly; energy passes back and forth between the bobs.
>
> *Source: Series II §5.1–5.5, §7.2 · Morin Problem 6.19 (and solution)*

> [!example] Example §B6.5.2: Bead on a Rotating Hoop
> A bead slides without friction on a hoop of radius $R$ that turns about its vertical diameter at constant angular speed $\omega$. Find the equilibria and the frequencies of small oscillations; $\theta$ is the angle from the bottom.
>
> The bead's velocity has components $R\dot\theta$ along the hoop and $\omega R\sin\theta$ perpendicular to it, so
>
> $$
> L = \tfrac12 mR^2\dot\theta^2 + \tfrac12 mR^2\omega^2\sin^2\theta + mgR\cos\theta = \tfrac12 mR^2\dot\theta^2 - U_{\text{eff}}(\theta), \qquad U_{\text{eff}} = -mgR\cos\theta - \tfrac12 mR^2\omega^2\sin^2\theta .
> $$
>
> The constraint moves, so the term $T_0 = \tfrac12 mR^2\omega^2\sin^2\theta$ appears ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]]); but $L$ has the form of [[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^mod-b6-5-1|Model §B6.5.1]] with $U_{\text{eff}} = U - T_0$ in place of $U$, and the conserved $h = \tfrac12 mR^2\dot\theta^2 + U_{\text{eff}}$ plays the part of the energy. Now $U_{\text{eff}}'(\theta) = mR\sin\theta\,(g - R\omega^2\cos\theta)$:
> - $\theta = \pi$ (top): $U_{\text{eff}}'' = -mR(g + R\omega^2) < 0$, always unstable.
> - $\theta = 0$ (bottom): $U_{\text{eff}}'' = mR(g - R\omega^2)$, stable for $\omega^2 < g/R$, with $\Omega^2 = U_{\text{eff}}''/mR^2 = g/R - \omega^2$.
> - $\cos\theta_0 = g/(R\omega^2)$, which exists for $\omega^2 > g/R$: here $g - R\omega^2\cos\theta_0 = 0$, so $U_{\text{eff}}'' = mR^2\omega^2\sin^2\theta_0 > 0$, stable, with $\Omega = \omega\sin\theta_0 = \sqrt{\omega^2 - g^2/R^2\omega^2}$.
>
> At $\omega_c = \sqrt{g/R}$ the bottom loses its stability and the bead moves out to $\pm\theta_0$; the oscillation frequency goes to zero there from both sides. For $\omega \to \infty$, $\Omega \to \omega$: gravity no longer matters, and the bead is a pendulum in the "centrifugal gravity" $\omega^2R$. Using $U = -mgR\cos\theta$ alone would predict the bottom stable at every $\omega$.
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 6.11 (and solution)*

> [!example] Example §B6.5.3: Masses on Perpendicular Axes
> Two masses $m$ slide without friction, one on the positive $x$ axis and one on the positive $y$ axis. Each is tied to the origin by a spring of constant $k$, and the two are joined by a third spring of constant $k'$; at equilibrium all three springs are relaxed. Find the normal modes of small oscillation.
>
> At equilibrium the masses sit at $(a, 0)$ and $(0, a)$, with $a$ the relaxed length of the axis springs, and the coupling spring has its relaxed length $a\sqrt2$. With displacements $x$ and $y$ along the axes, its length is
>
> $$
> \sqrt{(a + x)^2 + (a + y)^2} = a\sqrt2 + \frac{x + y}{\sqrt2} + O\Bigl(\frac{x^2 + y^2}{a}\Bigr),
> $$
>
> so its energy is $\tfrac12 k'\bigl(\tfrac{x + y}{\sqrt2}\bigr)^2 = \tfrac14 k'(x + y)^2$ up to cubic terms, and
>
> $$
> \mathsf M = m\,\mathbb 1, \qquad \mathsf K = \begin{pmatrix} k + k'/2 & k'/2 \\ k'/2 & k + k'/2 \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> The modes ([[§B6.5 Small Oscillations about Equilibrium#^thm-b6-5-3|Theorem §B6.5.3]]): $v = (1, 1)$, both masses moving outward together and stretching the coupling spring, with $\omega^2 = (k + k')/m$; and $v = (1, -1)$, one moving out while the other moves in, with $\omega^2 = k/m$: the coupling spring then only turns, and its length changes at second order, $\sqrt{2a^2 + 2x^2} - a\sqrt2 = O(x^2/a)$, so it exerts no linear restoring force. Only the first-order change in the length of a spring matters here because the spring is relaxed at equilibrium; a spring under tension $\tau_0$ at equilibrium would add $\tau_0$ times the second-order change in its length to $U$, a "geometric" stiffness.
>
> *Source: PHY 421, Final (Fall 2021), Problem 2*
