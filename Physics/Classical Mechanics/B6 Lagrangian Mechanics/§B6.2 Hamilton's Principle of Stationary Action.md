[[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations]] derived Lagrange's equations from Newton's second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]). They have exactly the form of the Euler–Lagrange equations of [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]], with time in place of $x$ and $L$ in place of $F$. This section reads them the other way: as the condition that a single number, the action, be stationary over whole histories. That is Hamilton's principle. Within its domain it says the same as Newton's laws, which remain the principles of level A; what it adds is a formulation that is visibly independent of coordinates, that fixes the Lagrangian only up to a total derivative, and that carries over to fields and to quantum mechanics. As in [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations]], $T$ is the kinetic energy (level A writes $K$) and $U$ the potential energy (Morin and the series write $V$).

## Hamilton's principle

> [!definition] Definition §B6.2.1: Action
> For a system with generalized coordinates $q = (q_1, \dots, q_n)$ and Lagrangian $L(q, \dot q, t)$ ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-4|Def. §B6.1.4]]), the **action** of a path $q(t)$, $t_1 \le t \le t_2$, is the functional
>
> $$
> S[q] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L\bigl(q(t), \dot q(t), t\bigr)\,dt .
> $$
>
> It is compared among $C^2$ paths with the same end configurations at the same times, $q(t_1) = q^{(1)}$ and $q(t_2) = q^{(2)}$. Its unit is energy × time, the unit of Planck's constant.
>
> *Source: Series II Definition 3.1, eq. (39) · Morin §6.2, eq. (6.14) · H&S §4.3, eq. (4.3) · Landau §2, eq. (2.1)*

^def-b6-2-1

> [!principle] Principle §B6.2.1: Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action
> Between a configuration $q^{(1)}$ at time $t_1$ and a configuration $q^{(2)}$ at time $t_2$, a mechanical system moves along a path that makes its action stationary:
>
> $$
> \delta S = \delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2} L(q, \dot q, t)\,dt = 0
> $$
>
> for all variations $\delta q(t)$ with $\delta q(t_1) = \delta q(t_2) = 0$ (in the sense of [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^def-b5-1-2|Def. §B5.1.2]]). For the systems of mechanics, $L = T - U$.
>
> *Domain: systems described by independent generalized coordinates, with ideal holonomic constraints ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^mod-b6-1-1|Model §B6.1.1]]) and with all other forces derived from a potential energy $U(q, t)$, or from a velocity-dependent potential such as that of a charge in an electromagnetic field; $T$ is the kinetic energy in an inertial frame. Speeds much less than $c$ for $L = T - U$ (a relativistic particle has another Lagrangian). Not covered: sliding friction and other dissipative forces, non-holonomic constraints.*
>
> *Source: Series II Postulate 3.2 · Morin §6.2 (the principle of stationary action) · H&S §4.3, eq. (4.4) · Landau §2*

^pr-b6-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.2: Hamilton's Principle Is Equivalent to Lagrange's Equations
> If $L$ is of class $C^2$, a $C^2$ path $q(t)$ with the given end configurations makes the action stationary if and only if it satisfies Lagrange's equations
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = 0, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n .
> $$
>
> *Source: Series II §3.1, eq. (40) · Morin §6.2, Theorem 6.1 · H&S §4.3, eq. (4.5) · Landau §2, eqs. (2.4)–(2.6)*

^thm-b6-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> This is [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]] with the independent variable $x$ renamed $t$, the unknown functions $y_i$ renamed $q_i$, and the integrand $F$ renamed $L$. The generalized coordinates are independent ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]]), so the variations $\delta q_i$ may be chosen separately, as that theorem requires.

^der-b6-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-4|Theorem §B5.1.4]], [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-2|Def. §B6.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Hamilton's principle and Newton's laws
> - *Within the domain they are equivalent.* By [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-2|Theorem §B6.2.2]], Hamilton's principle holds exactly when Lagrange's equations hold, and by [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]] Lagrange's equations hold for every Newtonian motion under ideal constraints and conservative forces. For unconstrained particles in Cartesian coordinates they *are* Newton's second law, $m_a\ddot{\vec r}_a = -\nabla_aV$. Conversely, Lagrange's equations with given initial $(q, \dot q)$ have only one solution (they can be solved for $\ddot q$, since $\mathsf M$ is invertible, [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]), and the Newtonian motion with the same initial data is one, so it is the solution. So either can be taken as the starting point; the other follows. The formulation adds no new mechanics. There is no circle: [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations]] uses only Newton's laws, and the present section uses only [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation]].
> - *The other laws.* Newton's first law enters through the requirement that $T$ be measured in an inertial frame ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]). The third law comes out of the form of $U$: if two particles interact through $U(|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|)$, then $-\nabla_1U = +\nabla_2U$, an equal and opposite pair ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|Principle §A3.5.1]]), and total momentum is conserved.
> - *The layers in this vault.* Newton's laws remain the principles of Classical Mechanics level A, and [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]] derives Lagrange's equations from them. Hamilton's principle is stated here as a principle in its own right, of the same content within its domain, because it is the form in which mechanics generalises: to fields, to relativity, and to quantum mechanics, where Morin and H&S argue it is the more fundamental of the two.
>
> *Source: Series II §3 (opening) and §3.1 · Morin §6.2 (after Theorem 6.1), Remark 7 · H&S §4.3 (points 1–4), §4.5*

^rem-b6-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why $T - U$?
> - *Because it works.* The sign is part of the principle, justified by the check above: $T - U$ reproduces $\vec F = m\vec a$. With $T + U$ the force would change sign.
> - *Symmetry fixes the free particle.* In an inertial frame space is homogeneous and isotropic and time homogeneous, so a free particle's Lagrangian can depend only on $v^2$. Requiring that a Galilean boost change $L$ only by a total time derivative (which is allowed, [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-4|Theorem §B6.2.4]]) forces $L \propto v^2$; the constant is $\tfrac12 m$ and defines the mass. Interactions are then described by subtracting a function of the positions, $-U$ (Landau §3–5).
> - *Relativity suggests it.* The relativistic free particle extremises its proper time, $\int d\tau = \int\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}\,dt$; at low speed this is, up to a constant factor and an additive constant, $\int\tfrac12 mv^2\,dt$, and in a weak uniform gravitational field the equivalence principle adds $-\int mgy\,dt$ (H&S §3.6–3.8).
> - *Feel.* For a thrown ball, the integrand is lowered by spending time high up, where $U$ is large, but getting there and back quickly costs kinetic energy. The parabola is the balance, struck at every instant.
>
> *Source: Series II Remark 3.3 · Landau §3–5 · H&S §3.6–3.8*

^rem-b6-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: What is held fixed
> The principle compares paths with the same end configurations *and* the same end times; it is a boundary-value problem, whereas a motion is usually specified by initial position and velocity. Both descriptions use two pieces of data per coordinate, and the end time of an actual motion is whatever it turns out to be: for that time and those end positions, the actual path is stationary. Nature is not choosing among histories by looking ahead; the stationarity condition is local, an equation at each instant.
>
> *Source: Morin §6.2, Remarks 1 and 5*

^rem-b6-2-3

## Stationary, not least

The traditional name "principle of least action" overstates. For a Lagrangian $T - U(q)$ the action is never a maximum: a small, fast wiggle added to the path raises the kinetic term without changing the potential term much. It is a minimum along sufficiently short stretches of the path (Landau's footnote to §2), but over longer times it can be a saddle.

> [!example] Example §B6.2.1: In a Uniform Field the True Path Minimises the Action
> Let a particle of mass $m$ move in a potential that is linear in position, $U = -\vec F\cdot\vec r$ (uniform gravity, $\vec F = m\vec g$; or no force). Vary the true path $\vec r(t)$ to $\vec r + \varepsilon\vec\eta$ with $\vec\eta(t_1) = \vec\eta(t_2) = \vec 0$. Since $T$ is quadratic in the velocity and $U$ linear in position, the expansion stops at second order:
>
> $$
> S[\vec r + \varepsilon\vec\eta] = S[\vec r] + \varepsilon\,\delta S[\vec r; \vec\eta] + \frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}\int_{t_1}^{t_2} m\,|\dot{\vec\eta}|^2\,dt .
> $$
>
> On the true path $\delta S = 0$, so $S[\vec r + \varepsilon\vec\eta] - S[\vec r] = \tfrac12\varepsilon^2 m\int|\dot{\vec\eta}|^2\,dt > 0$ for every nonzero variation: the true path is the global minimum among paths with the same ends and times. For a ball dropped from rest, $y = -gt^2/2$ on $0 \le t \le 1$, and the variation $\varepsilon\,t(t - 1)$, this gives $\Delta S = \tfrac12 m\varepsilon^2\int_0^1(2t - 1)^2\,dt = m\varepsilon^2/6$.
>
> *Source: Morin Exercises 6.30, 6.32 (solved here) · H&S §3.7*

> [!example] Example §B6.2.2: The Oscillator: Minimum or Saddle
> For $L = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 - \tfrac12 kx^2$ on $0 \le t \le T$, the action is quadratic, so for a variation $\xi(t)$ with $\xi(0) = \xi(T) = 0$ about the true path
>
> $$
> \Delta S = \frac12\int_0^T \bigl(m\dot\xi^2 - k\xi^2\bigr)\,dt
> $$
>
> exactly (the first-order terms vanish after an integration by parts, by the equation of motion). Expand $\xi = \sum_{n \ge 1} c_n\sin(n\pi t/T)$ ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-7|WO Theorem §B4.1.7]]). The cross terms integrate to zero, and
>
> $$
> \Delta S = \frac14\sum_{n \ge 1} c_n^2\Bigl(\frac{m\pi^2n^2}{T} - kT\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Every bracket is positive if $T < \pi\sqrt{m/k} = \tau/2$, with $\tau = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ the period: then the true path is a minimum. If $T > \tau/2$, the $n = 1$ variation lowers the action while high-$n$ wiggles raise it: a saddle. The action is never a maximum. Over a stretch shorter than half a period, a variation cannot grow large without being steep, and the kinetic cost wins.
>
> *Source: Morin §6.2, Remark 4, eqs. (6.24)–(6.26); Problem 6.6 and its solution, eq. (6.134)*

## Covariance

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.3: Covariance of Lagrange's Equations under Point Transformations
> Let $q_i = q_i(Q_1, \dots, Q_n, t)$ be a $C^2$ change of coordinates (possibly time-dependent) with invertible Jacobian matrix $\partial q_i/\partial Q_j$, and let $\tilde L(Q, \dot Q, t) = L\bigl(q(Q, t), \dot q(Q, \dot Q, t), t\bigr)$ be the same Lagrangian written in the new coordinates. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\tilde L}{\partial\dot Q_j} - \frac{\partial\tilde L}{\partial Q_j} = \sum_i \Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}\Bigr)\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial Q_j} .
> $$
>
> Hence a path satisfies Lagrange's equations in the $q$'s if and only if it satisfies them in the $Q$'s. This holds for any $C^2$ function $L$, not only for $T - U$.
>
> *Source: Series II Theorem 2.11 and App. C, eq. (230) · Morin §6.4, Claim 6.2*

^thm-b6-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Velocities transform by the chain rule, $\dot q_i = \sum_j (\partial q_i/\partial Q_j)\,\dot Q_j + \partial q_i/\partial t$, and exactly as in [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]] (with $\vec r_a \to q_i$ and $q \to Q$):
>
> $$
> \text{(i)}\ \ \frac{\partial\dot q_i}{\partial\dot Q_j} = \frac{\partial q_i}{\partial Q_j}, \qquad \text{(ii)}\ \ \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial Q_j} = \frac{\partial\dot q_i}{\partial Q_j} .
> $$
>
> By the chain rule and (i), $\partial\tilde L/\partial\dot Q_j = \sum_i (\partial L/\partial\dot q_i)\,\partial q_i/\partial Q_j$, since $q$ does not depend on $\dot Q$. Differentiating in $t$ and using (ii),
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\tilde L}{\partial\dot Q_j} = \sum_i \Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\Bigr)\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial Q_j} + \sum_i \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\,\frac{\partial\dot q_i}{\partial Q_j} .
> $$
>
> The coordinate slot gives $\partial\tilde L/\partial Q_j = \sum_i (\partial L/\partial q_i)\,\partial q_i/\partial Q_j + \sum_i (\partial L/\partial\dot q_i)\,\partial\dot q_i/\partial Q_j$. Subtracting, the terms with $\partial\dot q_i/\partial Q_j$ cancel and the identity remains. Since the Jacobian matrix is invertible, the left side vanishes for all $j$ exactly when the brackets vanish for all $i$.

*Uses:* [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]], [[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]], [[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why coordinates do not matter
> The statement "this path makes $\int L\,dt$ stationary" never mentions coordinates: the action is a number attached to a path, and relabelling the points of configuration space relabels the paths without changing their actions. The Euler–Lagrange expression transforms with the Jacobian, as the components of a covector do. Newton's equations, by contrast, keep their simple form only in Cartesian coordinates of an inertial frame; in polar coordinates or a rotating frame, extra terms appear. In the Lagrangian method one writes $T$ once in an inertial frame and changes to any convenient coordinates, including time-dependent ones such as coordinates that rotate with a turntable: the centrifugal and Coriolis terms then come out of the derivatives by themselves (compare [[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]]). This theorem is the licence for the phrase *generalized* coordinates.
>
> *Source: Series II §2.6 (after Theorem 2.11) · H&S §4.3 (point 1), eq. (4.6) · Morin §6.4*

## The freedom in the Lagrangian

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.4: Equivalent Lagrangians
> Let $c \ne 0$ be a constant and $\Lambda(q, t)$ a $C^3$ function of the coordinates and time. The Lagrangians
>
> $$
> L \qquad\text{and}\qquad L' = c\,L + \frac{d\Lambda}{dt}, \qquad \frac{d\Lambda}{dt} = \sum_i \frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial q_i}\,\dot q_i + \frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial t},
> $$
>
> give the same equations of motion. In particular, adding a constant to $U$, or adding to $L$ any function of $t$ alone, changes nothing.
>
> *Source: Landau §2, eq. (2.8); §4; §5 · Morin §6.1 (shift of the potential)*

^thm-b6-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Through the action.* $S'[q] = c\,S[q] + \Lambda(q^{(2)}, t_2) - \Lambda(q^{(1)}, t_1)$. The added term depends only on the fixed end data, so it does not change under variations with fixed ends; $\delta S' = c\,\delta S$, and the stationary paths are the same.
>
> *Directly.* $\partial(d\Lambda/dt)/\partial\dot q_i = \partial\Lambda/\partial q_i$, whose time derivative along a path is $\sum_j \frac{\partial^2\Lambda}{\partial q_j\,\partial q_i}\dot q_j + \frac{\partial^2\Lambda}{\partial t\,\partial q_i}$; and $\partial(d\Lambda/dt)/\partial q_i = \sum_j \frac{\partial^2\Lambda}{\partial q_i\,\partial q_j}\dot q_j + \frac{\partial^2\Lambda}{\partial q_i\,\partial t}$. They agree because mixed partials commute ([[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]), so the Euler–Lagrange expression of $d\Lambda/dt$ vanishes identically, for every path. Multiplying $L$ by $c$ multiplies its Euler–Lagrange expression by $c$.

^der-b6-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]], [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-2|Theorem §B6.2.2]], [[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the freedom changes
> The equations of motion are unchanged, but not every derived quantity is. The generalized momenta become $p_i' = c\,p_i + \partial\Lambda/\partial q_i$, and the energy function $\sum_i\dot q_i\,\partial L/\partial\dot q_i - L$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^def-b6-4-3|Def. §B6.4.3]]) becomes $c\,h - \partial\Lambda/\partial t$. So "the" Lagrangian of a system is a class of equivalent Lagrangians; $T - U$ is the conventional representative. (Morin's remark that the only Lagrangian for which the Euler–Lagrange equations hold is $T - U$ "or any constant multiple" is to be read up to such total derivatives.) The scale $c$ is fixed by the additivity of Lagrangians of independent subsystems: all must be scaled together, which amounts to the choice of a unit of mass (Landau §2, §4).
>
> *Source: Landau §2, §4*

^rem-b6-2-5

> [!example] Example §B6.2.3: A Pendulum with a Shaken Support
> The support of a simple pendulum (mass $m$, length $\ell$) is moved horizontally, $x_s = A\cos\Omega t$, in the plane of the swing. Find the equation of motion for the angle $\varphi$ from the vertical.
>
> With $y$ measured downward, the bob is at $x = A\cos\Omega t + \ell\sin\varphi$, $y = \ell\cos\varphi$, and $U = -mg\ell\cos\varphi$. From the Cartesian velocities,
>
> $$
> L = \tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\varphi^2 - mA\Omega\ell\,\sin\Omega t\,\cos\varphi\,\dot\varphi + \tfrac12 mA^2\Omega^2\sin^2\Omega t + mg\ell\cos\varphi .
> $$
>
> The third term depends on $t$ alone: drop it. In the second, $\cos\varphi\,\dot\varphi = \frac{d}{dt}\sin\varphi$, so
>
> $$
> -mA\Omega\ell\,\sin\Omega t\,\frac{d\sin\varphi}{dt} = -\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(mA\Omega\ell\,\sin\Omega t\,\sin\varphi\bigr) + mA\Omega^2\ell\,\cos\Omega t\,\sin\varphi ,
> $$
>
> and the total derivative can be dropped too ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-4|Theorem §B6.2.4]]). An equivalent Lagrangian is
>
> $$
> L' = \tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\varphi^2 + mA\Omega^2\ell\,\cos\Omega t\,\sin\varphi + mg\ell\cos\varphi ,
> $$
>
> with the equation of motion
>
> $$
> \ell\ddot\varphi = -g\sin\varphi + A\Omega^2\cos\Omega t\,\cos\varphi .
> $$
>
> The new term is the potential of the inertial force $-m\ddot x_s = mA\Omega^2\cos\Omega t$ that the bob feels in the frame of the support ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-3|Def. §A3.6.3]]). For small angles, $\ddot\varphi + \omega_0^2\varphi = (A\Omega^2/\ell)\cos\Omega t$ with $\omega_0^2 = g/\ell$: an undriven pendulum turned into a driven oscillator ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-3|WO Theorem §B1.3.3]]), with steady response $\varphi = \dfrac{A\Omega^2/\ell}{\omega_0^2 - \Omega^2}\cos\Omega t$ plus free oscillations at $\omega_0$.
>
> *Source: Landau §5, Problem 3(b) · Morin Problem 6.3 and its solution (solved here)*

^ex-b6-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Velocity-dependent potentials
> The domain of Hamilton's principle includes forces that depend on velocity, provided they come from a generalized potential. The standard case is a charge $q$ in electromagnetic potentials $\phi$, $\vec A$:
>
> $$
> L = \tfrac12 m|\dot{\vec r}|^2 - q\phi(\vec r, t) + q\,\dot{\vec r}\cdot\vec A(\vec r, t) ,
> $$
>
> whose Lagrange equations give the Lorentz force $q\vec E + q\vec v\times\vec B$ ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|EM Principle §A7.1.1]]), with $\vec E = -\nabla\phi - \partial\vec A/\partial t$ and $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$. The canonical momentum is $m\dot{\vec r} + q\vec A$, not $m\dot{\vec r}$. A gauge transformation of the potentials, $\vec A \to \vec A + \nabla\chi$, $\phi \to \phi - \partial\chi/\partial t$, changes $L$ by exactly $q\,d\chi/dt$: the electromagnetic gauge freedom is the freedom of [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-4|Theorem §B6.2.4]].
>
> *Source: Series II §11 (Outlook: the Lagrangian stated at pointer level; the gauge statement is checked here)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Hamilton's principle is the Euler–Lagrange theory of [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation]] with $x \to t$; covariance is the chain rule ([[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]) plus [[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]. The sine expansion in [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^ex-b6-2-2|Example §B6.2.2]] is complete in the sense of Parseval ([[§27 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-27-8|556 Thm. §27.8]], [[§27 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-27-11|556 Thm. §27.11]]).
> - Quantum mechanics: in Feynman's formulation every path contributes a phase $e^{iS/\hbar}$, and the classical path is the one near which the phases do not cancel, a stationary-phase argument (Morin §6.2, Remark 2; Quantum Mechanics level C — [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^thm-c4-2-6|QM Theorem §C4.2.6]], [[§C4.2 The Feynman Path Integral#^rem-c4-2-2|QM Remark: Hamilton's principle, from axiom to consequence]]). This is why stationarity, not minimality, is what matters.
> - Relativity: the free particle's Lagrangian $-mc^2\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ gives the momentum $\gamma m\vec v$ ([[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|REL Def. §A3.1.1]]); H&S §3.6.
