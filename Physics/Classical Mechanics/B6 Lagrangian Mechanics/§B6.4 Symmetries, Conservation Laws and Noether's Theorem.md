Level A obtained the three great conservation laws from Newton's laws: momentum from the third law ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]), angular momentum from the third law with central internal forces ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-4|Theorem §A6.6.4]]), and mechanical energy when no non-conservative force does work ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]]). Their Newtonian proofs at level B, from the equations of motion and not from symmetry, are [[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]], [[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]] and [[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]] for one particle and [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^thm-b3-1-1|Theorem §B3.1.1]] for a system; this section does not repeat them. In Lagrangian mechanics ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]], [[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|Principle §B6.2.1]]) each of them is the shadow of a symmetry of the Lagrangian: invariance under translations, rotations and shifts of time. This section proves Noether's theorem, which turns any continuous symmetry into a conserved quantity, and finds the conserved quantity of time translation, the energy function $h$, which is often but not always $T + U$.

*Notation.* $T$ is the kinetic energy (level A writes $K$) and $U$ the potential energy (Morin and the series write $V$). Because $L$ denotes angular momentum here ($L_z$, $\vec L$), the Lagrangian is written $\mathcal L$ in this section.

## Cyclic coordinates

> [!definition] Definition §B6.4.1: Cyclic Coordinate
> A generalized coordinate $q_k$ is **cyclic** (or **ignorable**) if it does not appear in the Lagrangian $\mathcal L(q, \dot q, t)$, although its velocity $\dot q_k$ may: $\partial \mathcal L/\partial q_k = 0$. Its generalized momentum $p_k = \partial \mathcal L/\partial\dot q_k$ ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^def-b6-1-5|Def. §B6.1.5]]) is then called a **conserved momentum**; it need not have the units of linear momentum.
>
> *Source: Series II §3.3, eq. (51) · Morin §6.5.1 · H&S §6.3*

^def-b6-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.1: Cyclic Coordinates Have Conserved Momenta
> If $q_k$ is cyclic, its conjugate momentum is constant along every solution of Lagrange's equations:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q_k} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_k = \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_k} = \text{const}.
> $$
>
> *Source: Series II §3.3, eq. (52) · Morin §6.5.1, eq. (6.48) · H&S §6.3*

^thm-b6-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Lagrange's equation for $q_k$ ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]) reads $\dot p_k = \dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_k} = \dfrac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q_k} = 0$. This is the cyclic-variable first integral of the calculus of variations ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-5|Theorem §B5.1.5]]) in mechanical clothing.

^der-b6-4-1

*Uses:* [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^def-b6-4-1|Def. §B6.4.1]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Which momenta come out
> - A ball in uniform gravity, $\mathcal L = \tfrac12 m(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 + \dot z^2) - mgz$: $x$ and $y$ are cyclic, so $p_x = m\dot x$ and $p_y = m\dot y$ are conserved, $p_z$ is not.
> - A potential that depends only on the distance $\rho$ from the $z$ axis, in cylindrical coordinates, $\mathcal L = \tfrac12 m(\dot\rho^2 + \rho^2\dot\varphi^2 + \dot z^2) - U(\rho)$: both $z$ and $\varphi$ are cyclic, so $p_z = m\dot z$ and $p_\varphi = m\rho^2\dot\varphi = L_z$ are conserved. The momentum conjugate to an angle is an angular momentum.
> - A star in the field of a galaxy squashed along one axis: $U = U(\rho, z)$, so only $\varphi$ is cyclic and only $L_z$ about the symmetry axis is conserved; no component of linear momentum is.
>
> The pattern: a coordinate is cyclic when the environment (potential and constraints) does not change as that coordinate changes, that is, when the system has a symmetry.
>
> *Source: Morin §6.5.1 · H&S §6.3, Examples 6.5–6.6*

^rem-b6-4-1

> [!caution] Caution: Cyclic depends on the coordinates; eliminate in the energy, not in $\mathcal L$
> - Two particles on a rail with $\mathcal L = \tfrac12 m_1\dot q_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2\dot q_2^2 - U(q_1 - q_2)$ have no cyclic coordinate, yet total momentum is conserved: in centre-of-mass and relative coordinates the centre-of-mass position is cyclic. A symmetry need not show up as a missing coordinate, which is why Noether's theorem below is stated for general transformations (H&S §6.4).
> - For $\mathcal L = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2) - U(r)$, the conserved $p_\varphi = mr^2\dot\varphi = L$ may be substituted for $\dot\varphi$ in the conserved energy, giving the effective potential ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]]). Substituting $\dot\varphi = L/mr^2$ into $\mathcal L$ *before* varying flips the sign of the centrifugal term in the radial equation (Series II, Example 3.9). In Hamiltonian form the substitution is legitimate ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^ex-b7-1-1|Example §B7.1.1]]).

## Noether's theorem

> [!definition] Definition §B6.4.2: Continuous Symmetry
> Let $\eta_i(q, \dot q, t)$, $i = 1, \dots, n$, be given functions, and consider the infinitesimal change $q_i \to q_i + \varepsilon\,\eta_i$ of an arbitrary curve $q(t)$, so that $\dot q_i \to \dot q_i + \varepsilon\,\dot\eta_i$ with $\dot\eta_i = d\eta_i/dt$ along the curve. It is a **symmetry** of $\mathcal L$ if, for *every* curve, the first-order change of $\mathcal L$ is a total time derivative:
>
> $$
> \delta \mathcal L \equiv \sum_i\Bigl(\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q_i}\,\eta_i + \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_i}\,\dot\eta_i\Bigr) = \frac{dK}{dt}
> $$
>
> for some function $K(q, \dot q, t)$. If $K = 0$, $\mathcal L$ itself is unchanged to first order: a **strict** symmetry. A one-parameter family $q \mapsto Q(q, s)$ with $Q(q, 0) = q$ that leaves $\mathcal L$ invariant for all $s$ gives a strict symmetry with $\eta_i = \partial Q_i/\partial s\,|_{s=0}$; invariance to first order in $s$ is all that is used. Adding a total derivative $dK/dt$ to a Lagrangian does not change its equations of motion ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^thm-b6-2-4|Theorem §B6.2.4]]), which is why such a change still counts as a symmetry.
>
> *Source: Morin §6.6, eq. (6.58) (strict, first order) · Series II §3.3, Theorem 3.7 (finite family) · H&S §6.6–6.7, eq. (6.117) (total derivative)*

^def-b6-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.2: Noether's Theorem
> If $\eta$ is a symmetry of $\mathcal L$ with total derivative $dK/dt$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^def-b6-4-2|Def. §B6.4.2]]), then
>
> $$
> I = \sum_i p_i\,\eta_i - K, \qquad p_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_i},
> $$
>
> is constant along every solution of Lagrange's equations. For a strict point symmetry, $\eta = \eta(q)$ and $K = 0$, the conserved quantity is $I = \sum_i p_i\,\eta_i(q)$.
>
> *Source: Series II §3.3, Theorem 3.7 · Morin §6.6, Theorem 6.4 · H&S §6.7, eqs. (6.113), (6.116)*

^thm-b6-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The symmetry condition holds for every curve, by assumption. Evaluate it on a solution, where Lagrange's equations ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]) replace $\partial \mathcal L/\partial q_i$ by $\dot p_i$:
>
> $$
> \frac{dK}{dt} = \sum_i\bigl(\dot p_i\,\eta_i + p_i\,\dot\eta_i\bigr) = \frac{d}{dt}\sum_i p_i\,\eta_i .
> $$
>
> Hence $dI/dt = 0$. For a family $Q(q, s)$ that leaves $\mathcal L$ invariant for all $s$, differentiate $\mathcal L(Q, \dot Q, t) = \mathcal L(q, \dot q, t)$ with respect to $s$ at $s = 0$; since $s$- and $t$-derivatives commute, $\partial\dot Q_i/\partial s\,|_0 = \dot\eta_i$, and the chain rule ([[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]) gives the condition with $K = 0$.

^der-b6-4-2

*Uses:* [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^def-b6-4-2|Def. §B6.4.2]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]], [[§10 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-10-2|452 Thm. §10.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the theorem gives, and what it does not
> - A cyclic coordinate is the special case $\eta_k = 1$, all other $\eta_i = 0$: then $I = p_k$, and [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-1|Theorem §B6.4.1]] is Noether's theorem for a translation in configuration space.
> - Morin's example: $\mathcal L = \tfrac m2(5\dot x^2 - 2\dot x\dot y + 2\dot y^2) + C(2x - y)$ has no cyclic coordinate, but is invariant under $x \to x + \varepsilon$, $y \to y + 2\varepsilon$ (so $\eta = (1, 2)$). The conserved quantity is $p_x + 2p_y = m(5\dot x - \dot y) + 2m(-\dot x + 2\dot y) = 3m(\dot x + \dot y)$. Adding the $x$ equation to twice the $y$ equation gives $3m(\ddot x + \ddot y) = 0$ directly: a conserved quantity is a first integral of Lagrange's equations and contains no information beyond them, but it is one integration closer to the solution.
> - A conserved quantity depends on $q$ and $\dot q$ (not on $\ddot q$), and its value depends on the initial conditions; otherwise every equation of motion would trivially "conserve" something.
> - The theorem needs a Lagrangian. Friction has none of the form $T - U$, and energy conservation fails accordingly; but see [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^ex-b6-4-2|Example §B6.4.2]].
> - Discrete symmetries (reflections, lattice rotations) are not continuous families and are not covered.
>
> *Source: Morin §6.6, Remarks 1–7 · H&S §6.8*

^rem-b6-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: A screw symmetry in one line
> The field of an infinite homogeneous helix of pitch $b$ along the $z$ axis is unchanged by no translation and no rotation alone, but by a rotation $\delta\varphi$ about the axis combined with a shift $b\,\delta\varphi/2\pi$ along it. In Cartesian coordinates this is the strict point symmetry $\vec\eta = (-y,\ x,\ b/2\pi)$, and [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]] gives at once the conserved
>
> $$
> I = -p_x\,y + p_y\,x + \frac{b}{2\pi}\,p_z = L_z + \frac{b}{2\pi}\,P_z ,
> $$
>
> neither term being conserved alone. This is Landau's argument; the same result by the Newtonian route, with the other partial symmetries of Landau's list, is [[§B1.2 Conservation Theorems for a Particle#^ex-b1-2-2|Example §B1.2.2]].
>
> *Source: Landau §9, Problem 3*

## The energy function

> [!definition] Definition §B6.4.3: Energy Function
> The **energy function** of a Lagrangian $\mathcal L(q, \dot q, t)$ is
>
> $$
> h(q, \dot q, t) = \sum_i \dot q_i\,\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_i} - \mathcal L = \sum_i p_i\,\dot q_i - \mathcal L .
> $$
>
> Morin calls it $E$, Landau the energy, H&S already $H$; rewritten as a function of $(q, p, t)$ it becomes the Hamiltonian ([[§B7.1 The Legendre Transform and Hamilton's Equations#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: Series II §3.3, eq. (53) · Morin §6.5.2, eq. (6.52) · Landau §6, eq. (6.1) · H&S §6.7, eq. (6.136)*

^def-b6-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.3: Energy Function and Time Translation
> Along every solution of Lagrange's equations,
>
> $$
> \frac{dh}{dt} = -\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> In particular, if $\mathcal L$ has no explicit time dependence, $h$ is conserved. It is the Noether quantity of the time translation $q(t) \to q(t + \varepsilon)$, that is, of $\eta_i = \dot q_i$ with $K = \mathcal L$.
>
> *Source: Series II §3.3, Proposition 3.6 · Morin §6.5.2, Claim 6.3 · Landau §6 · H&S §6.7, Example 6.11*

^thm-b6-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Directly.* By the chain rule along the trajectory,
>
> $$
> \frac{dh}{dt} = \sum_i\Bigl(\ddot q_i\,\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_i} + \dot q_i\,\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_i}\Bigr) - \Bigl[\sum_i\Bigl(\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q_i}\,\dot q_i + \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_i}\,\ddot q_i\Bigr) + \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial t}\Bigr] = \sum_i \dot q_i\Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_i} - \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial q_i}\Bigr) - \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial t},
> $$
>
> and the bracket vanishes on a solution. This is the Beltrami identity ([[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-6|Theorem §B5.1.6]]) in $n$ variables, with "no explicit $t$" in place of "no explicit $x$".
>
> *As a Noether quantity.* Shifting the curve in time, $q_i(t) \to q_i(t + \varepsilon) = q_i + \varepsilon\dot q_i + O(\varepsilon^2)$, is the change with $\eta_i = \dot q_i$. For any curve, $\delta \mathcal L = \sum_i(\partial_{q_i}\mathcal L\,\dot q_i + \partial_{\dot q_i}\mathcal L\,\ddot q_i) = dL/dt - \partial \mathcal L/\partial t$, which is $dK/dt$ with $K = \mathcal L$ exactly when $\partial \mathcal L/\partial t = 0$. [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]] then gives the conserved $I = \sum_i p_i\dot q_i - \mathcal L = h$. Allowing $\eta$ to depend on $\dot q$ and $\delta \mathcal L$ to be a total derivative is what fits time translation into the same theorem as the spatial symmetries; the series' point-symmetry version (Theorem 3.7) excludes it and treats energy separately. H&S phrase the same computation as a shift $\delta t = \varepsilon$ of the time coordinate, with charge $-h$.

^der-b6-4-3

*Uses:* [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^def-b6-4-3|Def. §B6.4.3]], [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]], [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^thm-b5-1-6|Theorem §B5.1.6]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.4: When the Energy Function Is T + U
> Let the positions of the particles be given by $\vec r_a = \vec r_a(q, t)$, so that the kinetic energy splits into parts of degree 2, 1 and 0 in the velocities, $T = T_2 + T_1 + T_0$ with $T_2 = \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}M_{ij}(q, t)\,\dot q_i\dot q_j$ ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]), and let the potential energy $U(q, t)$ not depend on the velocities. Then
>
> $$
> h = T_2 - T_0 + U .
> $$
>
> In particular, if the relations $\vec r_a(q)$ do not involve $t$ (time-independent constraints and coordinates), then $T = T_2$ and $h = T + U$, the mechanical energy.
>
> *Source: Series II §6.3, Proposition 6.4 and Remark 6.5 · Morin §15.1, Theorem 15.1 and eq. (15.6) · Landau §6, eq. (6.2)*

^thm-b6-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A function $f_k$ homogeneous of degree $k$ in the velocities satisfies Euler's relation $\sum_i\dot q_i\,\partial f_k/\partial\dot q_i = k\,f_k$. For $T_2$ it is a one-line check with the symmetric $\mathsf M$: $\partial T_2/\partial\dot q_i = \sum_j M_{ij}\dot q_j$, so $\sum_i\dot q_i\,\partial T_2/\partial\dot q_i = \sum_{ij}M_{ij}\dot q_i\dot q_j = 2T_2$; for $T_1 = \sum_i b_i\dot q_i$ it gives $T_1$, for $T_0$ zero. Since $U$ carries no velocities, $\sum_i\dot q_i\,\partial \mathcal L/\partial\dot q_i = 2T_2 + T_1$, and
>
> $$
> h = 2T_2 + T_1 - (T_2 + T_1 + T_0 - U) = T_2 - T_0 + U .
> $$
>
> If $\partial\vec r_a/\partial t = 0$, then $T_1 = T_0 = 0$ ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]), so $h = T + U$.

^der-b6-4-4

*Uses:* [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^def-b6-4-3|Def. §B6.4.3]], [[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-5|Theorem §B6.1.5]]

> [!caution] Caution: "Conserved" and "the energy" are different questions
> Conservation of $h$ needs $\partial \mathcal L/\partial t = 0$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]]); $h = T + U$ needs time-independent coordinate relations ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]]). All four combinations occur:
> - conserved and the energy: a closed system in fixed coordinates;
> - conserved but not the energy: a bead on a rod turned at constant rate by a motor ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^ex-b6-4-1|Example §B6.4.1]]);
> - the energy but not conserved: $\mathcal L = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 - U(x, t)$, a time-dependent field, $h = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + U(x, t)$;
> - neither: a bead on a horizontal rod pushed upward with constant acceleration $a$, $\mathcal L = \tfrac12 m(\dot x^2 + a^2t^2) - \tfrac12 mgat^2$, $h = \tfrac12 m(\dot x^2 - a^2t^2) + \tfrac12 mgat^2$.
>
> When the constraint moves, whatever drives it does work, so $T + U$ changes even when $h$ does not. Morin adds that $h$ is the energy only if the Lagrangian describes the whole closed system.
>
> *Source: Morin §15.1, Examples 2–3 and Remarks; §6.5.2, Remark · Series II §6.3, Remark 6.5*

^cau-b6-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Velocity-dependent potentials
> The charged particle has $\mathcal L = \tfrac12 m|\vec v|^2 - q\phi + q\,\vec v\cdot\vec A$ (SI units; Series II §11, stated; H&S Example 11.4 in Gaussian units), whose Lagrange equations give the Lorentz force ([[§A7.1 Magnetic Field and the Force on a Moving Charge#^pr-a7-1-1|EM Principle §A7.1.1]]). The term linear in $\vec v$ contributes $q\vec v\cdot\vec A - q\vec v\cdot\vec A = 0$ to $h$, so $h = \tfrac12 m|\vec v|^2 + q\phi$: the magnetic force does no work and does not enter the energy. [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]] assumed $U$ velocity-independent, but terms linear in the velocities always drop out of $h$ in this way.

## Momentum, angular momentum and energy of a closed system

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.4.5: Conservation Laws from Homogeneity and Isotropy
> Let a closed system of $N$ particles have
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = \sum_a \tfrac12 m_a|\dot{\vec r}_a|^2 - U(\vec r_1, \dots, \vec r_N),
> $$
>
> with $U$ unchanged when all particles are displaced by the same vector (**homogeneity of space**) and when all are rotated together about any axis through the origin (**isotropy of space**); $\mathcal L$ does not depend on $t$ (**homogeneity of time**). Then, along every motion:
> 1. the total momentum $\vec P = \sum_a m_a\dot{\vec r}_a$ is conserved, and the forces $\vec F_a = -\nabla_a U$ add to zero, $\sum_a\vec F_a = \vec 0$;
> 2. the total angular momentum $\vec L = \sum_a\vec r_a\times m_a\dot{\vec r}_a$ is conserved;
> 3. the energy $E = T + U$ is conserved;
> 4. the centre of mass $\vec X = \sum_a m_a\vec r_a/M$, $M = \sum_a m_a$, moves uniformly: $M\vec X - \vec P\,t$ is conserved.
>
> Landau counts $E$, $\vec P$ and $\vec L$ as the seven additive integrals of the motion of a closed system (their values for non-interacting parts add); the boost adds three conserved quantities that depend explicitly on $t$. If an external field has only part of the symmetry, only the matching components survive: a field symmetric about the $z$ axis conserves $L_z$ (about an origin on the axis), a field independent of $x$ conserves $P_x$.
>
> *Source: Landau §6–9 · H&S §6.7, Examples 6.10–6.13 · Series II §3.3, Example 3.8*

^thm-b6-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]] with the Cartesian components of all $\vec r_a$ as coordinates, so that $\vec p_a = \partial \mathcal L/\partial\dot{\vec r}_a = m_a\dot{\vec r}_a$, and a fixed unit vector $\hat n$.
>
> 1. *Translation*, $\vec\eta_a = \hat n$ for every $a$. The velocities do not change, and $\delta \mathcal L = -\sum_a\hat n\cdot\nabla_a U = 0$ by homogeneity, for every configuration. So $I = \sum_a\vec p_a\cdot\hat n = \vec P\cdot\hat n$ is conserved, for every $\hat n$. The same identity, $\sum_a\nabla_a U = \vec 0$, says $\sum_a\vec F_a = \vec 0$.
> 2. *Rotation*, $\vec\eta_a = \hat n\times\vec r_a$, the infinitesimal rotation about $\hat n$ (Landau, eq. (9.1)). The kinetic term changes by $\sum_a m_a\dot{\vec r}_a\cdot(\hat n\times\dot{\vec r}_a) = 0$, and $U$ by zero, by isotropy. So $I = \sum_a\vec p_a\cdot(\hat n\times\vec r_a) = \hat n\cdot\sum_a\vec r_a\times\vec p_a = \hat n\cdot\vec L$ is conserved.
> 3. *Time translation*: [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]], and $h = T + U$ by [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]], since Cartesian coordinates involve no $t$.
> 4. *Galilean boost*, $\vec\eta_a = t\,\hat n$ (every particle given the extra velocity $\varepsilon\hat n$). $U$ is unchanged by homogeneity, and the kinetic term changes by $\sum_a m_a\dot{\vec r}_a\cdot\hat n = \dfrac{d}{dt}(M\vec X\cdot\hat n)$. This is not zero but a total derivative, with $K = M\vec X\cdot\hat n$, and the conserved quantity is $I = \sum_a\vec p_a\cdot\hat n\,t - M\vec X\cdot\hat n = \hat n\cdot(\vec P t - M\vec X)$. With $\vec P$ constant, $\vec X(t) = \vec X(0) + (\vec P/M)\,t$.

> [!remark] Remark: The logic reversed
> At level A the third law was a principle ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|Principle §A3.5.1]]) and conservation of momentum a theorem derived from it ([[§A5.2 Conservation of Momentum#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]); conservation of angular momentum needed the extra assumption that internal forces act along the lines joining the particles ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^rem-a6-6-1|Remark: Law or theorem?]]). With Hamilton's principle as the starting point ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|Principle §B6.2.1]]), the order runs the other way: homogeneity of space gives conservation of momentum, and with it $\sum_a\vec F_a = \vec 0$, which for two particles is $\vec F_1 = -\vec F_2$, the third law in its weak form ([[§B3.1 Systems of Particles#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]]). Isotropy forces a two-particle potential $U(\vec r_1 - \vec r_2)$ to depend on $|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|$ only, so the pair force lies along the line joining the particles, the strong form: the assumption level A needed for angular momentum. The boost symmetry gives the uniform motion of the centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]]; systems of particles at level B: [[§B3.1 Systems of Particles]]); H&S note that this Noether quantity has no special name. Momentum is the conserved charge of translations, angular momentum of rotations, energy of time translation.
>
> *Source: Landau §7, eq. (7.4) · H&S §6.7, Examples 6.10, 6.13 · Series II §3.3, Example 3.8*

> [!remark] Remark: Noether in Hamiltonian form
> In the Hamiltonian formalism the relation becomes symmetric: the conserved $I = \sum_i p_i\eta_i(q)$, used as a Hamiltonian, generates the flow $dq_i/ds = \partial I/\partial p_i = \eta_i$, the symmetry itself. Momentum generates translations, $L_z$ generates rotations, $H$ generates time evolution; "conserved" and "generates a symmetry of $H$" become one statement about the Poisson bracket ([[§B8.1 Poisson Brackets#^thm-b8-1-5|Theorem §B8.1.5]]).
>
> *Source: Series II §8.3*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.4.1: A Bead on a Rotating Rod
> A bead of mass $m$ slides without friction on a rod in a horizontal plane; a motor turns the rod about one end at constant angular velocity $\omega$, and a spring of constant $k$ and zero relaxed length joins the bead to the pivot. Is $h$ conserved? Is it the energy?
>
> With $r$ the distance from the pivot, the bead's velocity has components $\dot r$ along the rod and $r\omega$ across it, so
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\omega^2) - \tfrac12 kr^2, \qquad h = \dot r\,\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial\dot r} - \mathcal L = \tfrac12 m\dot r^2 - \tfrac12 m\omega^2r^2 + \tfrac12 kr^2 .
> $$
>
> $\mathcal L$ has no explicit $t$, so $h$ is conserved ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]]). It is not the energy $E = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\omega^2) + \tfrac12 kr^2$: the sign of the $r^2\omega^2$ term is wrong, because $x = r\cos\omega t$, $y = r\sin\omega t$ involve $t$ and here $T_0 = \tfrac12 m\omega^2r^2$, $T_1 = 0$, so $h = T_2 - T_0 + U$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-4|Theorem §B6.4.4]]).
>
> Where the energy goes: $h = E - m\omega^2r^2 = E - \omega L_z$, with $L_z = mr^2\omega$ the bead's angular momentum about the pivot. The motor exerts the torque $\tau_z = dL_z/dt$ and delivers the power $\tau_z\omega = d(\omega L_z)/dt$, so $E$ changes by exactly as much as $\omega L_z$ does, and $E - \omega L_z$ stays constant. The equation of motion, $m\ddot r = (m\omega^2 - k)\,r$, makes the bead oscillate about the pivot if $k > m\omega^2$ and fly outward if $k < m\omega^2$.
>
> *Source: Morin §15.1, Example 2 (the interpretation $h = E - \omega L_z$ is added here)*

> [!example] Example §B6.4.2: A Lagrangian with Explicit Time Dependence
> Find the equation of motion of the one-dimensional system with
>
> $$
> \mathcal L = \tfrac12\,e^{\alpha t}\bigl(\dot x^2 - \omega^2x^2\bigr), \qquad \alpha > 0,
> $$
>
> and say what system it describes.
>
> $\partial \mathcal L/\partial\dot x = e^{\alpha t}\dot x$ and $\partial \mathcal L/\partial x = -e^{\alpha t}\omega^2x$, so Lagrange's equation is $\dfrac{d}{dt}\bigl(e^{\alpha t}\dot x\bigr) = -e^{\alpha t}\omega^2x$, that is,
>
> $$
> \ddot x + \alpha\,\dot x + \omega^2x = 0 :
> $$
>
> the damped harmonic oscillator, with damping rate $\gamma = \alpha$ and natural frequency $\omega_0 = \omega$ ([[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-1|WO Theorem §B1.2.1]]). The energy function is
>
> $$
> h = \dot x\,e^{\alpha t}\dot x - \mathcal L = \tfrac12\,e^{\alpha t}\bigl(\dot x^2 + \omega^2x^2\bigr),
> $$
>
> and it is not conserved, since $\mathcal L$ depends on $t$: $dh/dt = -\partial \mathcal L/\partial t = -\tfrac12\alpha\,e^{\alpha t}(\dot x^2 - \omega^2x^2)$ ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-3|Theorem §B6.4.3]]). The oscillator's energy per unit mass, $\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\dot x^2 + \omega^2x^2) = e^{-\alpha t}h$, obeys $d\varepsilon/dt = \dot x(\ddot x + \omega^2x) = -\alpha\dot x^2 \le 0$: it is dissipated. So friction can be described by a Lagrangian after all, but not by $\mathcal L = T - U$, and the Lagrangian is not unique; what Hamilton's principle with $\mathcal L = T - U$ cannot describe is dissipation ([[§B6.2 Hamilton's Principle of Stationary Action#^pr-b6-2-1|Principle §B6.2.1]]). Its phase-space picture is in [[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^ex-b7-2-3|Example §B7.2.3]].
>
> *Source: PHY 421, Midterm 2 (Fall 2021), Problem 3*
