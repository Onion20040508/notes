Bernoulli's equation ([[§A9.5 Bernoulli's Equation#^thm-a9-5-1|Theorem §A9.5.1]]) ignores friction. Real fluids have internal friction, **viscosity**, which drags neighbouring layers along, makes syrup pour slowly and requires a steady pressure difference just to keep a fluid moving through a level pipe. This section quantifies viscosity, gives the flow law for laminar flow in a tube (Poiseuille), and states when smooth flow gives way to turbulence (Reynolds number). It is the fluid counterpart of the friction and drag forces of [[§A3.8 Friction]] and [[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed]].

## Viscosity

> [!law] Law §A9.6.1: Viscous Force in Laminar Shear Flow
> A fluid layer of thickness $L$ lies between two parallel plates of area $A$. To keep the top plate moving at constant speed $v$ relative to the bottom one, a force
>
> $$
> F = \eta\,\frac{v A}{L}
> $$
>
> is needed, with a constant $\eta$ characteristic of the fluid (and its temperature). The fluid sticks to each plate, so its speed varies across the layer from $0$ to $v$.
>
> *Valid when:* the flow is laminar. At higher speeds the flow turns turbulent, and a much larger force with a more complicated dependence on $v$ is needed.
> *Source: UP1 §14.7*

^law-a9-6-1

> [!definition] Definition §A9.6.1: Coefficient of Viscosity
> The **coefficient of viscosity** $\eta$ of a fluid is the constant in [[§A9.6 Viscosity and Turbulence#^law-a9-6-1|Law §A9.6.1]]:
>
> $$
> \eta = \frac{F L}{v A}, \qquad [\eta] = \mathrm{N\cdot s/m^2} = \mathrm{Pa\cdot s}.
> $$
>
> Typical values: air $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$, water $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ (at $20\ ^\circ$C; $0.28 \times 10^{-3}$ at $100\ ^\circ$C), whole blood $2.1 \times 10^{-3}$ (at $37\ ^\circ$C), glycerin $1.5$, honey $2$–$10\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot s}$. Gases are far less viscous than liquids, and the viscosity of liquids falls steeply as they warm.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.7, eq. (14.17), Table 14.4*

^def-a9-6-1

> [!caution] Caution: Units in the viscosity table
> University Physics Table 14.4 heads its column "$\eta$ (Pa·s)", but the entries are in **mPa·s**: it lists water at $20\ ^\circ$C as 1.002 and air as 0.0181, and its own Example 14.8 uses $\eta_{\text{air}} = 0.0181\ \mathrm{mPa\cdot s}$. The values above are converted to Pa·s.

^cau-a9-6-1

## Laminar flow in a tube

> [!definition] Definition §A9.6.2: Resistance to Flow
> A pressure difference drives flow from high to low pressure. The **resistance** $R$ of a tube or channel is defined by
>
> $$
> Q = \frac{p_2 - p_1}{R} \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad p_2 - p_1 = R\,Q,
> $$
>
> where $Q$ is the volume flow rate ([[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^def-a9-4-2|Def. §A9.4.2]]) from the end at pressure $p_2$ to the end at pressure $p_1$. $R$ collects everything other than the pressure difference that affects the flow: it grows with length and viscosity, falls with width, and rises sharply with turbulence. For an ideal fluid $R = 0$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.7*

^def-a9-6-2

> [!law] Law §A9.6.2: Poiseuille's Law
> For laminar flow of an incompressible fluid of viscosity $\eta$ through a horizontal tube of radius $r$ and length $l$,
>
> $$
> R = \frac{8\,\eta\, l}{\pi r^4}, \qquad Q = \frac{(p_2 - p_1)\,\pi r^4}{8\,\eta\, l} .
> $$
>
> *Valid when:* the flow is laminar (Reynolds number below about 2000, [[§A9.6 Viscosity and Turbulence#^law-a9-6-3|Law §A9.6.3]]) and steady, through a long straight tube of uniform radius.
> *Source: UP1 §14.7, eqs. (14.18)–(14.19)*

^law-a9-6-2

![[ph-cm-a9-6-1.svg]]
*Velocity across a tube. Left: an ideal fluid slides through at the same speed everywhere (blue). Right: a viscous fluid in laminar flow sticks to the walls; its speed (red) is zero there and greatest on the axis. Poiseuille's law is the flow rate of such a profile.*

> [!remark] Remark: The fourth power, and pressure drops
> - Doubling the tube's length halves the flow, but halving its radius cuts the flow to $1/2^4 = 1/16$. Small changes of radius dominate: a slightly narrowed artery, or a partly closed tap, changes the flow enormously.
> - Read $p_2 - p_1 = RQ$ the other way: flow through a resistance *causes* a pressure drop, for laminar and turbulent flow alike. On hot summer days, when the flow $Q$ in a water main is large, the drop between the waterworks and the houses is large, and the pressure at the taps falls. For a given $Q$ the drop is greatest where the tube is narrowest.

^rem-a9-6-1

> [!example] Example §A9.6.1: Ventilating a Room
> An air-conditioning duct of diameter 18.00 cm and length 20 m supplies air at a gauge pressure of 0.054 Pa into a room at atmospheric pressure. The air is at $20\ ^\circ$C ($\eta = 1.81 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot s}$); assume laminar flow. By [[§A9.6 Viscosity and Turbulence#^law-a9-6-2|Law §A9.6.2]], with $r = 0.0900\ \mathrm m$,
>
> $$
> Q = \frac{(0.054\ \mathrm{Pa})\,\pi\,(0.0900\ \mathrm m)^4}{8\,(1.81 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot s})(20\ \mathrm m)} = 3.84 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}.
> $$
>
> Replacing the air of a $12\ \mathrm m \times 6\ \mathrm m \times 3\ \mathrm m$ room ($216\ \mathrm{m^3}$) then takes $216/(3.84 \times 10^{-3})\ \mathrm s = 5.6 \times 10^4\ \mathrm s \approx 16$ hours. A duct of half the diameter, chosen to save money, would carry only $(1/2)^4 = 1/16$ as much: $2.40 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 14.8*

^ex-a9-6-1

## Turbulence

> [!definition] Definition §A9.6.3: Reynolds Number
> For flow at average speed $v$ through a tube of radius $r$, the **Reynolds number** is the dimensionless combination
>
> $$
> N_R = \frac{2\,\rho\, v\, r}{\eta},
> $$
>
> with $\rho$ the fluid's density and $\eta$ its viscosity.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.7, eq. (14.20)*

^def-a9-6-3

> [!law] Law §A9.6.3: Onset of Turbulence
> Flow in a tube is laminar for $N_R$ below about 2000 and turbulent above about 3000. Between these values it is unstable: it may be laminar, but small obstructions or roughness can make it turbulent, and it may switch unpredictably between the two.
>
> *Valid when:* the flow is through a tube of uniform radius; the thresholds are empirical and approximate.
> *Source: UP1 §14.7*

^law-a9-6-3

> [!remark] Remark: Turbulence and chaos
> Turbulence has two main causes: obstructions and sharp corners, which give the fluid velocity components across the flow, and high speed. It mixes the fluid, dissipates energy as heat and greatly increases the resistance $R$. That is why designers try to avoid it, and why smoke rising from a candle slows down once it starts to swirl. In the unstable range the outcome depends so sensitively on small details that it is practically unpredictable, an example of **chaotic** behaviour (Chapter C4, planned).

^rem-a9-6-2

> [!example] Example §A9.6.2: Is the Duct Flow Laminar?
> Check the assumption of [[§A9.6 Viscosity and Turbulence#^ex-a9-6-1|Example §A9.6.1]]. The average speed in the 18-cm duct is
>
> $$
> v = \frac{Q}{\pi r^2} = \frac{3.84 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}}{\pi (0.0900\ \mathrm m)^2} = 0.151\ \mathrm{m/s},
> $$
>
> so with $\rho_{\text{air}} = 1.23\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$
>
> $$
> N_R = \frac{2\,(1.23\ \mathrm{kg/m^3})(0.151\ \mathrm{m/s})(0.0900\ \mathrm m)}{1.81 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot s}} \approx 1.8 \times 10^3 < 2000 .
> $$
>
> The flow is laminar, but only just. Setting $N_R = 2000$ gives the largest laminar speed, $v = 2000\,\eta/(2\rho r) = 0.16\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Pushing more air through this duct would make the flow turbulent and the system less efficient, and Poiseuille's law would no longer apply.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 14.9*

^ex-a9-6-2

> [!remark] Remark: Bodies moving through a fluid
> Viscosity also acts on bodies moving through a fluid. At low speeds the drag is proportional to the speed, at high speeds roughly to its square, and a falling body reaches a terminal speed where drag balances weight minus buoyancy ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed]], [[§A9.3 Buoyancy#^thm-a9-3-1|Theorem §A9.3.1]]).
