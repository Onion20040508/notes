Why does a steel ship float while a steel bolt sinks, and why does an anchor feel lighter under water? Pressure grows with depth ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-3|Theorem §A9.1.3]]), so the fluid pushes harder on the bottom of a submerged body than on its top. This section shows that the resulting upward force is exactly the weight of the displaced fluid, and turns that into a rule for floating and sinking.

## Archimedes' principle

> [!definition] Definition §A9.3.1: Buoyant Force
> The **buoyant force** $\vec F_B$ on a body wholly or partly immersed in a fluid is the net force exerted on it by the pressure of the surrounding fluid. It points up.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.4*

^def-a9-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.3.1: Archimedes' Principle
> The buoyant force on a body in a fluid at rest equals the weight of the fluid the body displaces:
>
> $$
> F_B = w_{\text{fl}} = \rho_{\text{fl}}\, V_{\text{disp}}\, g ,
> $$
>
> where $V_{\text{disp}}$ is the volume of the body below the fluid surface (all of it, if fully submerged).
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.4*

^thm-a9-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Replacement argument.* The force the fluid exerts on the body depends only on the pressure at each point of the body's surface. In a fluid at rest that pressure is fixed by depth ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-1|Theorem §A9.1.1]]), whatever is inside the surface. Replace the body by fluid of exactly the same shape. That parcel of fluid is at rest, so the pressure forces on its surface balance its weight $w_{\text{fl}}$: they add up to $w_{\text{fl}}$, pointing up. The body feels the same surface forces, so $F_B = w_{\text{fl}}$.
>
> *Check for a block.* Let a block with horizontal top and bottom of area $A$ lie between depths $h_1$ and $h_2$. On the vertical sides, pressures at equal heights are equal and opposite, so the side forces cancel. By [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-3|Theorem §A9.1.3]] the bottom is pushed up with $(p_0 + \rho_{\text{fl}} g h_2)A$ and the top down with $(p_0 + \rho_{\text{fl}} g h_1)A$, so
>
> $$
> F_B = \rho_{\text{fl}}\, g\,(h_2 - h_1)A = \rho_{\text{fl}}\, g\, V .
> $$

^der-a9-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-1|Theorem §A9.1.1]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-3|Theorem §A9.1.3]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^rem-a9-1-2|the remark on the direction of pressure forces]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]] (with $\vec a = \vec 0$)

![[ph-cm-a9-3-1.svg]]
*Pressure forces (blue) on a submerged block whose top and bottom faces have area $A$. They grow with depth: the side forces cancel in pairs, and the upward push on the bottom face exceeds the downward push on the top. The difference is the buoyant force $\vec F_B$ (red).*

> [!caution] Caution: What the buoyant force does and does not depend on
> - It acts on *every* immersed body, sinking ones included: an anchor under water is partly supported.
> - It depends on the displaced volume and the density of the **fluid**, not on the body's own mass or material. A lead block and a wooden block of the same volume, both fully submerged, feel the same buoyant force.
> - For an incompressible liquid it does not depend on depth: the pressure difference between top and bottom stays the same as the body sinks.
> - The atmosphere buoys us too, but air is so light that the effect is usually negligible; for a helium balloon it is everything.

^cau-a9-3-1

## Floating and sinking

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.3.2: Floating and Sinking
> A body whose average density $\rho_{\text{obj}}$ is less than the fluid's density $\rho_{\text{fl}}$ floats, with the fraction
>
> $$
> \frac{V_{\text{sub}}}{V_{\text{obj}}} = \frac{\rho_{\text{obj}}}{\rho_{\text{fl}}}
> $$
>
> of its volume below the surface. If $\rho_{\text{obj}} > \rho_{\text{fl}}$ it sinks; if they are equal it can stay suspended at any depth.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.4*

^thm-a9-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Fully submerged, the body feels $F_B = \rho_{\text{fl}} V_{\text{obj}} g$ up ([[§A9.3 Buoyancy#^thm-a9-3-1|Theorem §A9.3.1]]) and its weight $\rho_{\text{obj}} V_{\text{obj}} g$ down. If $\rho_{\text{obj}} > \rho_{\text{fl}}$ the weight wins and it sinks; if $\rho_{\text{obj}} < \rho_{\text{fl}}$ it rises until only part of it, $V_{\text{sub}}$, is under the surface and the forces balance: $\rho_{\text{fl}} V_{\text{sub}} g = \rho_{\text{obj}} V_{\text{obj}} g$. Dividing gives the fraction.

^der-a9-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A9.3 Buoyancy#^thm-a9-3-1|Theorem §A9.3.1]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-2|Def. §A9.1.2]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Average density is what counts
> A lump of clay sinks, but the same clay shaped into a boat floats: hollowed out, it encloses air and displaces much more water, so its *average* density (total mass over total enclosed volume) is below that of water. Steel ships, loaded ships riding lower than empty ones, submarines adjusting their ballast tanks and hot-air balloons all work the same way.

^rem-a9-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Apparent weight and measuring density
> A body hung from a scale in a fluid reads its **apparent weight** $w_{\text{app}} = w - F_B$: it loses exactly the weight of the fluid it displaces. Weighing it in air ($w = \rho_{\text{obj}} V g$) and fully submerged ($w - w_{\text{app}} = \rho_{\text{fl}} V g$) and dividing eliminates the unknown volume:
>
> $$
> \frac{\rho_{\text{obj}}}{\rho_{\text{fl}}} = \frac{w}{w - w_{\text{app}}} .
> $$
>
> This is a standard way to measure the density of a coin or a crown, or, with a body of known density, of a liquid.

^rem-a9-3-2

> [!example] Example §A9.3.1: How Deep Things Float
> (a) A 60.0-kg woman floats in fresh water with 97.0% of her volume submerged when her lungs are full. By [[§A9.3 Buoyancy#^thm-a9-3-2|Theorem §A9.3.2]] her average density is
>
> $$
> \rho_{\text{person}} = 0.970\,\rho_{\text{water}} = 0.970 \times 1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3} = 970\ \mathrm{kg/m^3},
> $$
>
> just below that of water, which is why breathing out can make a swimmer sink.
>
> (b) Ice ($917\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$) in sea water ($1030\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$) floats with $917/1030 = 89\%$ of its volume submerged: only about a ninth of an iceberg shows above the sea.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 14.4 (a); Tables 14.1–14.2 (b)*

^ex-a9-3-1

> [!remark]- Connections
> - For a body of any shape, the net pressure force is $-\oint p\,\hat n\,dA$, and the divergence theorem ([[§32 Flux Integrals and the Divergence Theorem in ℝ³#^thm-32-1|452 Thm. §32.1]], Multivariable Analysis), applied to each component, turns it into $-\int \nabla p\,dV = -\int \rho_{\text{fl}}\vec g\,dV$, the weight of displaced fluid reversed. This is the replacement argument in formulas.
