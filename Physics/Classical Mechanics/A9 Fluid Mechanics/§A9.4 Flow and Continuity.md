With this section the chapter turns from fluids at rest to fluids in motion. Even simple flows can be very complicated, so we first fix an idealised fluid and a language for its motion: streamlines and flow rate. The first law of fluid dynamics is then just conservation of mass, the continuity equation: where a pipe narrows, the fluid must speed up.

## Describing flow

> [!definition] Definition §A9.4.1: Streamline; Laminar and Turbulent Flow
> A **streamline** is the path followed by a small volume of fluid; the fluid's velocity is tangent to it at every point. In **laminar** (smooth) flow, neighbouring layers slide past each other along steady, regular streamlines without mixing. In **turbulent** flow the streamlines are irregular and change in time, and the fluid mixes in eddies and swirls. Flow tends to become turbulent above a certain speed, or behind obstructions and sharp corners ([[§A9.6 Viscosity and Turbulence]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.5*

^def-a9-4-1

> [!model] Model §A9.4.1: Ideal Fluid in Steady Flow
> An **ideal fluid** has negligible **viscosity** (internal friction). In addition, the flow is taken to be steady and laminar, and the fluid incompressible ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^mod-a9-1-2|Model §A9.1.2]]).
>
> - *Assumes:* no energy lost to friction within the fluid or at the walls; the same speed across a pipe's cross-section; constant density.
> - *Fails:* for viscous flows, where the speed drops to zero at the walls and a pressure difference is needed just to keep the fluid moving ([[§A9.6 Viscosity and Turbulence]]); for turbulent flow; for gases whose density changes along the flow.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.5*

^mod-a9-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §A9.4.2: Volume and Mass Flow Rate
> The **volume flow rate** $Q$ through a cross-section is the volume of fluid passing it per unit time; the **mass flow rate** is the mass passing per unit time. For fluid of density $\rho$ moving with average speed $v$ through a cross-section of area $A$,
>
> $$
> Q = \frac{dV}{dt} = A v, \qquad \frac{dm}{dt} = \rho A v .
> $$
>
> The SI unit of $Q$ is $\mathrm{m^3/s}$; often litres per second or minute are used ($1\ \mathrm L = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3}$).
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.5, eq. (14.13)*

^def-a9-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Why $Q = Av$
> In a time $dt$ the fluid that crosses the section moves forward $dx = v\,dt$, so it fills a cylinder of volume $dV = A\,dx = A v\,dt$; its mass is $\rho\,dV$. Flow rate and speed are different things: a fast mountain stream carries far less water than a slow, wide river.

^rem-a9-4-1

## The continuity equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.4.2: Equation of Continuity
> In steady flow through a pipe with no sources or sinks, the mass flow rate is the same through every cross-section:
>
> $$
> \rho_1 A_1 v_1 = \rho_2 A_2 v_2 .
> $$
>
> For an incompressible fluid the density cancels and the volume flow rate is the same everywhere:
>
> $$
> A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 \qquad (Q_1 = Q_2).
> $$
>
> The speed is inversely proportional to the cross-sectional area.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.5, eqs. (14.14)–(14.15)*

^thm-a9-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Consider the fluid in the pipe between sections 1 and 2. In steady flow the mass between them does not change with time, and fluid enters only through section 1 and leaves only through section 2. Mass is conserved, so the rate at which it enters equals the rate at which it leaves: $(dm/dt)_1 = (dm/dt)_2$. By [[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^def-a9-4-2|Def. §A9.4.2]] this is $\rho_1 A_1 v_1 = \rho_2 A_2 v_2$. With $\rho_1 = \rho_2$ ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^mod-a9-1-2|Model §A9.1.2]]), $A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$.

^der-a9-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^def-a9-4-2|Def. §A9.4.2]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^mod-a9-1-2|Model §A9.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Gases and real pipes
> - $A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2$ holds for liquids, which are nearly incompressible. For a gas that is compressed or expands along the flow, use the general form with $\rho$.
> - Here $v$ is the speed *averaged over the cross-section*. In a real, viscous flow the fluid moves fastest on the axis and not at all at the walls ([[§A9.6 Viscosity and Turbulence]]).

^cau-a9-4-1

> [!example] Example §A9.4.1: Speeding Up through a Nozzle
> A garden hose of radius 0.900 cm carries $0.500\ \mathrm{L/s}$ and ends in a nozzle of diameter 0.500 cm (radius 0.250 cm). In the hose,
>
> $$
> v_1 = \frac{Q}{\pi r_1^2} = \frac{0.500 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}}{\pi (9.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm m)^2} = 1.96\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> By [[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^thm-a9-4-2|Theorem §A9.4.2]], in the nozzle
>
> $$
> v_2 = \frac{A_1}{A_2}\,v_1 = \frac{r_1^2}{r_2^2}\,v_1 = \frac{(0.900\ \mathrm{cm})^2}{(0.250\ \mathrm{cm})^2}\,(1.96\ \mathrm{m/s}) = 25.5\ \mathrm{m/s}.
> $$
>
> The speed goes as $1/r^2$, so a modest narrowing gives a large effect. That is the point of a nozzle, and why pursed lips can blow out a distant candle. The pressure in the hose is found in [[§A9.5 Bernoulli's Equation#^ex-a9-5-1|Example §A9.5.1]].
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 14.5*
