Continuity ([[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^thm-a9-4-2|Theorem §A9.4.2]]) says that fluid speeds up in a constriction. Speeding up takes a net force, and in a fluid that force comes from a pressure difference. Bernoulli's equation makes this quantitative. It is the work–energy theorem applied to a moving parcel of ideal fluid, and it links pressure, speed and height along the flow.

## Bernoulli's equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.5.1: Bernoulli's Equation
> In the steady flow of an ideal fluid ([[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^mod-a9-4-1|Model §A9.4.1]]), the quantity
>
> $$
> p + \tfrac12 \rho v^2 + \rho g y = \text{constant}
> $$
>
> is the same at all points along a streamline (or along a pipe). For any two points 1 and 2,
>
> $$
> p_1 + \tfrac12 \rho v_1^2 + \rho g y_1 = p_2 + \tfrac12 \rho v_2^2 + \rho g y_2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.6, eq. (14.16)*

^thm-a9-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take as system the fluid in a pipe between section 1 (area $A_1$, height $y_1$) and section 2 (area $A_2$, height $y_2$). In a time $dt$ the back of the system moves forward $dx_1 = v_1\,dt$ and the front $dx_2 = v_2\,dt$. By continuity both sweep the same volume $dV = A_1\,dx_1 = A_2\,dx_2$.
>
> *Work by the surrounding fluid.* The fluid behind pushes forward with $p_1 A_1$ and the fluid ahead pushes back with $p_2 A_2$:
>
> $$
> dW = p_1 A_1\,dx_1 - p_2 A_2\,dx_2 = (p_1 - p_2)\,dV .
> $$
>
> *Change of energy.* The flow is steady, so the fluid between the two slabs looks the same before and after. The net effect is that a mass $dm = \rho\,dV$ has moved from section 1 to section 2:
>
> $$
> dK = \tfrac12 \rho\,dV\,(v_2^2 - v_1^2), \qquad dU = \rho\,dV\,g\,(y_2 - y_1).
> $$
>
> With no viscous losses, the work done by the pressure forces changes the kinetic energy plus the gravitational potential energy: $dW = dK + dU$ ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem]], [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy]]). Dividing by $dV$,
>
> $$
> p_1 - p_2 = \tfrac12 \rho (v_2^2 - v_1^2) + \rho g (y_2 - y_1),
> $$
>
> which rearranges to the stated form. The two sections were arbitrary, so the sum is constant along the flow.

^der-a9-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Reading Bernoulli's equation
> - Each term is an energy per unit volume: $\tfrac12\rho v^2$ is kinetic energy density, $\rho g y$ gravitational potential energy density, and $p$ the work done by pressure per unit volume pushed. The equation is energy conservation for an incompressible fluid without friction.
> - *Fluid at rest* ($v_1 = v_2 = 0$): $p_2 = p_1 + \rho g (y_1 - y_2)$, the hydrostatic law of [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-3|Theorem §A9.1.3]]. Bernoulli's equation contains fluid statics as a special case.
> - *Constant height* ($y_1 = y_2$): $p_1 + \tfrac12\rho v_1^2 = p_2 + \tfrac12\rho v_2^2$. This is often called **Bernoulli's principle**: where the fluid moves faster, its pressure is lower. In a moving fluid, points at the same height can therefore have different pressures.

^rem-a9-5-1

> [!caution] Caution: When Bernoulli does not apply
> The equation relates points along one flow of an *ideal* fluid in *steady* flow. It does not hold across a pump or turbine (which adds or removes energy), in viscous flow, where friction turns mechanical energy into heat and the pressure drops along a level pipe even at constant speed ([[§A9.6 Viscosity and Turbulence]]), or in turbulent flow.

^cau-a9-5-1

## Applications

> [!remark] Remark: Low pressure in fast flow
> - *Entrainment.* A fast jet has low pressure, so the surrounding fluid at higher pressure is pushed into it. Bunsen burners draw in air this way, atomizers and paint sprayers draw up liquid, and aspirators use a fast stream of water as a suction pump.
> - *Passing vehicles.* Air squeezed between a car and a truck speeds up, its pressure drops, and the higher pressure outside pushes the two together. A shower curtain bulges inward for the same reason.
> - *Measuring speed.* In a Prandtl (pitot) tube, one opening faces the flow and brings the fluid there to rest, while another lies along the flow at speed $v$. Bernoulli's principle gives $p_{\text{facing}} - p_{\text{side}} = \tfrac12\rho v^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2\,\Delta p/\rho}$. A manometer reads $\Delta p$, and the device serves as an airspeed indicator on aircraft.

^rem-a9-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.5.2: Torricelli's Theorem
> Fluid flows out of a small opening a depth $h$ below the free surface of a large open tank with speed
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{2 g h},
> $$
>
> the speed of a body falling freely from rest through the height $h$.
>
> *Source: UP1 eq. (14.16), applied (this consequence is not stated in UP's text)*

^thm-a9-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply [[§A9.5 Bernoulli's Equation#^thm-a9-5-1|Theorem §A9.5.1]] between a point on the free surface (1) and a point in the emerging jet (2). Both are open to the air, so $p_1 = p_2 = p_{\text{atm}}$. The tank is much wider than the opening, so by continuity ([[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^thm-a9-4-2|Theorem §A9.4.2]]) the surface sinks very slowly, $v_1 \approx 0$. With $y_1 - y_2 = h$, Bernoulli gives $\rho g h = \tfrac12\rho v_2^2$, so $v_2 = \sqrt{2gh}$.

^der-a9-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A9.5 Bernoulli's Equation#^thm-a9-5-1|Theorem §A9.5.1]], [[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^thm-a9-4-2|Theorem §A9.4.2]], [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall|§A2.3]]

> [!example] Example §A9.5.1: Pressure in a Garden Hose
> In [[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^ex-a9-4-1|Example §A9.4.1]] water sped up from $v_1 = 1.96\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in the hose to $v_2 = 25.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in the nozzle. The jet leaves into the air, so the absolute pressure in the nozzle is atmospheric, $p_2 = 1.01 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$. For level, frictionless flow, Bernoulli's principle gives the pressure in the hose:
>
> $$
> p_1 = p_2 + \tfrac12\rho\,(v_2^2 - v_1^2) = 1.01 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa} + \tfrac12(1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3})\big[(25.5)^2 - (1.96)^2\big]\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2} = 4.24 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}.
> $$
>
> The slow water in the hose is at about four times atmospheric pressure; that excess pressure is what accelerates it through the nozzle.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 14.6*

^ex-a9-5-1

> [!example] Example §A9.5.2: A Fire Hose up a Ladder
> A fire hose of inside diameter 6.40 cm carries $40.0\ \mathrm{L/s}$, starting at ground level at a gauge pressure of $1.62 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{Pa}$. It rises 10.0 m up a ladder to a nozzle of inside diameter 3.00 cm. What is the pressure in the nozzle? Here pressure, speed and height all change.
>
> *Speeds* from $Q = Av$:
>
> $$
> v_1 = \frac{40.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}}{\pi (3.20 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm m)^2} = 12.4\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad v_2 = \frac{40.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/s}}{\pi (1.50 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm m)^2} = 56.6\ \mathrm{m/s}.
> $$
>
> *Bernoulli* with $y_1 = 0$, $y_2 = 10.0\ \mathrm m$ (gauge pressures throughout):
>
> $$
> p_2 = p_1 + \tfrac12\rho\,(v_1^2 - v_2^2) - \rho g y_2 = 1.62 \times 10^6 - 1.53 \times 10^6 - 0.098 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{Pa} \approx 0 .
> $$
>
> To the precision of the data, the gauge pressure in the nozzle is zero: atmospheric, as it must be where the water exits into the air. Almost all the pressure at the pump has gone into speed, and a little into height.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 14.7*

^ex-a9-5-2
