A fluid has no shape of its own, so it is described not by forces on a body but by two fields defined at every point: the mass per volume (density) and the force per area (pressure). This section defines both and applies Newton's laws to a fluid at rest. The result is the basic law of hydrostatics, that pressure grows with depth in proportion to the weight of fluid above, and it underlies the rest of the chapter.

## Fluids and density

> [!definition] Definition §A9.1.1: Fluid
> A **fluid** is a substance that cannot resist a shearing (sideways) force: it flows instead. Liquids and gases are fluids; solids resist shear and keep their shape ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli]]).
> - A **liquid** has nearly fixed volume, because its molecules are in close contact and strongly resist compression, but it takes the shape of its container.
> - A **gas** has neither fixed shape nor fixed volume: its molecules are far apart, interact weakly except in collisions, and fill any container. Gases are easy to compress.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.1*

^def-a9-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A9.1.2: Density, Local Density, Specific Gravity
> The (average) **density** of a body or sample of mass $m$ and volume $V$ is
>
> $$
> \rho = \frac{m}{V}, \qquad [\rho] = \mathrm{kg/m^3}, \qquad 1\ \mathrm{g/cm^3} = 1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}.
> $$
>
> If the material is not **homogeneous**, the **local density** at a point is $\rho(x,y,z) = \lim_{\Delta V \to 0} \Delta m/\Delta V$ over small volumes around the point. The **specific gravity** of a material is its density divided by that of water at $4\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, $1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$; it is a pure number.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.1, eqs. (14.1)–(14.2)*

^def-a9-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Typical densities
> | Substance | $\rho$ ($\mathrm{kg/m^3}$) | Substance | $\rho$ ($\mathrm{kg/m^3}$) |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | air ($0\ ^\circ$C, 1 atm) | 1.29 | ice ($0\ ^\circ$C) | 917 |
> | helium | 0.180 | water ($4\ ^\circ$C) | 1000 |
> | gasoline | 680 | sea water | 1030 |
> | aluminium | 2700 | iron | 7860 |
> | mercury | 13 600 | gold | 19 300 |
>
> Solids and liquids have comparable densities (their atoms touch); gases are about a thousand times less dense and depend strongly on temperature and pressure. Water is unusual: its density is greatest at $4\ ^\circ$C, and ice is less dense than liquid water, so lakes freeze from the top.

^rem-a9-1-1

## Pressure

> [!definition] Definition §A9.1.3: Pressure
> The **pressure** on a surface is the normal (perpendicular) force per unit area. For a force $F$ spread uniformly over an area $A$, and at a point in general,
>
> $$
> p = \frac{F}{A}, \qquad p = \frac{dF}{dA}.
> $$
>
> Pressure is a scalar. Its SI unit is the **pascal**, $1\ \mathrm{Pa} = 1\ \mathrm{N/m^2}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.1, eq. (14.3)*

^def-a9-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Pressure has no direction, its force does
> A fluid at rest cannot exert a shear force, so the force it exerts on any surface, a container wall or a submerged body, is perpendicular to that surface and pushes on it: $d\vec F$ has magnitude $p\,dA$ and points from the fluid into the surface. At a given point of a static fluid the pressure is the same on surfaces of every orientation; otherwise a small element there would feel a net force and accelerate. The same force spread over a smaller area means a larger pressure: that is why a needle pierces skin where a finger does not.

^rem-a9-1-2

## Hydrostatics

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.1.1: Hydrostatic Equilibrium
> In a fluid at rest in a uniform gravitational field, with the $y$ axis pointing up,
>
> $$
> \frac{dp}{dy} = -\rho g ,
> $$
>
> and the pressure does not change in horizontal directions. Pressure decreases going up, at a rate set by the local density.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.1, eqs. (14.6)–(14.8)*

^thm-a9-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take as system a thin horizontal slab of fluid of area $A$ between heights $y$ and $y + \Delta y$, of mass $\Delta m = \rho A\,\Delta y$. Vertically, three forces act: the fluid below pushes up with $p(y)A$, the fluid above pushes down with $p(y+\Delta y)A$, and the weight $\Delta m\,g$ points down. The slab does not accelerate, so with up positive
>
> $$
> p(y)A - p(y+\Delta y)A - \rho g A\,\Delta y = 0
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> \frac{p(y+\Delta y) - p(y)}{\Delta y} = -\rho g .
> $$
>
> Letting $\Delta y \to 0$ gives $dp/dy = -\rho g$. For a horizontal element the weight has no component along its length, so the pressures on its two ends balance, and $p$ is the same at all points of equal height.

^der-a9-1-1

> [!model] Model §A9.1.2: Incompressible Fluid
> The density is the same everywhere in the fluid and does not change under pressure: $\rho = $ const.
>
> - *Assumes:* a liquid, over depth ranges where its compression is negligible. Water at the bottom of a swimming pool is compressed very little by the water above.
> - *Fails:* for gases over large heights. The density of air changes noticeably within a short distance above the ground ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-4|Theorem §A9.1.4]]). How much a material compresses is measured by its bulk modulus ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.1, §14.5*

^mod-a9-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.1.3: Pressure at a Depth
> In an incompressible fluid at rest whose surface is at pressure $p_0$, the pressure at depth $h$ below the surface is
>
> $$
> p = p_0 + \rho g h .
> $$
>
> It depends only on the depth, not on the shape or size of the container. Fluid that can flow freely between connected vessels stands at the same level in all of them.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.1, eqs. (14.4), (14.9)*

^thm-a9-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $y = 0$ at the surface, where $p = p_0$; depth $h$ is $y = -h$. With $\rho$ constant ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^mod-a9-1-2|Model §A9.1.2]]), integrate [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-1|Theorem §A9.1.1]]:
>
> $$
> p - p_0 = \int_0^{-h} \frac{dp}{dy}\,dy = -\int_0^{-h}\rho g\,dy = \rho g h .
> $$
>
> Only the vertical distance enters, and by the theorem points at equal height have equal pressure, whatever the container's shape. In connected vessels, the free surfaces are at pressure $p_0$, so they must lie at the same height. Equivalently: $\rho g h$ is the weight of a column of fluid of height $h$ and cross-section $A$, divided by $A$.

^der-a9-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-1|Theorem §A9.1.1]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^mod-a9-1-2|Model §A9.1.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]] (FTC)

> [!caution] Caution: Depth, not volume
> A narrow tube of water 10 m tall produces the same pressure at its base as a lake 10 m deep. The amount of fluid does not matter, only the height of the column above the point. For the same reason the force on a dam depends on the water's depth and the dam's width, not on how far back the reservoir extends.

^cau-a9-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.1.4: Isothermal Atmosphere
> If the air is an ideal gas at constant temperature $T$, with molecular mass $m$, and $g$ is constant, the pressure falls exponentially with height:
>
> $$
> p(y) = p_0\, e^{-\alpha y}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{m g}{k_B T},
> $$
>
> with $k_B = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J/K}$ Boltzmann's constant. The **scale height** $1/\alpha$ is the height over which the pressure drops by a factor $1/e$.
>
> This is a rough estimate. Constant $g$ is an excellent assumption over these heights (it changes by about 0.3% in 10 km, [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^mod-a8-1-3|Model §A8.1.3]]), but the real atmosphere's temperature is far from constant over kilometres of height.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.1*

^thm-a9-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the form $p = \rho k_B T/m$, the ideal gas law ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|TH Law §A2.1.1]]) gives $\rho = p\,m/(k_B T)$: the density is proportional to the pressure. [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-1|Theorem §A9.1.1]] becomes $dp/dy = -\alpha p$. Separating variables and integrating from the ground ($y = 0$, $p = p_0$),
>
> $$
> \int_{p_0}^{p(y)} \frac{dp}{p} = -\alpha\int_0^y dy \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln\frac{p(y)}{p_0} = -\alpha y .
> $$
>
> For air, take $m \approx 4.8 \times 10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$ (the mean mass of an air molecule, about 29 u; a nitrogen molecule has $4.65 \times 10^{-26}\ \mathrm{kg}$) and $T = 300\ \mathrm K$: $\alpha = (4.8 \times 10^{-26})(9.80)/[(1.38 \times 10^{-23})(300)]\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} = 1/(8800\ \mathrm m)$. The pressure falls to about a third every 8.8 km.

^der-a9-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-1|Theorem §A9.1.1]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-2|Def. §A9.1.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]] (FTC)

> [!example] Example §A9.1.1: The Force on a Dam
> A dam 500 m wide holds back water 80.0 m deep. Atmospheric pressure acts on both faces of the dam and cancels, so only the water's own contribution $\rho g h$ matters. Measure $h$ down from the surface. A horizontal strip of the dam of height $dh$ at depth $h$ feels the force $dF = \rho g h\,(w\,dh)$, so
>
> $$
> F = \int_0^H \rho g h\, w\, dh = \tfrac12 \rho g w H^2 = \tfrac12(1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3})(9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2})(500\ \mathrm m)(80.0\ \mathrm m)^2 = 1.57 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm N .
> $$
>
> Because pressure grows linearly with depth, this is the same as the pressure at mid-depth, $\rho g (H/2) = 3.92 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$, times the area $wH = 4.00 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{m^2}$. The force grows as the square of the depth, and it does not depend on the size of the reservoir.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 14.1*

^ex-a9-1-1

> [!example] Example §A9.1.2: Two Liquids in a U-Tube
> A U-tube open at both ends contains a liquid of density $\rho_1$. A lighter liquid of density $\rho_2 < \rho_1$ is poured into one arm and floats on top. The lighter column stands a height $h_2$ above the interface; in the other arm liquid 1 stands $h_1$ above the level of the interface.
>
> Compare the pressure at the interface with the pressure at the same height in the other arm. Both points are in liquid 1, which is connected and at rest, so the pressures are equal ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-1|Theorem §A9.1.1]]); each is $p_0$ plus the weight per area of the column above it ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-3|Theorem §A9.1.3]]):
>
> $$
> p_0 + \rho_2 g h_2 = p_0 + \rho_1 g h_1
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> \rho_1 h_1 = \rho_2 h_2, \qquad h_2 - h_1 = \left(1 - \frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}\right) h_2 > 0 .
> $$
>
> The lighter liquid stands higher, and measuring the two heights gives the density ratio $\rho_2/\rho_1 = h_1/h_2$. Comparing points at equal height in the *same* connected liquid is the key step; across the interface the rule does not apply.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 14.2*

^ex-a9-1-2

## Measuring pressure

> [!definition] Definition §A9.1.4: Gauge Pressure and Absolute Pressure
> Most gauges read the **gauge pressure** $p_g$, the pressure above atmospheric pressure. The **absolute pressure** is
>
> $$
> p_{\text{abs}} = p_g + p_{\text{atm}} .
> $$
>
> A fluid can push but not pull, so $p_{\text{abs}} \ge 0$ and hence $p_g \ge -p_{\text{atm}}$; there is no upper limit. A tyre gauge reading 34 psi means an absolute pressure of $34 + 14.7 = 48.7$ psi.
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.2, eq. (14.11)*

^def-a9-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: Manometers, barometers and units
> - An **open-tube manometer** is a U-tube of liquid with one arm connected to the system and the other open to the air. A level difference $h$ means a gauge pressure $p_g = \rho g h$ (negative if the liquid stands higher on the system's side).
> - A **mercury barometer** (Torricelli, 1643) is a tube closed at the top, filled with mercury and inverted into a dish of mercury. The space above the column is nearly a vacuum, so the column height measures absolute atmospheric pressure, $\rho g h = p_{\text{atm}}$: 760 mm of mercury at sea level. With water the column would have to be $1.013 \times 10^5/(1000 \times 9.80)\ \mathrm m = 10.3\ \mathrm m$ tall.
> - Units: $1\ \mathrm{atm} = 1.013 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa} = 760\ \mathrm{mm\,Hg} = 14.7\ \mathrm{psi} = 1013\ \mathrm{mbar}$; $1\ \mathrm{bar} = 10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$; $1\ \mathrm{torr} = 1\ \mathrm{mm\,Hg} = 133.3\ \mathrm{Pa}$; $1\ \mathrm{psi} = 6.895 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{Pa}$.
