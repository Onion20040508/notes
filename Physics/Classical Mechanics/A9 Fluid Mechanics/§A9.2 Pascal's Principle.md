What happens to the pressure everywhere in a liquid when one part of it is squeezed? Pascal observed in 1653 that the change reaches every point in full. Here this follows from the hydrostatics of [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure]]. It is the working principle of hydraulic machines, which turn a small force into a large one.

## Pascal's principle

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.2.1: Pascal's Principle
> A change in pressure applied to an enclosed incompressible fluid at rest is transmitted undiminished to every point of the fluid and to the walls of its container:
>
> $$
> \Delta p_{\text{anywhere}} = \Delta p_{\text{applied}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.3*

^thm-a9-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let the pressure at the top surface of the fluid be $p_{\text{top}}$. At depth $h$ below it, [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-3|Theorem §A9.1.3]] gives $p(h) = p_{\text{top}} + \rho g h$. Increase $p_{\text{top}}$ by $\Delta p$, for example by adding a weight $Mg$ to a piston of area $A$ that closes the container, so $\Delta p = Mg/A$. The fluid is incompressible ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^mod-a9-1-2|Model §A9.1.2]]), so $\rho$ and the depths $h$ stay the same, and therefore $p(h)$ rises by the same $\Delta p$ at every depth. By [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-1|Theorem §A9.1.1]] the same holds at every horizontal position.

^der-a9-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-3|Theorem §A9.1.3]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-1|Theorem §A9.1.1]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^mod-a9-1-2|Model §A9.1.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Changes, not values
> Pascal's principle does **not** say that the pressure is the same everywhere in the fluid: it still grows with depth by $\rho g h$. It says that a *change* made at one place appears unchanged at every other place. Put differently, the pressures from different sources simply add: at depth $h$ under an open surface, $p = p_{\text{atm}} + \rho g h$, and a piston pushing on top adds its own $F/A$ everywhere.

^cau-a9-2-1

## Hydraulic systems

> [!theorem] Theorem §A9.2.2: Hydraulic Force Multiplication
> Two pistons of areas $A_1$ and $A_2$, at the same height, close an enclosed liquid. In equilibrium (friction negligible) the forces on them satisfy
>
> $$
> \frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2}, \qquad F_2 = \frac{A_2}{A_1}\,F_1 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §14.3, eq. (14.12)*

^thm-a9-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The force $F_1$ on piston 1 raises the pressure there by $p_1 = F_1/A_1$ ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-3|Def. §A9.1.3]]). By [[§A9.2 Pascal's Principle#^thm-a9-2-1|Theorem §A9.2.1]] the same pressure increase acts at piston 2, and since the pistons are at the same height there is no extra $\rho g h$ between them. Piston 2 is then pushed with $F_2 = p_1 A_2$.

^der-a9-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A9.2 Pascal's Principle#^thm-a9-2-1|Theorem §A9.2.1]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-3|Def. §A9.1.3]]

![[ph-cm-a9-2-1.svg]]
*A hydraulic press. A small force $\vec F_1$ (blue) on the narrow piston produces the same pressure under the wide piston, which therefore pushes up with the much larger force $\vec F_2$ (red). The narrow piston moves down a long way, $d_1$, while the wide one rises only $d_2$, because the liquid's volume is unchanged.*

> [!remark] Remark: More force, not more work
> A hydraulic system is a simple machine, like a lever. The liquid is incompressible, so the volume pushed out under piston 1 equals the volume arriving under piston 2: $A_1 d_1 = A_2 d_2$. Then
>
> $$
> W_2 = F_2 d_2 = \left(\frac{A_2}{A_1}F_1\right)\left(\frac{A_1}{A_2}d_1\right) = F_1 d_1 = W_1 .
> $$
>
> The force is multiplied by $A_2/A_1$ and the distance divided by the same factor; no work is gained ([[§A4.1 Work]]). Its advantage over a lever is that pressure can be sent through long, curved lines to several places at once. A hydraulic jack lifts a car this way, and power brakes add a motorized pump that does most of the work. The incompressibility matters: with a gas in the lines, pushing the pedal would mostly compress the gas.

^rem-a9-2-1

> [!example] Example §A9.2.1: Hydraulic Brakes
> A driver's foot, through a lever, pushes the master (pedal) cylinder of a car's brakes with 500 N. The master cylinder has diameter 0.500 cm; each of the four wheel cylinders has diameter 2.50 cm. All cylinders are connected by brake fluid, so by [[§A9.2 Pascal's Principle#^thm-a9-2-2|Theorem §A9.2.2]] each wheel cylinder pushes with
>
> $$
> F_2 = \frac{A_2}{A_1}F_1 = \frac{\pi (1.25\ \mathrm{cm})^2}{\pi (0.250\ \mathrm{cm})^2}\,(500\ \mathrm N) = 25 \times 500\ \mathrm N = 1.25 \times 10^4\ \mathrm N .
> $$
>
> Every wheel cylinder gets this full force, however many there are: the pressure, not the force, is shared. The price is distance: the master piston displaces the fluid for all four wheel cylinders, so it must travel $4 \times 25 = 100$ times as far as each wheel piston moves.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 14.3*

^ex-a9-2-1
