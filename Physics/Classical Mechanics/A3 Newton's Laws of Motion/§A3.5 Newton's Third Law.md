The first two laws are about one body and the forces on it. The third law is about the *pair* of bodies behind every force: when you push a wall, the wall pushes back. It explains how anything can propel itself, and it is the reason internal forces drop out of the second law for a system (§A3.3).

## The law

> [!principle] Principle §A3.5.1: Newton's Third Law
> Whenever a body $A$ exerts a force on a body $B$, $B$ simultaneously exerts on $A$ a force of equal magnitude and opposite direction:
>
> $$
> \vec F_{B\text{ on }A} = -\,\vec F_{A\text{ on }B} .
> $$
>
> Forces therefore always occur in pairs; no body can exert a force on another without experiencing one itself.
>
> *Domain: every real force between two bodies in Newtonian mechanics ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-3|Def. §A3.6.3]]); the inertial forces that appear in accelerating frames have no partner.*
> *Source: UP1 §5.5, eq. (5.10)*

^pr-a3-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Equal and opposite, but on different bodies
> The two forces of a pair are always equal and opposite, whatever the bodies are doing and whatever their masses. And they act on *different* bodies, so they never cancel each other: on any one body's free-body diagram at most one of them appears.
>
> A swimmer pushes off the wall of a pool. Take the swimmer as the system. The force of the wall on her feet, $\vec F_{\text{wall on feet}}$, is external and accelerates her away from the wall. Its partner $\vec F_{\text{feet on wall}}$ acts on the wall, so it does not appear in her free-body diagram and does not cancel anything. She pushes in the direction *opposite* to the one she wants to go, and the reaction carries her. The names "action" and "reaction" are loose: neither force comes first.

^rem-a3-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Propulsion is always by reaction
> - Walking and running: the foot pushes backward on the ground, the ground pushes the walker forward.
> - A car: the drive wheels push backward on the road (on gravel they throw stones backward), the road pushes the car forward.
> - Helicopters push air down and are pushed up; birds and planes push air down and back to get lift and forward motion; an octopus ejects water backward.
> - A climber pulls down on a rope, and the rope pulls up on the climber.
> - A rocket pushes its exhaust gas backward, and the gas pushes the rocket forward. It does not push on the air behind it; it works better in vacuum, where the exhaust escapes more freely.

^rem-a3-5-2

> [!definition] Definition §A3.5.1: Thrust
> **Thrust** is the reaction force that pushes a body forward when it pushes matter backward, as when a rocket expels gas or a jet ski ejects water.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.5*

^def-a3-5-1

## Internal forces cancel

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.2: Only External Forces Accelerate a System
> Let a system consist of parts that move together with a common acceleration $\vec a$, and let $M$ be its total mass. The internal forces (those between parts of the system) add up to zero, and
>
> $$
> \sum \vec F_{\text{ext}} = M\vec a .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.3, §5.5, Example 5.10*

^thm-a3-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Label the parts $i = 1,\dots,n$, with masses $m_i$. The force on part $i$ is the external force $\vec F_i^{\text{ext}}$ plus the forces $\vec F_{j\text{ on }i}$ from the other parts. The second law for part $i$ reads
>
> $$
> \vec F_i^{\text{ext}} + \sum_{j\neq i} \vec F_{j\text{ on }i} = m_i\vec a .
> $$
>
> Add these $n$ equations. The double sum of internal forces contains each pair of parts twice, once as $\vec F_{j\text{ on }i}$ and once as $\vec F_{i\text{ on }j}$; by the third law each such pair adds to zero. What remains is $\sum_i \vec F_i^{\text{ext}} = \bigl(\sum_i m_i\bigr)\vec a = M\vec a$.

^der-a3-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|Principle §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]]

This is why a free-body diagram shows only external forces ([[§A3.1 Forces#^def-a3-1-6|Def. §A3.1.6]]), and why the muscular forces inside a pushing student, or the countless forces between atoms of a car, never need to be known. When the parts accelerate differently, the same cancellation shows that the net external force governs the motion of the system's centre of mass ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass|§A5.4]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.5.1: A Package on a Scale
> A package rests on a scale. The forces *on the package* are the upward force $\vec S$ of the scale and its weight $\vec w$ (down). It does not accelerate, so the second law gives
>
> $$
> \vec S + \vec w = m\vec a = \vec 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S = w .
> $$
>
> $\vec S$ and $\vec w$ are **not** a third-law pair: they act on the same body, and they are equal only because $\vec a = \vec 0$. The partner of $\vec S$ is the force $-\vec S$ that the package exerts on the scale's surface, and that is what the scale responds to. The partner of $\vec w$ is the package's gravitational pull $-\vec w$ on Earth. So the scale reading equals the weight only when the package is not accelerating; in an accelerating elevator it does not ([[§A3.7 Free-Body Diagrams and Problem Solving#^ex-a3-7-2|Example §A3.7.2]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 5.9*

^ex-a3-5-1

> [!example] Example §A3.5.2: Pushing a Cart, and Choosing the System
> A professor ($65.0\ \mathrm{kg}$) pushes a cart ($12.0\ \mathrm{kg}$) carrying equipment ($7.0\ \mathrm{kg}$). She pushes backward on the floor with $150\ \mathrm N$; the forces opposing the motion total $f = 24.0\ \mathrm N$, and we take them all to act on the cart. Forward is positive.
>
> **(a) Acceleration.** System 1: professor, cart and equipment. By the third law, the floor pushes her forward with $F_{\text{floor}} = 150\ \mathrm N$; this force acts on the system and is external. Her push on the cart and the cart's push back on her are internal and cancel ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]); her push on the floor acts on the floor. Vertical forces balance. So
>
> $$
> a = \frac{F_{\text{floor}} - f}{m_1} = \frac{150\ \mathrm N - 24.0\ \mathrm N}{84.0\ \mathrm{kg}} = 1.50\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.
> $$
>
> **(b) Force on the cart.** System 2: cart and equipment ($19.0\ \mathrm{kg}$). Now the professor's push $F_{\text{prof}}$ is external:
>
> $$
> F_{\text{prof}} - f = m_2 a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_{\text{prof}} = (19.0\ \mathrm{kg})(1.50\ \mathrm{m/s^2}) + 24.0\ \mathrm N = 53\ \mathrm N .
> $$
>
> Only about a third of the $150\ \mathrm N$ reaches the cart; the rest accelerates the professor. The same force is internal for one choice of system and external for another, so the system must be chosen to fit the question.
>
> *Source: UP1 Examples 5.10–5.11*

^ex-a3-5-2
