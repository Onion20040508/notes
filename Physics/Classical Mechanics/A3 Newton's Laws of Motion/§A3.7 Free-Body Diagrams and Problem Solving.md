The laws are short; applying them is where mistakes happen. This section collects the rules for free-body diagrams and the strategy for turning them into equations, then works through the standard problem types: equilibrium in two dimensions, apparent weight, bodies connected by strings, and forces that vary in time.

## Drawing free-body diagrams

> [!remark] Remark: Rules for a free-body diagram
> 1. **Isolate** the body: circle it, and if it is treated as a particle, draw it as a point at the origin of the axes.
> 2. **Draw every force acting on it**, as an arrow from the point: weight, normal force, friction, tension, spring force, applied forces ([[§A3.6 Common Forces]]). Apart from gravity, every force needs contact, so go around the boundary of the circle and find each contact.
> 3. **Leave out** the net force, $m\vec a$, and every force the body exerts on something else. Never draw both forces of a third-law pair on the same diagram ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^rem-a3-5-1|Remark in §A3.5]]).
> 4. **Resolve** oblique forces into components along the chosen axes, and strike through the original arrow so it is not counted twice.
> 5. **One diagram per body.** If the acceleration is known, it may be drawn beside the diagram (in another colour), never as one of the forces.

^rem-a3-7-1

> [!remark] Remark: Strategy for Newton's-law problems
> 1. List the knowns and the unknowns; identify the principles involved.
> 2. Sketch the situation with all forces.
> 3. Choose the system. Which forces are external depends on this choice, and the third law tells you whether a force acts between parts of the system (internal) or between the system and the outside (external). The result is the free-body diagram.
> 4. Write the second law in components, $\sum F_x = m a_x$, $\sum F_y = m a_y$. Choose axes so that one of them lies along the acceleration if its direction is known (on an incline: parallel and perpendicular to the slope); then the other component of $\vec a$ is zero. Use $\sum\vec F = \vec 0$ for equilibrium.
> 5. If positions, velocities or times are asked for, combine with kinematics.
> 6. Check: units, signs, and limiting cases where the answer is obvious.

^rem-a3-7-2

## Equilibrium in two dimensions

> [!example] Example §A3.7.1: Holding a Crate on a Frictionless Ramp
> A crate of mass $M$ sits on a frictionless ramp inclined at $\theta$. (a) What horizontal force $F$ keeps it from sliding down? (b) What is its acceleration if the force is doubled to $2F$?
>
> Forces: weight $Mg$ (down), normal force $N$ (perpendicular to the ramp), horizontal push $F$ (toward the ramp). Axes: $x$ up the slope, $y$ perpendicular to it. The push has components $F\cos\theta$ up the slope and $F\sin\theta$ into the ramp.
>
> (a) Equilibrium:
>
> $$
> x:\ F\cos\theta - Mg\sin\theta = 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ F = Mg\tan\theta, \qquad y:\ N - Mg\cos\theta - F\sin\theta = 0 \ \Longrightarrow\ N = \frac{Mg}{\cos\theta}.
> $$
>
> (b) With $2F$, the $x$-equation becomes $2F\cos\theta - Mg\sin\theta = Ma$, so $a = 2g\sin\theta - g\sin\theta = g\sin\theta$, up the slope.
>
> The push both holds the crate and presses it into the ramp, so here $N > Mg\cos\theta$ ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^cau-a3-6-1|Caution in §A3.6]]). Doubling the push gives exactly the acceleration the crate would have sliding *down* a bare ramp, but upward.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 10*

^ex-a3-7-1

## Apparent weight

> [!example] Example §A3.7.2: A Scale in an Elevator
> A $75.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ man stands on a bathroom scale in an elevator. What does the scale read?
>
> System: the man; up is positive. The forces on him are his weight $mg$ (down) and the upward force $F_s$ of the scale. By the third law he pushes down on the scale with $F_s$, and that is what the scale reads. The second law gives
>
> $$
> F_s - mg = ma \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_s = m(g + a).
> $$
>
> - Accelerating upward at $1.20\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$: $F_s = (75.0\ \mathrm{kg})(11.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}) = 825\ \mathrm N$, more than his $735\ \mathrm N$ weight.
> - Constant velocity, up or down or at rest: $a = 0$, $F_s = mg = 735\ \mathrm N$.
> - Accelerating downward, $a < 0$: the reading is less than $mg$. In free fall, $a = -g$, it is zero: he is "weightless" ([[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^cau-a3-4-1|Caution in §A3.4]]).
>
> Only the acceleration matters, not the direction of motion. An elevator moving *up* but slowing down has a downward acceleration, so the floor pushes up on a passenger with less than $Mg$ and, by the third law, the passenger pushes down on the floor with the same smaller force: both forces are less than $Mg$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 6.3; PHY 181, TBL 10 and Exam 2 (F21)*

^ex-a3-7-2

## Connected bodies

> [!example] Example §A3.7.3: Two Blocks Joined over a Pulley
> A block $m_1$ on a frictionless horizontal table is tied by a light string, passing over a light frictionless pulley at the edge, to a block $m_2$ hanging below. Find the acceleration and the tension.
>
> One free-body diagram per block. By [[§A3.6 Common Forces#^mod-a3-6-2|Model §A3.6.2]] the tension $T$ is the same at both ends, and when $m_1$ moves right by some distance, $m_2$ moves down by the same distance, so $a_{1x} = -a_{2y} \equiv a$. Block 1's vertical forces balance; block 2 has no horizontal forces:
>
> $$
> \text{block 1, } x:\ T = m_1 a, \qquad \text{block 2, } y:\ T - m_2 g = -m_2 a .
> $$
>
> Adding eliminates $T$: $m_2 g = (m_1 + m_2)a$, so
>
> $$
> a = \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g, \qquad T = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g .
> $$
>
> The tension is *less* than the hanging block's weight $m_2 g$: if it were equal, $m_2$ could not accelerate. Checks: $m_1 \to 0$ gives free fall and $T \to 0$; $m_2 \to 0$ gives $a \to 0$. The same method applied to an Atwood machine (two masses hanging over one pulley, $m_2 > m_1$) gives $a = \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,g$, the unbalanced weight divided by the total mass; for $m_1 = 2.00\ \mathrm{kg}$, $m_2 = 4.00\ \mathrm{kg}$ this is $3.27\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, with $T = m_1(g + a) = 26.1\ \mathrm N$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Examples 6.4–6.5*

## Forces that vary in time

> [!example] Example §A3.7.4: A Baggage Tractor
> A baggage tractor ($650.0\ \mathrm{kg}$) pulls cart A ($250.0\ \mathrm{kg}$) and cart B ($150.0\ \mathrm{kg}$), starting from rest. The horizontal driving force on the tractor grows with time as $F = (820.0\ \mathrm{N/s})\,t$ for $3.00\ \mathrm s$. Find (a) the speed at $t = 3.00\ \mathrm s$ and (b) the force in the cable between tractor and cart A at that moment.
>
> (a) System: tractor and both carts, $M = 1050.0\ \mathrm{kg}$; the cable forces are internal. Horizontally, $F = Ma$, so $a = (0.7810\ \mathrm{m/s^3})\,t$. The acceleration is not constant, so integrate:
>
> $$
> v(t) = \int_0^t a\,dt' = (0.3905\ \mathrm{m/s^3})\,t^2, \qquad v(3.00\ \mathrm s) = 3.51\ \mathrm{m/s}.
> $$
>
> (b) System: the tractor alone. Now the cable's backward pull $T$ is external: $F - T = m_{\text{tractor}}\,a$. Since $a = F/M$,
>
> $$
> T = F\Bigl(1 - \frac{m_{\text{tractor}}}{M}\Bigr) = F\,\frac{m_A + m_B}{M} = (2460\ \mathrm N)\,\frac{400.0}{1050.0} = 937\ \mathrm N .
> $$
>
> The cable carries just the share of the driving force needed to accelerate the carts. When a force depends on time, the constant-acceleration formulas do not apply; $v$ follows from integrating $a(t)$ ([[§A2.4 Velocity and Displacement from Acceleration|§A2.4]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 6.8*

^ex-a3-7-4

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Velocity from a time-dependent acceleration, $v(t) = v(0) + \int_0^t a\,dt'$, is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|Theorem §34.1]].
