With forces defined (§A3.1), the first law says what happens when they balance: the velocity does not change. It makes a *change* of velocity the effect that forces cause, introduces inertia, and picks out the inertial frames in which all three of Newton's laws hold.

## The law

> [!principle] Principle §A3.2.1: Newton's First Law
> A body at rest remains at rest or, if in motion, remains in motion at constant velocity, unless it is acted on by a net external force:
>
> $$
> \vec v = \text{constant} \qquad \text{when} \qquad \vec F_{\text{net}} = \vec 0 .
> $$
>
> "Constant velocity" means neither the speed nor the direction changes: straight-line motion at constant speed, which includes rest ($\vec v = \vec 0$).
>
> *Domain: inertial frames ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]), which the law itself is used to identify; speeds much less than $c$.*
> *Source: UP1 §5.2, eq. (5.2)*

^pr-a3-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Every change of velocity has a cause
> Experience seems to say that moving things stop by themselves. They do not: a block sliding on a rough floor stops quickly, on a floor dusted with talcum powder it slides farther, on an oiled floor farther still, and an air-hockey puck on its cushion of air glides almost without slowing. Extrapolating to no friction and no air resistance, the block would slide in a straight line forever. Friction is the external force that slows it, as the first law requires. The law therefore does not contradict experience; it says that any change in velocity, in magnitude *or* direction, needs a cause, and the cause is a net external force. Galileo first had the idea and Newton made it precise; the step was to ask "what is the cause?" instead of attributing motion to the nature of the body.

^rem-a3-2-1

## Inertia and mass

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.1: Inertia
> **Inertia** is the ability of a body to resist changes in its motion, that is, to resist acceleration. The inertia of a body is measured by its **mass**: a boulder is harder to set moving than a basketball because it has more mass. For this reason the first law is also called the **law of inertia**.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.2*

^def-a3-2-1

The second law makes the measure quantitative: a given net force produces an acceleration inversely proportional to the mass ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^rem-a3-3-2|Remark in §A3.3]]). Mass is also what gravity pulls on; the relation between mass and weight is the subject of [[§A3.4 Mass and Weight]].

## Inertial reference frames

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.2: Inertial Reference Frame
> A reference frame is **inertial** if Newton's first law holds in it: a body on which the net force is zero moves with constant velocity relative to the frame.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.2*

^def-a3-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.2: Which Frames Are Inertial
> A reference frame moving at constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is also inertial. A frame accelerating relative to an inertial frame is not inertial.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.2*

^thm-a3-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $S$ be inertial and let $S'$ move relative to $S$ with velocity $\vec u(t)$. For any body $P$, velocities add ([[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|Theorem §A2.8.1]]): $\vec v_{PS} = \vec v_{PS'} + \vec u$. Differentiating,
>
> $$
> \vec a_{PS} = \vec a_{PS'} + \frac{d\vec u}{dt}.
> $$
>
> Take a body with zero net force. Since $S$ is inertial, $\vec a_{PS} = \vec 0$, so $\vec a_{PS'} = -\,d\vec u/dt$.
> - If $\vec u$ is constant, $\vec a_{PS'} = \vec 0$: the body has constant velocity in $S'$ too, and $S'$ is inertial.
> - If $d\vec u/dt \neq \vec 0$, the force-free body accelerates relative to $S'$. The first law fails there, so $S'$ is not inertial.
>
> The first case also shows that all inertial frames measure the same acceleration for every body. Forces, being interactions between bodies, do not depend on the frame, so $\sum\vec F = m\vec a$ has the same form in every inertial frame.

^der-a3-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^pr-a3-2-1|Principle §A3.2.1]], [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|Theorem §A2.8.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]] (derivative of a sum)

> [!remark] Remark: Inertial frames in practice
> - Within experimental error, a frame at rest relative to the distant ("fixed") stars is inertial, and so is every frame moving uniformly relative to it. A non-rotating frame attached to the Sun is inertial for all practical purposes: its velocity relative to the fixed stars varies by less than one part in $10^{10}$.
> - A frame attached to Earth's surface is not strictly inertial, since Earth rotates and orbits the Sun. But the acceleration of a point on the surface relative to the fixed stars is less than $3.4\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ (under $0.4\%$ of $g$), so an Earth-fixed frame is treated as inertial unless stated otherwise. Where the rotation does matter (weather systems, the Coriolis force) see [[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^def-a3-9-2|Def. §A3.9.2]].
> - No inertial frame is special: the laws of nature are the same in all of them, and one picks whichever is convenient.

^rem-a3-2-2

## Equilibrium

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.3: Equilibrium
> A system is in **equilibrium** when the forces on it balance, $\vec F_{\text{net}} = \vec 0$. By the first law its velocity is then constant:
> - **static equilibrium**: the system is at rest (a parked car: its weight is balanced by the upward push of the pavement);
> - **dynamic equilibrium**: the system moves at constant velocity (a car cruising straight at steady speed: the forward friction of the road on the tyres balances the air drag).
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.2, Example 5.1*

^def-a3-2-3

> [!caution] Caution: Equilibrium is not rest, and zero net force is not zero forces
> - Several forces can act on a body in equilibrium, and the body can be moving; what vanishes is their vector sum.
> - Whether an equilibrium is "static" or "dynamic" depends on the inertial frame: a body at rest in one frame moves uniformly in another.
> - Constant *speed* is not constant *velocity*. A car rounding a curve at steady speed is not in equilibrium; a net force points toward the centre ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^thm-a3-9-1|Theorem §A3.9.1]]).
> - For an extended body, balanced forces are not enough to keep it from turning: the torques must balance too. Equilibrium of a rigid body is defined in [[§A7.1 Conditions for Equilibrium#^def-a7-1-1|Def. §A7.1.1]], with its two conditions in [[§A7.1 Conditions for Equilibrium#^thm-a7-1-1|Theorem §A7.1.1]].

^cau-a3-2-1

> [!example] Example §A3.2.1: The Balancing Force
> The two pushes of [[§A3.1 Forces#^ex-a3-1-1|Example §A3.1.1]] have resultant $\vec F_R = (30.0\,\hat\imath + 40.0\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm N$. What third push $\vec F_3$ puts the skater in equilibrium? We need $\vec F_R + \vec F_3 = \vec 0$, so
>
> $$
> \vec F_3 = -\vec F_R = (-30.0\,\hat\imath - 40.0\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm N,
> $$
>
> of magnitude $50.0\ \mathrm N$, pointing at $233.1^\circ$ from the $+x$ axis (exactly opposite $\vec F_R$). With all three pushes acting, the skater keeps whatever velocity she had: she stays at rest, or glides on in a straight line at constant speed.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.2*

^ex-a3-2-1
