A body moving in a circle accelerates toward the centre even at constant speed ([[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]]). By the second law, some net force must point to the centre. This section asks which real forces supply it (friction on a flat road, the normal force on a banked one, the tension in a string), and explains the "centrifugal" and Coriolis forces that are felt in rotating frames.

## Centripetal force

> [!definition] Definition §A3.9.1: Centripetal Force
> A **centripetal force** is any net force that causes uniform circular motion. It points toward the centre of curvature, perpendicular to the velocity. It is a role, not a new kind of force: tension, gravity, friction, a normal force, or a combination of them can play it.
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.3*

^def-a3-9-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.9.1: Magnitude of the Centripetal Force
> A body of mass $m$ moving uniformly on a circle of radius $r$ with speed $v$ (angular velocity $\omega$) requires a net force toward the centre of magnitude
>
> $$
> F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r} = m r\omega^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.3, eq. (6.3)*

^thm-a3-9-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In uniform circular motion the acceleration points to the centre with magnitude $a_c = v^2/r = r\omega^2$ ([[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]]). The second law, $F_{\text{net}} = ma$, with $a = a_c$ gives $F_c = ma_c$. Solving for the radius, $r = mv^2/F_c$: at a given speed, a larger centripetal force makes a tighter curve.

^der-a3-9-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^def-a3-9-1|Def. §A3.9.1]], [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: No extra arrow for the centripetal force
> The centripetal force never appears as an additional arrow on a free-body diagram. Draw the real forces; then require that their components toward the centre add up to $mv^2/r$. And a car rounding a curve at constant *speed* does have a net force on it, pointing toward the centre of the curve; on a level road it is supplied by friction of the road on the tyres.

^cau-a3-9-1

> [!example] Example §A3.9.1: Friction on a Flat Curve
> A $900.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ car takes an unbanked curve of radius $500.0\ \mathrm m$ at $25.00\ \mathrm{m/s}$. (a) What centripetal force is needed? (b) What is the least coefficient of static friction that allows it?
>
> (a) $F_c = mv^2/r = (900.0\ \mathrm{kg})(25.00\ \mathrm{m/s})^2/(500.0\ \mathrm m) = 1125\ \mathrm N$.
>
> (b) The only horizontal force is the friction of the road on the tyres. The tyres roll without slipping, so it is static friction, at most $\mu_s N = \mu_s mg$ on level ground. We need $mv^2/r \le \mu_s mg$:
>
> $$
> \mu_s \ge \frac{v^2}{rg} = \frac{(25.00\ \mathrm{m/s})^2}{(500.0\ \mathrm m)(9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2})} = 0.13 .
> $$
>
> The mass cancels: how heavily the car is loaded does not matter. Real tyres on dry roads have much larger $\mu_s$, so this curve can be taken faster; with a smaller coefficient (ice) the car slides outward onto a larger circle.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 6.15*

^ex-a3-9-1

## Banked curves

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.9.2: Ideal Banking Angle
> On a frictionless curve of radius $r$ banked at angle $\theta$, a car can follow the curve at exactly one speed $v$, given by
>
> $$
> \tan\theta = \frac{v^2}{rg} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.3, eq. (6.4)*

^thm-a3-9-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Without friction the road can only push perpendicular to its surface, so the forces are the weight $mg$ (down) and the normal force $N$, tilted from the vertical by $\theta$ toward the centre. The net force must be horizontal, toward the centre, of size $mv^2/r$. With vertical and horizontal axes:
>
> $$
> \text{vertical: } N\cos\theta - mg = 0, \qquad \text{horizontal: } N\sin\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r}.
> $$
>
> Dividing the second equation by the first eliminates $N$: $\tan\theta = v^2/(rg)$.

^der-a3-9-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^thm-a3-9-1|Theorem §A3.9.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]

![[ph-cm-a3-9-1.svg]]
*A car on a frictionless banked curve, seen from behind; the centre of the curve is to the left. The normal force (blue) is perpendicular to the road. Its vertical component balances the weight, and its horizontal component (red) is the centripetal force.*

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the banking formula
> - The angle does not depend on the car's mass. Fast, tight curves need steep banking.
> - On the ideal banked curve $N = mg/\cos\theta > mg$: the road pushes harder than on level ground.
> - Friction widens the range of safe speeds around the ideal one, both above and below.
> - Example: a curve of radius $100.0\ \mathrm m$ banked at $31.0^\circ$ has ideal speed $v = \sqrt{rg\tan\theta} = \sqrt{(100.0)(9.80)(0.601)}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 24.3\ \mathrm{m/s}$, about $87\ \mathrm{km/h}$ (UP1 Example 6.16).
> - An aircraft turns the same way: it banks, and the horizontal component of the lift (perpendicular to the wings) supplies the centripetal force while the vertical component balances the weight.

^rem-a3-9-1

## Tension as centripetal force

> [!example] Example §A3.9.2: The Conical Pendulum
> A mass $M$ on a string is whirled at constant speed in a horizontal circle of radius $R$, the string making angle $\varphi$ with the vertical. Find the tension and the angular velocity.
>
> Forces: weight $Mg$ and tension $T$ along the string. Vertically there is no acceleration; horizontally the net force is centripetal, $MR\omega^2$:
>
> $$
> T\cos\varphi = Mg \ \Longrightarrow\ T = \frac{Mg}{\cos\varphi}, \qquad T\sin\varphi = MR\omega^2 \ \Longrightarrow\ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{g\tan\varphi}{R}} .
> $$
>
> This is the geometry of the banked curve with the tension in place of the normal force. The faster the whirl, the larger $\varphi$ and the tension; the string can never be horizontal, since then nothing would balance the weight.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 10*

^ex-a3-9-2

> [!example] Example §A3.9.3: A Pendulum at Its Lowest Point
> A pendulum bob of mass $m$ hangs on a string of length $L$. As it passes the lowest point, the tension is $2mg$. How fast is it moving?
>
> At the lowest point both forces are vertical, so the tangential acceleration there is zero and the only acceleration is the centripetal $v^2/L$, pointing up to the pivot ([[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-3|Theorem §A2.7.3]]). With up positive:
>
> $$
> T - mg = \frac{mv^2}{L} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2mg - mg = \frac{mv^2}{L} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{gL}.
> $$
>
> Whenever the bob moves, the tension at the bottom exceeds its weight; it equals $mg$ only for a bob hanging at rest.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, Exam 2 (F21)*

^ex-a3-9-3

## Rotating frames: inertial forces

> [!remark] Remark: Inertial forces in everyday life
> The **inertial** (fictitious) forces of [[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-3|Def. §A3.6.3]] arise only because the observer's frame accelerates or rotates; no body exerts them and they have no third-law partner. Including them lets one use Newton's second law in a noninertial frame when that is convenient. In an inertial frame each of the following sensations has a plain explanation:
> - A driver turning right feels pushed to the left, toward the door. In Earth's (nearly inertial) frame there is no leftward force: she tends to keep going straight (first law) while the car turns right under her, until the door or seat pushes her right.
> - In an airliner on take-off you feel pressed back into the seat; in fact you tend to stay where you are and the seat pushes you forward.
> - On a rapidly turning merry-go-round a rider feels flung outward; this is the **centrifugal force** of the rotating frame. In the ground frame nothing pushes him out. He must hold on to be pulled *inward*, because without a centripetal force he would move off along the tangent.
> - A centrifuge uses the same inertia: sediment particles tend to move along the tangent while the tube is forced around the circle, so they reach the far end of the tube, where the wall finally supplies their centripetal force.

^rem-a3-9-2

> [!caution] Caution: Centrifugal is not centripetal
> The centripetal force is a real net force, pointing inward, needed in an inertial frame for circular motion. The centrifugal force is an inertial force, pointing outward, that exists only in the rotating frame. In an inertial-frame free-body diagram there is never a centrifugal force.

^cau-a3-9-2

> [!definition] Definition §A3.9.2: Coriolis Force
> The **Coriolis force** is the inertial force in a rotating frame that deflects bodies moving relative to that frame. On a platform rotating counterclockwise (seen from above), a ball rolled straight outward from the centre moves in a straight line relative to the ground, but on the platform its path curves to the right; observers on the platform attribute the curving to the Coriolis force.
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.3*

^def-a3-9-2

> [!remark] Remark: The Coriolis force on Earth
> Seen from above the North Pole, Earth rotates counterclockwise, like the platform. So in the Northern Hemisphere moving air and water are deflected to the right; in the Southern Hemisphere, to the left. Earth's angular velocity is small, so the effect is usually negligible, but it shapes large-scale flows. Air flowing into a low-pressure region is deflected to the right in the north and circulates counterclockwise: hurricanes in the Northern Hemisphere turn counterclockwise, tropical cyclones in the Southern Hemisphere clockwise. Air flowing out of a high-pressure region circulates the other way (clockwise in the north). The same motions can be explained in an inertial frame by inertia and the rotation of the ground beneath; that description is simpler in the sense that every force in it has a physical source.

^rem-a3-9-3
