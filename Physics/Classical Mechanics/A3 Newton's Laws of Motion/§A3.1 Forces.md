Chapter A2 described motion without asking what causes it. This section introduces the cause, force, as a vector quantity, together with the two bookkeeping tools in which Newton's laws are stated: the net external force and the free-body diagram.

## Kinematics and dynamics

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.1: Kinematics and Dynamics
> **Kinematics** describes how objects move: position, velocity, acceleration. **Dynamics** studies how forces affect the motion of objects and systems, that is, the causes of motion. Its foundation is Newton's three laws of motion.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.1*

^def-a3-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: The realm of Newtonian mechanics
> Newton's laws describe motion well only for speeds much less than the speed of light $c$ and for objects larger than about $10^{-9}\ \mathrm m$ (the size of most molecules). Beyond these limits, relativity and quantum mechanics take over (subjects Relativity and Quantum Mechanics, planned). Everything in Chapters A3–A9 lies inside the Newtonian realm.

^rem-a3-1-1

## Force as a vector

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.2: Force
> A **force** is a push or a pull on an object, with a definite magnitude and direction: it is a vector, drawn as an arrow, and forces add by the rules of vector addition ([[§A1.4 Vector Algebra|§A1.4]]).
>
> Quantitatively, a force can be expressed as a multiple of a standard force, for example the restoring force of a spring stretched by a fixed amount. A spring scale measures forces this way.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.1*

^def-a3-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.3: Net External Force
> The **net external force** on an object or system is the vector sum of all external forces acting on it, that is, of the forces exerted on it by things outside it (made precise in [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^def-a3-3-1|Def. §A3.3.1]]):
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{net}} = \sum \vec F = \vec F_1 + \vec F_2 + \cdots .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.1, eq. (5.1)*

^def-a3-1-3

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.4: The Newton
> The SI unit of force is the **newton**: $1\ \mathrm N$ is the force that gives a mass of $1\ \mathrm{kg}$ an acceleration of $1\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$,
>
> $$
> 1\ \mathrm N = 1\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s^2}.
> $$
>
> The definition relies on the second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]). A small apple weighs about $1\ \mathrm N$. In components a force is written $\vec F = F_x\hat\imath + F_y\hat\jmath + F_z\hat k$, each component in newtons.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.1*

^def-a3-1-4

## Contact and field forces

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.5: Contact and Field Forces
> A **contact force** is exerted through direct physical contact between objects: a chair on a seated student, the floor on his feet, the table on his forearms. A **field force** acts without contact, through a field in the space around the body: Earth's gravitational field pulls on the student whether or not he touches anything, and this pull is his weight.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.1*

^def-a3-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: Four fundamental fields
> Physics knows four fundamental force fields: gravitational, electromagnetic, strong nuclear and weak. The strong and weak forces act only over distances of about the size of a nucleus ($10^{-15}\ \mathrm m$), so they play no role in Newtonian mechanics. Contact forces are electromagnetic at bottom: the charges in the skin interact with the charges in the tabletop, and the net result is the contact force. In mechanics this origin does not matter; contact forces are treated as forces in their own right ([[§A3.6 Common Forces]]).

^rem-a3-1-2

## Free-body diagrams

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.6: Free-Body Diagram
> A **free-body diagram** is a sketch of all the external forces acting on an object or system. The object is drawn as a single point (the "free body"), and each external force as an arrow starting at that point. It does **not** show:
> - internal forces, between parts of the system;
> - forces that the object exerts on its environment;
> - the net force itself.
>
> Only external forces appear because only they affect the motion of the object as a whole ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]). Draw one diagram for each object. The full rules are in [[§A3.7 Free-Body Diagrams and Problem Solving]].
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.1*

^def-a3-1-6

> [!example] Example §A3.1.1: Two Skaters Push a Third
> Viewed from above, skater 1 pushes a third skater with $\vec F_1 = 30.0\,\hat\imath\ \mathrm N$ and skater 2 pushes her with $\vec F_2 = 40.0\,\hat\jmath\ \mathrm N$. The net external force (ignoring the vertical forces, which cancel, and friction) is the vector sum:
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{net}} = \vec F_1 + \vec F_2 = (30.0\,\hat\imath + 40.0\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm N, \qquad F_{\text{net}} = \sqrt{(30.0\ \mathrm N)^2 + (40.0\ \mathrm N)^2} = 50.0\ \mathrm N,
> $$
>
> at $\theta = \tan^{-1}(40.0/30.0) = 53.1^\circ$ from the $+x$ axis. Forces combine like any other vectors, component by component; the third skater accelerates along $\vec F_{\text{net}}$, not along either push.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.1*
