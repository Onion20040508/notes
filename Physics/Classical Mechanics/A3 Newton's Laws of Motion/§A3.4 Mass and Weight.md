The second law treats mass as the measure of inertia. Near Earth every mass is also pulled down by gravity. This section names that pull, weight, relates it to mass through the free-fall acceleration, and separates two quantities that everyday language runs together.

## Weight

> [!definition] Definition §A3.4.1: Weight
> The **weight** $\vec w$ of a body is the gravitational force exerted on it by the nearest large body: Earth, or the Moon, or the Sun. Its direction defines "down"; its magnitude is written $w$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.4*

^def-a3-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.4.1: Weight Is Mass Times g
> A body of mass $m$ at a place where the free-fall acceleration is $\vec g$ has weight
>
> $$
> \vec w = m\vec g, \qquad w = mg .
> $$
>
> Near Earth's surface $g = 9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, so a $1.00\ \mathrm{kg}$ body weighs $9.80\ \mathrm N$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.4, eqs. (5.8)–(5.9)*

^thm-a3-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Drop the body where air resistance is negligible. The only force on it is its weight, so $\vec F_{\text{net}} = \vec w$. Galileo showed that, without air resistance, all bodies fall with the same acceleration $\vec g$ ([[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^law-a2-3-2|Law §A2.3.2]]), so $\vec a = \vec g$. The second law $\vec F_{\text{net}} = m\vec a$ gives $\vec w = m\vec g$. The weight is a force that acts whether or not the body is falling; free fall is only the situation in which it can be read off most simply.

^der-a3-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^def-a3-4-1|Def. §A3.4.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^law-a2-3-2|Law §A2.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Weight depends on where you are
> The free-fall acceleration varies slightly over Earth's surface, so the same person weighs a little more in low-lying New Orleans than in Denver, a mile higher. Away from Earth the change is large: on the Moon $g = 1.67\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, and a $5.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ object weighing $49\ \mathrm N$ on Earth weighs only $8.4\ \mathrm N$ there. Its mass is $5.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ in both places.

^rem-a3-4-1

## Mass and weight are different quantities

> [!remark] Remark: Mass versus weight
> | | Mass $m$ | Weight $\vec w$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | kind | scalar | vector (a force) |
> | SI unit | kilogram | newton |
> | what it is | amount of matter; measure of inertia | gravitational pull of Earth (Moon, …) |
> | depends on location | no (in Newtonian physics) | yes, through $g$ |
> | measured by | comparison with the standard kilogram ([[§A1.1 Units, Standards and Conversion\|§A1.1]]) | a force measurement, e.g. a spring scale at rest |
>
> Mass is intrinsic: it is fixed by the numbers of atoms and molecules the body contains, which do not change, so its response to a given force does not change either. Where $g$ is constant, weight is proportional to mass, which is why the two are so easily confused. Medical records that give "weight" in kilograms are really recording mass. In the US the everyday unit of force is the pound: $1\ \mathrm N = 0.225\ \mathrm{lb}$, so a $225$-lb person weighs $1000\ \mathrm N$.

^rem-a3-4-2

## Free fall and "weightlessness"

> [!definition] Definition §A3.4.2: Free Fall
> A body is in **free fall** when the only force acting on it is gravity, that is, when its net external force is its weight. Near Earth's surface this is the motion with constant acceleration $\vec g$ of [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^mod-a2-3-3|Model §A2.3.3]]. Bodies falling near Earth are never exactly in free fall, because the air always exerts some upward resistance ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.4*

^def-a3-4-2

> [!caution] Caution: "Weightless" astronauts have weight
> When space agencies and the media speak of "weightlessness" or "microgravity", they mean free fall in the sense of [[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^def-a3-4-2|Def. §A3.4.2]]. An astronaut in orbit still has weight: Earth's gravity acts on her, and it is exactly what bends her path into an orbit. What is missing is a supporting force. A scale under her feet falls with her and reads zero; a scale measures the force it exerts, not the weight ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^ex-a3-5-1|Example §A3.5.1]], and the elevator in [[§A3.7 Free-Body Diagrams and Problem Solving#^ex-a3-7-2|Example §A3.7.2]]).

^cau-a3-4-1

## Example

> [!example] Example §A3.4.1: Lifting a Stone
> A farmer lifts a stone that weighs $180\ \mathrm N$ (about $40$ lb), giving it an upward acceleration of $1.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. What force does he apply?
>
> System: the stone; up is positive. Only vertical forces act: his upward force $F$ and the weight $w$. The second law needs the mass, which the weight provides: $m = w/g = (180\ \mathrm N)/(9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2}) = 18.4\ \mathrm{kg}$. Then
>
> $$
> F - w = ma \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F = w + ma = 180\ \mathrm N + (18.4\ \mathrm{kg})(1.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}) = 2.1\times10^{2}\ \mathrm N .
> $$
>
> Holding the stone still, or lifting it at constant speed, takes exactly its weight; making it speed up takes an extra $ma \approx 28\ \mathrm N$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 5.8*

^ex-a3-4-1
