The first law says *when* motion changes: only under a nonzero net force. The second law says *by how much*: it fixes the acceleration in terms of the force and the mass. Before it can be stated, one has to decide which forces count.

## Systems and external forces

> [!definition] Definition §A3.3.1: System; External and Internal Forces
> A **system** is the collection of bodies chosen for study; everything else is its environment. A force on the system is **external** if it is exerted by something outside the system, and **internal** if it acts between two parts of the system.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.3*

^def-a3-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Draw the boundary first
> Two students push a stalled car with its driver inside. With the system "car + driver", the pushes, friction from the road, the weight and the normal force are external; the driver's grip on the steering wheel is internal. Only external forces change the motion of the system as a whole: internal forces come in equal and opposite pairs ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law]]) and cancel. Which forces are external depends on the boundary, so the system has to be chosen before any force is listed.

^rem-a3-3-1

## The law

> [!principle] Principle §A3.3.1: Newton's Second Law
> The acceleration of a system points along the net external force, is proportional to it, and is inversely proportional to the system's mass:
>
> $$
> \vec a = \frac{\vec F_{\text{net}}}{m}, \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad \vec F_{\text{net}} = \sum \vec F = m\,\vec a .
> $$
>
> For magnitudes alone, $F_{\text{net}} = m a$.
>
> *Domain: inertial frames ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law]]); speeds much less than $c$ (relativistic momentum: [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-1|REL Def. §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.1 Relativistic Momentum#^def-a3-1-2|REL Def. §A3.1.2]]); bodies far larger than atoms.*
> *Source: UP1 §5.3, eqs. (5.3)–(5.4)*

^pr-a3-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: What the law says, and what it does not
> - It combines two experimental facts: at fixed mass, $a \propto F_{\text{net}}$; at fixed net force, $a \propto 1/m$. Force, mass and acceleration can each be measured on their own, so the law is a statement about nature, not a definition, and it predicts.
> - Read $\vec a = \vec F_{\text{net}}/m$ as cause and response: the environment acts through $\vec F_{\text{net}}$, the system responds with $\vec a$, and the mass measures how sluggish the response is. That is why mass is the measure of inertia ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law]]).
> - First law or second law? If $\sum \vec F = \vec 0$, then $\vec a = \vec 0$: the system is at rest (static equilibrium) or moves with constant velocity (dynamic equilibrium). If $\sum \vec F \neq \vec 0$, it accelerates.

^rem-a3-3-2

> [!caution] Caution: Common slips
> - $m\vec a$ is not a force. It never appears as an arrow on a free-body diagram.
> - $\vec F_{\text{net}}$ is the sum of the **external** forces only, after the system has been chosen.
> - A moving object need not feel a net force: constant velocity means $\sum \vec F = \vec 0$.
> - Vectors cannot be divided. To get a mass from force and acceleration, use magnitudes (or a component along the common direction): $m = F_{\text{net}}/a$.

^cau-a3-3-1

## Component form

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.2: Second Law in Components
> In Cartesian components,
>
> $$
> \sum F_x = m a_x, \qquad \sum F_y = m a_y, \qquad \sum F_z = m a_z .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.3, eq. (5.5)*

^thm-a3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write each force and the acceleration in the basis $\hat\imath, \hat\jmath, \hat k$. The vector sum adds components: $\sum \vec F = \bigl(\sum F_x\bigr)\hat\imath + \bigl(\sum F_y\bigr)\hat\jmath + \bigl(\sum F_z\bigr)\hat k$. Two vectors are equal exactly when their components are, so $\sum \vec F = m\vec a$ splits into the three scalar equations.

^der-a3-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]

## Momentum form

> [!definition] Definition §A3.3.2: Momentum
> The **momentum** of a body of mass $m$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ is $\vec p = m\vec v$. It is a vector, along $\vec v$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.3, eq. (5.7)*

^def-a3-3-2

> [!principle] Principle §A3.3.3: Second Law in Momentum Form
> The net external force equals the rate of change of momentum:
>
> $$
> \vec F_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec p}{dt} .
> $$
>
> This is how Newton stated the law: the instantaneous rate of change of a body's "quantity of motion" equals the net force on it.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.3, eq. (5.6)*

^pr-a3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> If the mass is constant, $\dfrac{d\vec p}{dt} = \dfrac{d(m\vec v)}{dt} = m\,\dfrac{d\vec v}{dt} = m\vec a$, which is [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]. So the momentum form contains the familiar one.

^der-a3-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^def-a3-3-2|Def. §A3.3.2]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Constant mass
> The step $d(m\vec v)/dt = m\,d\vec v/dt$ needs $m$ constant. For a system whose mass changes, such as a rocket burning fuel, $\vec F = m\vec a$ alone is wrong and the momentum form has to be applied to a carefully chosen system ([[§A5.5 Rocket Propulsion#^thm-a5-5-2|Theorem §A5.5.2]]).

^cau-a3-3-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.3.1: Which Force Is Bigger?
> A car is driven by the friction force of the road on its tyres, $\vec F_{\text{friction}}$ (forward), against air drag $\vec F_{\text{drag}}$ (backward).
> - At constant speed the net force is zero (first law), so the two have equal magnitude.
> - While the car speeds up, there must be a net forward force (second law), so friction is larger than drag.
>
> The question looks trivial and is often answered wrongly: moving does not require a net force; *changing* velocity does.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 5.3*

^ex-a3-3-1

> [!example] Example §A3.3.2: Rocket Sled
> Four identical rockets of thrust $T$ push a sled of total mass $m = 2100\ \mathrm{kg}$ against friction $f = 650\ \mathrm N$; the initial acceleration is $a = 49\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. Take the system to be sled, rockets and rider, and the direction of motion as positive. The vertical forces cancel, and horizontally
>
> $$
> F_{\text{net}} = 4T - f = m a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 4T = ma + f = (2100\ \mathrm{kg})(49\ \mathrm{m/s^2}) + 650\ \mathrm N = 1.04 \times 10^5\ \mathrm N,
> $$
>
> so each rocket supplies $T = 2.6 \times 10^4\ \mathrm N$. Such sleds reached accelerations of about $45g \approx 440\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ in tests of human endurance in the early 1960s.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 5.4*

^ex-a3-3-2

> [!example] Example §A3.3.3: Several Forces on a Particle
> A particle of mass $m = 4.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ feels $F_1 = 10.0\ \mathrm N$ at $30^\circ$ above the $+x$ axis, $F_2 = 40.0\ \mathrm N$ along $-y$, $F_3 = 5.0\ \mathrm N$ along $-x$ and $F_4 = 2.0\ \mathrm N$ along $+y$. Apply [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]] in each direction:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> x:&\quad F_1\cos 30^\circ - F_3 = m a_x &&\Longrightarrow\quad a_x = \frac{8.66 - 5.0}{4.0}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} = 0.92\ \mathrm{m/s^2},\\
> y:&\quad F_1\sin 30^\circ + F_4 - F_2 = m a_y &&\Longrightarrow\quad a_y = \frac{5.0 + 2.0 - 40.0}{4.0}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} = -8.3\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> So $\vec a = (0.92\,\hat\imath - 8.3\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, of magnitude $8.3\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, at $276^\circ$ from the $+x$ axis.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 5.7*

^ex-a3-3-3
