Newton's laws connect forces with motion, but a problem first needs the forces themselves. This section catalogues the forces that recur in mechanics (normal force, tension, friction, the spring force), together with the idealisations used for strings and pulleys, and separates real forces from the fictitious ones that appear in accelerating frames.

## Normal force

> [!definition] Definition §A3.6.1: Normal Force
> The **normal force** $\vec N$ is the part of a contact force perpendicular to the surface of contact ("normal" means perpendicular), exerted by a support on the load it carries. It arises because the support deforms slightly: a table sags under a load, like a stiff trampoline, until its elastic restoring force is as large as needed. For a body resting on a horizontal surface with no other vertical forces,
>
> $$
> \vec N = -m\vec g, \qquad N = mg .
> $$
>
> (The symbol $N$ for the normal force is not the unit $\mathrm N$.)
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.6, eqs. (5.11)–(5.12)*

^def-a3-6-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.6.1: Body on an Incline
> A body of mass $m$ on a plane inclined at angle $\theta$ to the horizontal. Take $x$ along the incline (pointing down the slope) and $y$ perpendicular to it. The weight has components
>
> $$
> w_x = mg\sin\theta \ (\text{down the slope}), \qquad w_y = mg\cos\theta \ (\text{into the surface}).
> $$
>
> If no other force has a component perpendicular to the incline, the normal force is $N = mg\cos\theta$. If moreover the incline is frictionless and nothing else acts along it, the body accelerates down the slope with
>
> $$
> a = g\sin\theta ,
> $$
>
> whatever its mass.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.6, eq. (5.13), Example 5.12*

^thm-a3-6-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Tilting the plane by $\theta$ tilts its normal by $\theta$ from the vertical, so the angle between $\vec w$ (vertical) and the $-y$ direction is $\theta$; resolving gives $w_y = w\cos\theta$, $w_x = w\sin\theta$ with $w = mg$. The body does not leave the surface, so $a_y = 0$ and the $y$-component of the second law gives $N - mg\cos\theta = 0$. Along $x$, with only $w_x$ acting, $mg\sin\theta = ma$, so $a = g\sin\theta$.

^der-a3-6-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]

![[ph-cm-a3-6-1.svg]]
*Forces on a body on an incline. The weight $\vec w$ (red) is replaced by its components along the axes (red, dashed): $mg\sin\theta$ down the slope and $mg\cos\theta$ into it. The normal force $\vec N$ (blue) balances the second. The angle between $\vec w$ and the perpendicular to the incline equals the incline angle $\theta$.*

> [!caution] Caution: N is whatever the perpendicular equation says
> $N = mg$ and $N = mg\cos\theta$ are special cases, not rules. The normal force is found from the second law perpendicular to the surface, and it changes whenever something else pushes or pulls across the surface or the body accelerates across it: in an accelerating elevator, with a push at an angle, on a banked curve ([[§A3.7 Free-Body Diagrams and Problem Solving#^ex-a3-7-1|Example §A3.7.1]], [[§A3.7 Free-Body Diagrams and Problem Solving#^ex-a3-7-2|Example §A3.7.2]], [[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^thm-a3-9-2|Theorem §A3.9.2]]). To get the sine and cosine right without memorising, check $\theta \to 0$: the slope component must vanish and $N \to mg$.

^cau-a3-6-1

> [!example] Example §A3.6.1: A Skier on a Slope
> A skier of total mass $60.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ descends a $25^\circ$ slope. With $x$ down the slope, the weight component along the slope is $mg\sin 25^\circ = (60.0)(9.80)(0.4226)\ \mathrm N = 248.5\ \mathrm N$, and $N = mg\cos 25^\circ = 533\ \mathrm N$.
> - Without friction: $a = g\sin 25^\circ = 4.14\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
> - With a friction force $f = 45.0\ \mathrm N$ up the slope: $a = (mg\sin 25^\circ - f)/m = (248.5\ \mathrm N - 45.0\ \mathrm N)/(60.0\ \mathrm{kg}) = 3.39\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
>
> Friction can only reduce the acceleration down the slope; without it, every body slides down the same incline with the same acceleration, just as every body falls with the same $g$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 5.12*

^ex-a3-6-1

## Tension

> [!definition] Definition §A3.6.2: Tension
> **Tension** is the pulling force exerted by a stretched flexible connector (string, rope, cable, chain, tendon). A flexible connector can only pull, never push ("you can't push a rope"), and the force it exerts on whatever is attached to its end points along the connector, away from that body.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.6*

^def-a3-6-2

> [!model] Model §A3.6.2: Ideal String and Pulley
> *Assumptions:* the string is massless, perfectly flexible and inextensible; a pulley it passes over is massless and frictionless.
>
> *Consequences:*
> - The tension $T$ has the same magnitude at every point of the string, and on both sides of the pulley: a pulley changes the direction of the tension, not its size.
> - The string pulls each attached body with force $T$ along the string; by the third law each body pulls back on the string with $T$.
> - Bodies joined by a taut string move with the same speed and the same magnitude of acceleration along the string.
> - A mass $m$ hanging at rest from a string has $T - mg = 0$, so $T = mg$ (for $5.00\ \mathrm{kg}$, $T = 49.0\ \mathrm N$).
>
> *Fails when:* the connector's own weight is comparable to the loads (tension then varies along it, and any real rope sags); the pulley has appreciable mass or friction (the tensions on its two sides differ, a rotation problem: [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation|§A6.3]]); the connector stretches noticeably (then it acts as a spring).
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.6, Fig. 5.24–5.25; §6.1, Example 6.4*

^mod-a3-6-2

> [!example] Example §A3.6.2: The Tightrope Walker
> A $70.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ tightrope walker stands at the middle of a wire, which sags by $5.0^\circ$ on each side. Take as the system the walker (with the point of the wire under him), with $x$ horizontal and $y$ up; the wire's weight is negligible. Horizontally, $T_R\cos 5.0^\circ - T_L\cos 5.0^\circ = 0$, so $T_L = T_R = T$. Vertically,
>
> $$
> 2T\sin 5.0^\circ - mg = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = \frac{mg}{2\sin 5.0^\circ} = \frac{686\ \mathrm N}{2(0.08716)} = 3.94\times10^{3}\ \mathrm N ,
> $$
>
> almost six times his weight. Only the small vertical components hold him up; the large horizontal ones cancel. More generally, a force $F_\perp$ applied perpendicular to a taut connector at its middle produces $T = F_\perp/(2\sin\theta)$, which grows without bound as $\theta \to 0$. That is why no rope can be pulled perfectly straight under its own weight, and why a sideways push on a taut chain tied between a tree and a stuck car pulls hard on the car.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 5.13*

> [!example] Example §A3.6.3: Holding an Unbalanced Atwood Machine
> Masses $M > m$ hang on the two sides of a light, frictionless pulley from a light string, and are kept at rest by an extra push. What is the tension, and what push will do?
>
> The small mass $m$ is at rest with only the tension and its weight on it, so $T - mg = 0$: **$T = mg$**, on both sides of the pulley ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^mod-a3-6-2|Model §A3.6.2]]), not $Mg$ and not anything in between. For $M$ at rest, $T + F_{\text{push}} - Mg = 0$, so the push is upward on $M$ with $F_{\text{push}} = (M - m)g$. Another push also works: downward on $m$ with $(M - m)g$, which raises the tension to $Mg$. The tension is fixed by the equilibrium of the bodies it holds, not by which mass is larger.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 12*

^ex-a3-6-3

## Friction and the spring force

> [!remark] Remark: Friction
> **Friction** is the part of a contact force parallel to the surface, opposing relative motion or its tendency. A body resting on a tilted surface is held by the normal force perpendicular to the surface and by friction along it, which balances $mg\sin\theta$. The rules for its size, static and kinetic, are in [[§A3.8 Friction]].

^rem-a3-6-1

> [!law] Law §A3.6.3: Hooke's Law
> A spring stretched or compressed by a displacement $\vec x$ from its relaxed length exerts a restoring force proportional to the displacement and opposite to it:
>
> $$
> \vec F = -k\,\vec x ,
> $$
>
> along the spring's axis. The **spring constant** $k$ (in $\mathrm{N/m}$) measures the stiffness; $\vec x$ is measured from the relaxed position, where $\vec x = \vec 0$.
>
> *Valid when: the deformation is small enough that the spring returns to its original shape (the elastic regime; for materials in general, [[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli|§A7.2]]).*
> *Source: UP1 §5.6*

^law-a3-6-3

A stretched spring is the standard force of [[§A3.1 Forces#^def-a3-1-2|Def. §A3.1.2]]: cut a rope that holds a $5.00\ \mathrm{kg}$ mass and insert a spring, and its extension measures the $49.0\ \mathrm N$ tension directly.

## Real and fictitious forces

> [!definition] Definition §A3.6.3: Real and Fictitious Forces
> A **real force** has a physical origin: some body exerts it (gravity, a contact force, a tension). A **fictitious** (or **inertial**) force has no physical origin; it appears only because the observer uses a noninertial frame, one that accelerates or rotates relative to an inertial frame. Test: name the body that feels the reaction force ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|Principle §A3.5.1]]). If there is none, the force is fictitious. In an inertial frame all forces are real, and Newton's laws hold in the form given in this chapter.
>
> *Source: UP1 §5.6*

^def-a3-6-3

> [!remark] Remark: A satellite seen from the rotating Earth
> A satellite moving due north above the Northern Hemisphere appears, to an observer on Earth, to drift west as if pushed by a westward force. Nothing pushes it: Earth rotates east underneath it. The "westward force" would need an eastward reaction on Earth, and there is none, so it is fictitious. Earth rotates slowly enough to be almost inertial, so such effects usually need precise experiments; on the scale of weather systems and ocean currents they are plain to see (Coriolis force, [[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^def-a3-9-2|Def. §A3.9.2]]). Unless stated otherwise, all analysis in these notes is done in inertial frames.
