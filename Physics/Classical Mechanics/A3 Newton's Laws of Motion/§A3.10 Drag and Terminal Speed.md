Sliding friction hardly depends on speed (§A3.8); the resistance of a fluid does. This section gives the two standard drag laws, shows how drag caps the speed of a falling body at a terminal speed, and solves the second law when the force depends on the velocity, which turns it into a differential equation.

## Drag forces

> [!definition] Definition §A3.10.1: Drag Force
> The **drag force** $\vec F_D$ is the force a fluid (gas or liquid) exerts on a body moving through it. It opposes the motion of the body relative to the fluid, and its magnitude depends on the speed, in a way that also depends on the body's shape and size and on the fluid.
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.4*

^def-a3-10-1

> [!law] Law §A3.10.1: Quadratic Drag
> For a body moving through a fluid at not too low a speed,
>
> $$
> F_D = \tfrac12\,C\rho A\,v^2 ,
> $$
>
> where $C$ is the dimensionless **drag coefficient**, $\rho$ the density of the fluid and $A$ the area of the body facing the flow. Often written $F_D = bv^2$ with $b = \tfrac12 C\rho A$.
>
> *Valid when: large objects (cyclists, cars, baseballs, skydivers) move through air at ordinary speeds. $C$ is found empirically, usually in a wind tunnel, and is treated as constant although it can depend on speed.*
> *Source: UP1 §6.4, eq. (6.5)*

^law-a3-10-1

> [!remark] Remark: Drag coefficients
> | Object | $C$ |
> |---|---|
> | airfoil | 0.05 |
> | Toyota Camry | 0.28 |
> | sphere | 0.45 |
> | Hummer H2 SUV | 0.64 |
> | skydiver, feet first | 0.70 |
> | bicycle | 0.90 |
> | skydiver, horizontal | 1.0 |
> | circular flat plate | 1.12 |
>
> (Selection from UP1 Table 6.2.) At highway speeds more than half of a car's power goes into overcoming air drag; the most fuel-efficient cruising speed is about $70$–$80\ \mathrm{km/h}$. Streamlined shapes, body suits and shaved skin in racing sports all aim at a smaller $CA$.

^rem-a3-10-1

> [!law] Law §A3.10.2: Stokes' Law
> For a sphere of radius $r$ moving slowly through a fluid of viscosity $\eta$, the drag is proportional to the speed:
>
> $$
> F_s = 6\pi r\eta v .
> $$
>
> More generally, for a body moving at moderate speed through a liquid the resistive force is often approximately linear, $\vec f_R = -b\,\vec v$, with $b$ depending on the body's size and shape and on the liquid.
>
> *Valid when: the object is very small, slow, or in a medium denser than air (bacteria, pollen, dust, sediment in water; a small object sinking in a liquid; a motorboat at moderate speed). Viscosity: [[§A9.6 Viscosity and Turbulence|§A9.6]].*
> *Source: UP1 §6.4, eq. (6.6)*

^law-a3-10-2

## Terminal speed

> [!definition] Definition §A3.10.2: Terminal Velocity
> A body falling through a fluid speeds up until the drag balances its weight. From then on the net force is zero and the velocity stays constant at the **terminal velocity** $\vec v_T$; its magnitude is the terminal speed.
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.4*

^def-a3-10-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.10.3: Terminal Speed with Quadratic Drag
> A body of mass $m$ falling through a fluid with quadratic drag ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^law-a3-10-1|Law §A3.10.1]]), buoyancy neglected, has terminal speed
>
> $$
> v_T = \sqrt{\frac{2mg}{\rho C A}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.4*

^thm-a3-10-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take down as positive. The forces are the weight $mg$ (down) and the drag $\tfrac12 C\rho A v^2$ (up), so $mg - \tfrac12 C\rho A v^2 = ma$. The weight is constant while the drag grows with $v$, until $a = 0$. Then $mg = \tfrac12 C\rho A v_T^2$, and solving for $v_T$ gives the result.

^der-a3-10-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^law-a3-10-1|Law §A3.10.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^def-a3-10-2|Def. §A3.10.2]]

> [!example] Example §A3.10.1: Terminal Speed of Skydivers
> Air density $\rho = 1.21\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$.
> - A $75\ \mathrm{kg}$ skydiver in a compact, vertical position: $A \approx 0.18\ \mathrm{m^2}$, $C \approx 0.70$.
>
> $$
> v_T = \sqrt{\frac{2(75\ \mathrm{kg})(9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2})}{(1.21\ \mathrm{kg/m^3})(0.70)(0.18\ \mathrm{m^2})}} = 98\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 350\ \mathrm{km/h}.
> $$
>
> - An $85\ \mathrm{kg}$ skydiver spread-eagled: $A \approx 0.70\ \mathrm{m^2}$, $C \approx 1.0$, giving $v_T = \sqrt{2(85)(9.80)/((1.21)(1.0)(0.70))}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 44\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 160\ \mathrm{km/h}$.
>
> The heavier diver falls more slowly: $v_T \propto \sqrt{m/(CA)}$, and spreading out quadruples the area. Opening the parachute raises $CA$ enormously and lowers $v_T$ to a safe landing speed.
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.4, Example 6.17*

^ex-a3-10-1

> [!remark] Remark: Size and falling
> Scale an animal down by a factor $10$ in every length: its weight falls by $10^3$, but its cross-section only by $10^2$, so the air resistance per unit weight is ten times larger (J. B. S. Haldane's 1928 argument: a mouse dropped down a mine shaft walks away). By [[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^thm-a3-10-3|Theorem §A3.10.3]], $v_T \propto \sqrt{m/A}$ grows like the square root of the size. A squirrel reaches its small terminal speed within a short fall and survives a drop from a $5\ \mathrm m$ branch that would likely injure a person, who in so short a fall does not come near terminal speed.

^rem-a3-10-2

## Velocity-dependent forces

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.10.4: Falling with Linear Drag
> A body of mass $m$ released from rest falls through a liquid that exerts a resistive force $-b\vec v$. With $y$ measured downward from the release point,
>
> $$
> v(t) = \frac{mg}{b}\bigl(1 - e^{-bt/m}\bigr), \qquad y(t) = \frac{mg}{b}\,t + \frac{m^2 g}{b^2}\bigl(e^{-bt/m} - 1\bigr).
> $$
>
> The velocity approaches the terminal velocity $v_T = mg/b$ as $t \to \infty$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.4*

^thm-a3-10-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With down positive, the second law is the differential equation
>
> $$
> m\frac{dv}{dt} = mg - bv .
> $$
>
> Setting $dv/dt = 0$ gives $v_T = mg/b$. To find $v(t)$, separate the variables, $\dfrac{dv}{g - (b/m)v} = dt$, and integrate from $(t, v) = (0, 0)$:
>
> $$
> \int_0^v \frac{dv'}{g - (b/m)v'} = -\frac{m}{b}\Bigl[\ln\Bigl(g - \frac{b}{m}v'\Bigr)\Bigr]_0^v = -\frac{m}{b}\ln\frac{g - (b/m)v}{g} = t .
> $$
>
> Hence $g - (b/m)v = g\,e^{-bt/m}$, which is the formula for $v(t)$. Integrating once more with $y(0) = 0$,
>
> $$
> y(t) = \int_0^t \frac{mg}{b}\bigl(1 - e^{-bt'/m}\bigr)\,dt' = \frac{mg}{b}\,t + \frac{m^2 g}{b^2}\bigl(e^{-bt/m} - 1\bigr).
> $$

^der-a3-10-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^law-a3-10-2|Law §A3.10.2]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]] (chain rule, for the antiderivative of $1/(g - (b/m)v)$)

> [!example] Example §A3.10.2: A Motorboat Coasting to a Stop
> A motorboat moves at $v_0$ when its motor stops; the water then exerts $f_R = -bv$. (a) Find $v(t)$ and $x(t)$. (b) If it slows from $4.0$ to $1.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in $10\ \mathrm s$, how far does it coast?
>
> (a) $m\,dv/dt = -bv$. Separating, $\int_{v_0}^{v} dv'/v' = -(b/m)\int_0^t dt'$, so $\ln(v/v_0) = -bt/m$ and
>
> $$
> v = v_0\,e^{-bt/m}, \qquad x = \int_0^t v_0 e^{-bt'/m}\,dt' = \frac{mv_0}{b}\bigl(1 - e^{-bt/m}\bigr).
> $$
>
> The boat never quite stops, but it approaches $x_{\max} = mv_0/b$ quickly: at $t = 10\,m/b$, $v \approx 4.5\times10^{-5}\,v_0$ and $x \approx 0.99995\,x_{\max}$.
>
> (b) $1.0 = 4.0\,e^{-(b/m)(10\ \mathrm s)}$ gives $b/m = (\ln 4.0)/(10\ \mathrm s) = 0.139\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $x_{\max} = v_0/(b/m) = (4.0\ \mathrm{m/s})/(0.139\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}) = 29\ \mathrm m$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 6.18*

^ex-a3-10-2

> [!example] Example §A3.10.3: A Shell Fired Straight Up, with Quadratic Drag
> A $10.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ shell is fired vertically upward at $v_0 = 50.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$; air resistance is $F_D = bv^2$ with $b = 0.0100\ \mathrm{kg/m}$. How high does it rise?
>
> Up is positive; on the way up both weight and drag point down: $m\,dv/dt = -mg - bv^2$. The force depends on $v$ and we want a height, so trade time for position with the chain rule, $\dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{dv}{dy}\dfrac{dy}{dt} = v\dfrac{dv}{dy}$:
>
> $$
> v\,\frac{dv}{dy} = -\Bigl(g + \frac{b}{m}v^2\Bigr) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = \int_0^{v_0}\frac{v\,dv}{g + (b/m)v^2} = \frac{m}{2b}\ln\Bigl(1 + \frac{b v_0^2}{mg}\Bigr).
> $$
>
> Numerically $m/(2b) = 500\ \mathrm m$ and $bv_0^2/(mg) = 25.0/98.0 = 0.255$, so $h = (500\ \mathrm m)\ln 1.255 = 114\ \mathrm m$, compared with $v_0^2/(2g) = 128\ \mathrm m$ without air.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 6.9*

^ex-a3-10-3
