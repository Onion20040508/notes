Friction appeared in §A3.6 as the part of a contact force parallel to the surface. Unlike weight or tension, its size is not set by a simple rule but has to be found case by case. This section gives the empirical laws that bound it, distinguishes static from kinetic friction, and applies them on level ground, on inclines and between stacked bodies.

## Static and kinetic friction

> [!definition] Definition §A3.8.1: Friction
> **Friction** is a force that opposes relative motion, or attempted relative motion, between systems in contact. It acts parallel to the contact surface, perpendicular to the normal force.
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.2*

^def-a3-8-1

> [!definition] Definition §A3.8.2: Static and Kinetic Friction
> If two systems in contact are at rest relative to each other, the friction between them is **static friction**. If they slide relative to each other, it is **kinetic friction**.
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.2*

^def-a3-8-2

> [!law] Law §A3.8.1: Laws of Friction
> Let $N$ be the magnitude of the normal force between the surfaces.
> - **Static friction** takes whatever magnitude is needed to prevent slipping, up to a maximum:
>
> $$
> f_s \le \mu_s N, \qquad f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N .
> $$
>
> - **Kinetic friction** has the magnitude
>
> $$
> f_k = \mu_k N ,
> $$
>
> and points opposite the velocity of the body relative to the surface.
>
> The coefficients of static and kinetic friction $\mu_s$, $\mu_k$ are dimensionless, depend on both materials, usually lie between $0$ and $1$, and satisfy $\mu_k \le \mu_s$ (almost always strictly). These are relations between magnitudes, not vector equations: $\vec f$ is perpendicular to $\vec N$.
>
> *Valid when: dry, unlubricated surfaces sliding at small but nonzero speeds, where kinetic friction is nearly independent of speed. They fail for lubricated surfaces and for surfaces sliding fast. Approximate: the coefficients are known to one or two digits.*
> *Source: UP1 §6.2, eqs. (6.1)–(6.2)*

^law-a3-8-1

> [!remark] Remark: Typical coefficients
> | Surfaces | $\mu_s$ | $\mu_k$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | rubber on dry concrete | 1.0 | 0.7 |
> | rubber on wet concrete | 0.5–0.7 | 0.3–0.5 |
> | wood on wood | 0.5 | 0.3 |
> | steel on steel, dry | 0.6 | 0.3 |
> | steel on steel, oiled | 0.05 | 0.03 |
> | Teflon on steel | 0.04 | 0.04 |
> | shoes on ice | 0.1 | 0.05 |
> | bone joint with synovial fluid | 0.016 | 0.015 |
>
> (Selection from UP1 Table 6.1.)

^rem-a3-8-1

> [!remark] Remark: Where friction comes from
> Surfaces that look smooth are rough on a small scale, and only their high spots touch. Part of friction comes from lifting the body over these peaks or breaking them off, part from adhesion between the surface molecules (which is why it depends on the materials, and why rubber soles slip less than leather). The true contact area is a tiny fraction of the apparent one and grows in proportion to the normal force; friction is proportional to the true area. That explains why friction is proportional to $N$ but independent of the apparent contact area. Once sliding, the surfaces have fewer points of contact, so less force keeps a body moving than starts it: $\mu_k < \mu_s$. These are empirical laws, not principles of the rank of Newton's laws.

^rem-a3-8-2

> [!caution] Caution: Static friction is not always μs N
> - $f_s = \mu_s N$ only at the point of slipping. Otherwise the static friction is an unknown, found from the second law like any other unknown force.
> - Friction opposes *relative* motion (or its tendency), not necessarily the motion of the body. It can be the force that drives a body forward: the road on a car's tyres, the floor on a walker's foot, a truck bed on its load ([[§A3.8 Friction#^ex-a3-8-2|Example §A3.8.2]]).
> - $N$ is not always $mg$: pushing down on a body, or pulling up on it, changes $N$ and therefore the friction.

^cau-a3-8-1

> [!example] Example §A3.8.1: Pushing a Crate
> A $20.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ crate rests on a level floor with $\mu_s = 0.700$ and $\mu_k = 0.600$. A horizontal force $P$ is applied. Find the friction force for $P = 20.0,\ 30.0,\ 120.0$ and $180.0\ \mathrm N$.
>
> Vertically $N = mg = 196\ \mathrm N$, so $f_{s,\max} = (0.700)(196\ \mathrm N) = 137\ \mathrm N$. Horizontally $P - f = ma_x$.
> - For $P = 20.0$, $30.0$ and $120.0\ \mathrm N$, all below $137\ \mathrm N$: the crate stays at rest, and static friction equals the push, $f_s = 20.0$, $30.0$ and $120.0\ \mathrm N$.
> - For $P = 180.0\ \mathrm N > 137\ \mathrm N$: the crate slides, friction becomes kinetic, $f_k = (0.600)(196\ \mathrm N) = 118\ \mathrm N$, and $a_x = (180.0 - 118)\ \mathrm N/(20.0\ \mathrm{kg}) = 3.1\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
>
> Static friction matches the push until it reaches its maximum; once the crate breaks free, the friction *drops* to the kinetic value.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 6.10*

^ex-a3-8-1

## Friction on an incline

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.8.2: Sliding Down an Incline with Friction
> A body on a plane inclined at $\theta$, with friction coefficients $\mu_s$, $\mu_k$ and no other forces besides weight, normal force and friction:
> - it can stay at rest if and only if $\tan\theta \le \mu_s$;
> - while it slides down, its acceleration down the slope is
>
> $$
> a = g\,(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta);
> $$
>
> - in particular, it slides at constant velocity exactly when $\mu_k = \tan\theta$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §6.2, Examples 6.11, 6.14*

^thm-a3-8-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take $x$ down the slope and $y$ perpendicular to it. Perpendicular to the slope nothing accelerates, so $N = mg\cos\theta$ ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^thm-a3-6-1|Theorem §A3.6.1]]).
> - At rest, friction must balance the downslope weight component: $f_s = mg\sin\theta$. By [[§A3.8 Friction#^law-a3-8-1|Law §A3.8.1]] this is possible iff $mg\sin\theta \le \mu_s mg\cos\theta$, i.e. $\tan\theta \le \mu_s$.
> - Sliding down, kinetic friction $\mu_k N$ points up the slope: $mg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta = ma$, which gives $a$. Setting $a = 0$ gives $\mu_k = \tan\theta$.

^der-a3-8-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.8 Friction#^law-a3-8-1|Law §A3.8.1]], [[§A3.6 Common Forces#^thm-a3-6-1|Theorem §A3.6.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Using the incline result
> - A snowboarder on a $13^\circ$ slope with $\mu_k = 0.20$: $a = (9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2})(\sin 13^\circ - 0.20\cos 13^\circ) = 0.29\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. On a gentler slope, or with more friction, $a < 0$: she slows down.
> - Measuring $\mu_k$: if the friction force is known, $\mu_k = f_k/N = f_k/(mg\cos\theta)$. The skier of [[§A3.6 Common Forces#^ex-a3-6-1|Example §A3.6.1]] ($60.0\ \mathrm{kg}$, $25^\circ$, $f_k = 45.0\ \mathrm N$) has $\mu_k = 45.0/533 = 0.084$; since this is far below $\tan 25^\circ = 0.47$, she accelerates. At constant velocity the friction would have to equal $mg\sin\theta$, and then $\mu_k = \tan\theta$. At home: tilt a book until a coin on it slides at constant speed (a tap may be needed to start it); then $\mu_k = \tan\theta$. The coin does not *start* sliding until the steeper angle $\tan^{-1}\mu_s$.
> - Mass cancels in every one of these results, because both the driving force and the friction are proportional to $m$.

^rem-a3-8-3

## Friction that drives, and several surfaces

> [!example] Example §A3.8.2: A Crate on an Accelerating Truck
> A $50.0\ \mathrm{kg}$ crate rests on a truck bed with $\mu_s = 0.400$, $\mu_k = 0.300$. Find the friction on the crate when the truck accelerates forward at (a) $2.00\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, (b) $5.00\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. Work in the ground frame; $N = mg = 490\ \mathrm N$, so $f_{s,\max} = 196\ \mathrm N$.
>
> (a) Assume the crate does not slip, so it shares the truck's acceleration. The only horizontal force on it is friction, which must therefore point forward: $f_s = ma = (50.0)(2.00)\ \mathrm N = 100\ \mathrm N < 196\ \mathrm N$. The assumption holds.
>
> (b) Not slipping would need $f_s = (50.0)(5.00)\ \mathrm N = 250\ \mathrm N > 196\ \mathrm N$: impossible, so the crate slips. Kinetic friction $f_k = (0.300)(490\ \mathrm N) = 147\ \mathrm N$, still forward, gives the crate $a = 2.94\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ relative to the ground; relative to the truck it slides backward at $2.94 - 5.00 = -2.06\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
>
> Friction points *forward*, along the crate's acceleration: it opposes the crate's motion relative to the bed (backward), not its motion relative to the ground. The method (assume no slipping, then check $f_s \le \mu_s N$) is the standard one.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 6.13*

^ex-a3-8-2

> [!example] Example §A3.8.3: Two Stacked Crates
> Two crates, each of mass $m$, are stacked. The upper one is tied to a wall by a horizontal cable; the lower one is pulled away from the wall (to the left) by a horizontal force $F$. Between the crates and between the lower crate and the floor the coefficients are $\mu_s = \mu$ and $\mu_k = 0.25\,\mu$. Find $F$ and the cable tension when the lower crate slides at constant velocity.
>
> *Upper crate* (at rest): $N_1 = mg$ from the lower crate. The lower crate slides left beneath it, so kinetic friction on the upper crate points left, $0.25\,\mu mg$; the cable balances it:
>
> $$
> T = 0.25\,\mu m g .
> $$
>
> *Lower crate* (constant velocity, so $\sum\vec F = \vec 0$): weight $mg$; push $N_1 = mg$ down from the upper crate (third law); floor normal force $N_2 = 2mg$; friction from the upper crate $0.25\,\mu mg$ to the right (the third-law partner of the friction on the upper crate); kinetic friction from the floor $0.25\,\mu(2mg)$ to the right. Hence
>
> $$
> F = 0.25\,\mu mg + 0.50\,\mu mg = 0.75\,\mu m g .
> $$
>
> To *start* it moving, both surfaces must slip at once, so $F$ must first exceed $\mu mg + \mu(2mg) = 3\mu mg$, four times the force that keeps it going. Note that the floor's normal force carries both crates, and that the lower crate feels friction on two faces.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, Exam 2 (F21)*

^ex-a3-8-3

> [!remark] Remark: Push or pull?
> A crate of mass $M$ is moved at constant velocity by a handle at angle $\varphi$ to the floor, with kinetic coefficient $\mu_k$. Pushing down along the handle presses the crate into the floor, $N = Mg + F\sin\varphi$; pulling up along it lifts the crate, $N = Mg - F\sin\varphi$. From $F\cos\varphi = \mu_k N$:
>
> $$
> F_{\text{push}} = \frac{\mu_k Mg}{\cos\varphi - \mu_k\sin\varphi}, \qquad F_{\text{pull}} = \frac{\mu_k Mg}{\cos\varphi + \mu_k\sin\varphi},
> $$
>
> so pulling takes less force. (For pushing, if $\tan\varphi \ge 1/\mu_k$, no push however large will move the crate.)
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 12*

^rem-a3-8-4
