**Kinematics** describes motion through position, velocity and acceleration, without asking what causes it; the causes, forces, are the subject of dynamics (Chapter A3). This section answers the first questions about a moving object, for motion along a straight line: where is it, how far has it moved, and how fast? The one-dimensional quantities here are the components of the vectors of [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components|§A1.3]] along a single axis.

## Position and displacement

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.1: Frame of Reference; Position
> A **frame of reference** is a set of axes, with an origin and a positive direction, relative to which positions and motion are described. Often it is fixed to Earth, but it may move: the position of a passenger is naturally described in the frame of the airplane. The **position** $x(t)$ of an object moving along a line is its coordinate on the axis at time $t$. (For vertical motion the coordinate is called $y$.)
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.1*

^def-a2-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.2: Displacement; Distance Traveled
> The **displacement** is the change in position,
>
> $$
> \Delta x = x_f - x_0 ,
> $$
>
> final minus initial. It carries a sign that gives its direction. For a motion in several legs the **total displacement** is the sum of the individual ones, $\Delta x_{\text{Total}} = \sum_i \Delta x_i$. The **distance traveled** is the total length of the path, $\sum_i \lvert\Delta x_i\rvert$ over legs in which the direction does not change. It is never negative.
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.1, eqs. (3.1)–(3.2)*

^def-a2-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Displacement is not distance traveled
> Always $\lvert\Delta x_{\text{Total}}\rvert \le$ distance traveled, with equality exactly when the motion never reverses. A professor who paces $2$ m right and then $4$ m left has displacement $-2$ m, magnitude $2$ m, but has walked $6$ m. A round trip has zero displacement.

^cau-a2-1-1

## Velocity and speed

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.3: Average Velocity
> If an object is at $x_1$ at time $t_1$ and at $x_2$ at time $t_2$, its **average velocity** over the interval is
>
> $$
> \bar v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1} .
> $$
>
> Like the displacement it has a sign. On a graph of $x$ against $t$ it is the slope of the chord joining the two points.
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.1, eq. (3.3)*

^def-a2-1-3

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.4: Instantaneous Velocity
> The **instantaneous velocity**, or simply the **velocity**, is the limit of the average velocity as the interval shrinks to zero, the derivative of the position:
>
> $$
> v(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{x(t + \Delta t) - x(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{dx}{dt} .
> $$
>
> On an $x$–$t$ graph it is the slope of the tangent line at time $t$. Its dimension is $\mathrm{LT^{-1}}$ and its SI unit is $\mathrm{m/s}$. For a power law $x = At^n$, $v = nAt^{n-1}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.2, eqs. (3.4), (3.7)*

^def-a2-1-4

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.5: Speed
> **Speed** has no direction. The **average speed** is the total distance traveled divided by the elapsed time. The **instantaneous speed** is the magnitude of the instantaneous velocity, $\lvert v(t)\rvert$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.2, eqs. (3.5)–(3.6)*

^def-a2-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: Reading an x–t graph
> The sign of the slope gives the direction of motion: positive slope, moving toward $+x$; negative slope, toward $-x$. Where the graph turns around (a maximum or minimum of $x$) the tangent is horizontal and $v = 0$: the object is momentarily at rest while reversing. A straight segment means constant velocity. Two particles moving at $+7.0$ and $-7.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ have different velocities but the same speed.

^rem-a2-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Average speed is not the magnitude of the average velocity
> Average speed uses the distance traveled, average velocity uses the displacement. They agree only if the motion never reverses; otherwise $\lvert\bar v\rvert$ is smaller. After a round trip $\bar v = 0$, while the average speed is positive. By contrast, the *instantaneous* speed always equals $\lvert v\rvert$: over a vanishing interval there is no time to turn back.

^cau-a2-1-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.1.1: Delivering Flyers
> Jill walks east (positive) along her street: $0.5$ km out in $9$ min, back home in $9$ min, out to $1.0$ km in $15$ min, then $1.75$ km west in $25$ min, where she rests. Her positions are $x = 0, 0.5, 0, 1.0, -0.75$ km at $t = 0, 9, 18, 33, 58$ min. Then
>
> $$
> \Delta x_{\text{Total}} = (0.5 - 0.5 + 1.0 - 1.75)\ \mathrm{km} = -0.75\ \mathrm{km}, \qquad \text{distance} = (0.5 + 0.5 + 1.0 + 1.75)\ \mathrm{km} = 3.75\ \mathrm{km},
> $$
>
> $$
> \bar v = \frac{-0.75\ \mathrm{km}}{58\ \mathrm{min}} = -0.013\ \mathrm{km/min}, \qquad \text{average speed} = \frac{3.75\ \mathrm{km}}{58\ \mathrm{min}} = 0.065\ \mathrm{km/min}.
> $$
>
> She ends $0.75$ km west of home. Someone walking steadily west at $0.013\ \mathrm{km/min}$ from the same start would arrive at the same moment.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 3.1*

^ex-a2-1-1

> [!example] Example §A2.1.2: Velocity versus Speed
> A particle moves as $x(t) = 3.0\,t - 3.0\,t^2$ (m, s). By [[§A2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity#^def-a2-1-4|Def. §A2.1.4]],
>
> $$
> v(t) = 3.0 - 6.0\,t\ \ \mathrm{m/s}: \qquad v(0.25\ \mathrm s) = 1.5\ \mathrm{m/s}, \quad v(0.50\ \mathrm s) = 0, \quad v(1.0\ \mathrm s) = -3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}.
> $$
>
> The speeds are $1.5$, $0$ and $3.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$. The particle moves toward $+x$ until $t = 0.50$ s, where it turns around at $x = 0.75$ m, and it is back at the origin at $t = 1.0$ s. Over the first second $\bar v = 0$, yet it covered $1.5$ m, so its average speed was $1.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 3.4*

^ex-a2-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Velocity is the derivative of position ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^def-28-1|Def. §28.1]]): $\bar v$ is the difference quotient and $v$ its limit.
> - By the Mean Value Theorem ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-3|Theorem §29.3]]), for smooth motion the instantaneous velocity equals the average velocity at some instant inside every interval, as for the parallel tangent and chord in the figure. For $x = 3.0t + 0.5t^3$ (UP1 Example 3.3), $\bar v = 9.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$ on $[1.0, 3.0]$ s, and $v = 3.0 + 1.5t^2$ reaches that value at $t = 2.08$ s.
