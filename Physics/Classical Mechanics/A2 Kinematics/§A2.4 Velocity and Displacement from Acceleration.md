Differentiation takes position to velocity to acceleration ([[§A2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity|§A2.1]], [[§A2.2 Acceleration|§A2.2]]). Dynamics usually supplies the acceleration, so the practical problem runs the other way: given $a(t)$ and the initial state, find $v(t)$ and $x(t)$. Integration does this for any acceleration, and for a constant one it recovers the equations of [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]].

## Integrating the motion

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.1: Velocity and Position from Acceleration
> If the acceleration $a(t)$ is known and $v(0) = v_0$, $x(0) = x_0$, then
>
> $$
> v(t) = v_0 + \int_0^t a(t')\,dt', \qquad x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t v(t')\,dt' .
> $$
>
> In indefinite form, $v(t) = \int a(t)\,dt + C_1$ and $x(t) = \int v(t)\,dt + C_2$, with the constants of integration fixed by the initial conditions.
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.6, eqs. (3.18)–(3.19)*

^thm-a2-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By definition $dv/dt = a$. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives $\int_0^t a(t')\,dt' = \int_0^t \frac{dv}{dt'}\,dt' = v(t) - v(0)$. The same argument with $dx/dt = v$ gives the second formula.

^der-a2-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Acceleration#^def-a2-2-2|Def. §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity#^def-a2-1-4|Def. §A2.1.4]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Areas under graphs
> An integral is a signed area. So the change in velocity between two times is the area under the $a$–$t$ graph, and the displacement is the area under the $v$–$t$ graph, both counted negative below the axis. The distance traveled is the *unsigned* area, $\int \lvert v\rvert\,dt$. Slopes take a graph down the chain $x \to v \to a$; areas take it back up.

^rem-a2-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.2: The Constant-Acceleration Equations Recovered
> For constant $a$, [[§A2.4 Velocity and Displacement from Acceleration#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]] gives
>
> $$
> v(t) = v_0 + at, \qquad x(t) = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2 ,
> $$
>
> and the average velocity over $[0, t]$ is exactly $\tfrac12(v_0 + v)$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.6*

^thm-a2-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\int_0^t a\,dt' = at$, so $v = v_0 + at$. Then $\int_0^t (v_0 + at')\,dt' = v_0 t + \tfrac12 at^2$, so $x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12at^2$. Dividing the displacement by $t$ gives $\bar v = v_0 + \tfrac12 at = \tfrac12\bigl(v_0 + (v_0 + at)\bigr) = \tfrac12(v_0 + v)$. This is the step that [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]] took from the trapezoid picture.

^der-a2-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Velocity and Displacement from Acceleration#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]], [[§A2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity#^def-a2-1-3|Def. §A2.1.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Constants and range of validity
> - Leaving out the constant of integration, or fixing it at the wrong instant, is the commonest slip. Fix $C_1$ from the velocity and $C_2$ from the position, both at the same reference time.
> - A formula for $a(t)$ usually describes the motion only over some interval. Beyond it the integrated $v(t)$ and $x(t)$ may describe something that does not happen, such as a docking boat that starts to reverse.
> - If $a(t)$ changes form at some instant, integrate piece by piece. Velocity and position are continuous, so the final values of one piece are the initial values of the next.

^cau-a2-4-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.4.1: A Motorboat Coming to the Dock
> A motorboat moving at $v_0 = 5.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ starts to slow down at $t = 0$, with $a(t) = -\tfrac14\,t\ \mathrm{m/s^3}$. Take $x_0 = 0$. By [[§A2.4 Velocity and Displacement from Acceleration#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]],
>
> $$
> v(t) = 5.0 - \int_0^t \tfrac14 t'\,dt' = 5.0 - \tfrac18 t^2 \ \ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad x(t) = \int_0^t \bigl(5.0 - \tfrac18 t'^2\bigr)\,dt' = 5.0\,t - \tfrac1{24} t^3 \ \ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> The boat stops when $\tfrac18 t^2 = 5.0$, at $t = \sqrt{40}\ \mathrm s = 6.3\ \mathrm s$, having moved
>
> $$
> x(6.3\ \mathrm s) = 5.0(6.32) - \tfrac{1}{24}(6.32)^3\ \mathrm m = 31.6\ \mathrm m - 10.5\ \mathrm m = 21.1\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> This is two thirds of $v_0 t_{\text{stop}}$, because $v$ falls off slowly at first. After $6.3$ s the formulas give a negative velocity: the boat would back away from the dock if the same acceleration continued.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 3.17*

^ex-a2-4-1

> [!example] Example §A2.4.2: A Bottle Rocket
> A model rocket starts from rest on the ground and moves straight up with constant acceleration $a_R$ while its fuel lasts, for a time $t_R$; then it moves in free fall. Take $y$ up. The acceleration is piecewise constant, so integrate each stage, with the end of the first stage as the start of the second:
> - *Powered stage:* $v = a_R t$ and $y = \tfrac12 a_R t^2$. At burnout the rocket has $v_1 = a_R t_R$ and $h_1 = \tfrac12 a_R t_R^2$.
> - *Free fall:* $y = h_1 + v_1 t' - \tfrac12 g t'^2$, with $t' = t - t_R$. It lands when $y = 0$, at $t' = \bigl(v_1 + \sqrt{v_1^2 + 2gh_1}\bigr)/g$, taking the positive root. Since $v_1^2 + 2gh_1 = a_R t_R^2\,(a_R + g)$, the total flight time is
>
> $$
> T = t_R + t' = t_R\left[1 + \frac{a_R + \sqrt{a_R(a_R + g)}}{g}\right].
> $$
>
> *Checks:* the bracket is dimensionless, so $T$ has the dimension of $t_R$. For $t_R \to 0$ there is no flight, $T \to 0$. For $a_R \to 0$ the rocket never leaves the ground, and $T \to t_R$ is just the burn time. The highest point is $h_1 + v_1^2/(2g) = \tfrac12 a_R t_R^2\,(1 + a_R/g)$.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 3*

^ex-a2-4-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - [[§A2.4 Velocity and Displacement from Acceleration#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]] is the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]). Conversely, $\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^t a\,dt' = a(t)$ for continuous $a$ ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-4|451 Thm. §34.4]]).
> - Two velocity functions with the same derivative differ by a constant ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-5|451 Cor. §29.5]]). This is why $a(t)$ together with one initial velocity fixes $v(t)$ uniquely.
