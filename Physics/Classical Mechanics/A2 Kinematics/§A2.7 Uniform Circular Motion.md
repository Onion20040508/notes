In one dimension, constant speed means zero acceleration. In two dimensions it does not: a particle going around a circle at constant speed keeps changing the direction of its velocity, so it accelerates ([[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^rem-a2-5-3|Remark]]). This section finds that acceleration, which points to the center, and then adds the tangential acceleration that appears when the speed changes too.

## Centripetal acceleration

> [!definition] Definition §A2.7.1: Uniform Circular Motion
> A particle is in **uniform circular motion** if it moves on a circle of fixed radius $r$ at constant speed $v$. Examples are a point on a propeller spinning at a steady rate, or the tip of a clock hand.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.4*

^def-a2-7-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.7.1: Centripetal Acceleration
> In uniform circular motion the acceleration points toward the center of the circle and has magnitude
>
> $$
> a_C = \frac{v^2}{r} .
> $$
>
> It is called the **centripetal** ("center-seeking") acceleration.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.4, eq. (4.27)*

^thm-a2-7-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a short time $\Delta t$ the position vector turns from $\vec r(t)$ to $\vec r(t + \Delta t)$ and the velocity from $\vec v(t)$ to $\vec v(t + \Delta t)$. Both pairs have equal lengths ($r$ and $v$), and the velocities turn through the same angle as the positions, since $\vec v \perp \vec r$. So the triangle formed by the two position vectors and $\Delta\vec r$ is similar to the triangle formed by the two velocities and $\Delta\vec v$, and both are isosceles. Hence
>
> $$
> \frac{\lvert\Delta\vec v\rvert}{v} = \frac{\lvert\Delta\vec r\rvert}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\lvert\Delta\vec v\rvert}{\Delta t} = \frac{v}{r}\,\frac{\lvert\Delta\vec r\rvert}{\Delta t} \;\xrightarrow{\ \Delta t \to 0\ }\; a = \frac vr \cdot v = \frac{v^2}{r} .
> $$
>
> As $\Delta t \to 0$ the base angles of the velocity triangle approach $90^\circ$, so $\Delta\vec v$ becomes perpendicular to $\vec v$. A vector perpendicular to the tangent points along the radius, here inward.

^der-a2-7-1

> [!definition] Definition §A2.7.2: Angular Frequency; Period
> As the particle moves, its position vector sweeps out the angle $\theta(t)$ measured from the $+x$ axis. In uniform circular motion $\theta = \omega t$, where the constant $\omega$ is the **angular frequency**, in radians per second. The **period** $T$ is the time for one revolution:
>
> $$
> \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T}, \qquad v = \frac{2\pi r}{T} = r\omega .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.4*

^def-a2-7-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.7.2: Equations of Uniform Circular Motion
> For counterclockwise motion on a circle of radius $A$ centered at the origin, starting on the $+x$ axis,
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \vec r(t) &= A\cos\omega t\,\hat\imath + A\sin\omega t\,\hat\jmath,\\
> \vec v(t) &= -A\omega\sin\omega t\,\hat\imath + A\omega\cos\omega t\,\hat\jmath,\\
> \vec a(t) &= -A\omega^2\cos\omega t\,\hat\imath - A\omega^2\sin\omega t\,\hat\jmath = -\omega^2\,\vec r(t).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> So $\lvert\vec v\rvert = A\omega$, $\vec v \perp \vec r$, and $\lvert\vec a\rvert = A\omega^2 = v^2/A = 4\pi^2 A/T^2$, directed toward the center.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.4, eqs. (4.28)–(4.30)*

^thm-a2-7-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\vec r(t)$ has length $A$ and makes the angle $\omega t$ with the $x$ axis ([[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^def-a1-3-7|Def. §A1.3.7]]). Differentiate component by component, using $\frac{d}{dt}\cos\omega t = -\omega\sin\omega t$ and $\frac{d}{dt}\sin\omega t = \omega\cos\omega t$ (chain rule). Then $\vec v \cdot \vec r = -A^2\omega\sin\omega t\cos\omega t + A^2\omega\cos\omega t\sin\omega t = 0$, so the velocity is tangent. A second differentiation gives $\vec a = -\omega^2\vec r$, which points from the particle to the origin. Finally, $A\omega^2 = (A\omega)^2/A = v^2/A$, in agreement with [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]].

^der-a2-7-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-a2-5-2|Def. §A2.5.2]], [[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-a2-5-3|Def. §A2.5.3]], [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^def-a2-7-2|Def. §A2.7.2]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|Theorem §28.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Constant speed, changing acceleration
> - Uniform circular motion has constant *speed*, not constant velocity, and its acceleration is not zero.
> - The magnitude $v^2/r$ is constant, but the direction of $\vec a$ turns with the particle. It is not a constant-acceleration motion, and the equations of [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]] do not apply.
> - If the object is released (a string snaps), it leaves along the tangent, in the direction of its velocity at that instant, not along the radius.

## Nonuniform circular motion

> [!definition] Definition §A2.7.3: Tangential Acceleration
> If the speed of a particle on a circle changes, it has a **tangential acceleration**, along the tangent to the circle, whose component is the rate of change of the speed:
>
> $$
> a_T = \frac{d\lvert\vec v\rvert}{dt} .
> $$
>
> It points along $\vec v$ when the particle speeds up and against $\vec v$ when it slows down.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.4, eq. (4.31)*

^def-a2-7-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.7.3: Total Acceleration in Circular Motion
> For motion on a circle of radius $r$ with speed $v(t)$, the acceleration is the sum of two perpendicular parts:
>
> $$
> \vec a = \vec a_C + \vec a_T = -\frac{v^2}{r}\,\hat r + \frac{dv}{dt}\,\hat t, \qquad \lvert\vec a\rvert = \sqrt{a_C^2 + a_T^2} ,
> $$
>
> with $\hat r$ pointing outward from the center and $\hat t$ along the direction of motion. The centripetal part changes the direction of the velocity, and the tangential part changes its magnitude.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.4, eq. (4.32)*

^thm-a2-7-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The text states the decomposition; a short calculation proves it. For counterclockwise motion, the polar unit vectors at angle $\theta(t)$ are $\hat r = \cos\theta\,\hat\imath + \sin\theta\,\hat\jmath$ and $\hat t = -\sin\theta\,\hat\imath + \cos\theta\,\hat\jmath$ ([[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^def-a1-3-7|Def. §A1.3.7]]). The velocity is tangent, $\vec v = v\,\hat t$, and $v = r\,d\theta/dt$. By the product and chain rules,
>
> $$
> \frac{d\vec v}{dt} = \frac{dv}{dt}\,\hat t + v\,\frac{d\hat t}{dt}, \qquad \frac{d\hat t}{dt} = -\frac{d\theta}{dt}\,(\cos\theta\,\hat\imath + \sin\theta\,\hat\jmath) = -\frac{v}{r}\,\hat r .
> $$
>
> So $\vec a = \frac{dv}{dt}\,\hat t - \frac{v^2}{r}\,\hat r$. The two parts are perpendicular, so their magnitudes combine by Pythagoras. For constant $v$ this reduces to [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.7.1: Ladybug and Ant on a Merry-Go-Round
> A ladybug sits at the rim of a merry-go-round turning once per second; an ant sits halfway to the axis.
> - *Angular speed.* Both sweep out one full turn per second, so both have $\omega = 2\pi\ \mathrm{rad/s}$: the same. The ladybug has twice the *speed*, $v = r\omega$.
> - *Acceleration, steady rotation.* There is no tangential part, so the ladybug's acceleration points from its position straight toward the axis, with magnitude $\omega^2 r = (39.5\ \mathrm{s^{-2}})\,r$. The ant's is half as large.
> - *Slowing down.* The radial component still points toward the axis, with magnitude $v^2/r$ at that instant, since the ladybug is still going around. The tangential component points opposite to the ladybug's velocity.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 7*

^ex-a2-7-1

> [!example] Example §A2.7.2: Training for High Acceleration
> An astronaut in a centrifuge rides at the end of an arm $r = 4\ \mathrm m$ long. What speed gives an acceleration of $49\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, about $5g$? From $a_C = v^2/r$,
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{a_C\,r} = \sqrt{(49\ \mathrm{m/s^2})(4\ \mathrm m)} = \sqrt{196}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 14\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> This is a modest $50\ \mathrm{km/h}$: high accelerations need tight circles more than high speeds. Similarly, a jet at $134\ \mathrm{m/s}$ pulling $1g$ flies a circle of radius $v^2/g = 1.8$ km.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, Exam 1, F21; UP1 Example 4.10*

^ex-a2-7-2

> [!example] Example §A2.7.3: Total Acceleration
> A particle moves on a circle of radius $r = 2.0$ m with speed $v(t) = c_1 - c_2/t^2$, where $c_1 = 4.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $c_2 = 6.0\ \mathrm{m\cdot s}$. At $t = 2.0$ s:
>
> $$
> v = 4.0 - \frac{6.0}{(2.0)^2} = 2.5\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad a_C = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{(2.5)^2}{2.0} = 3.13\ \mathrm{m/s^2}, \qquad a_T = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{2c_2}{t^3} = \frac{12.0}{8.0} = 1.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.
> $$
>
> By [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-3|Theorem §A2.7.3]], $\lvert\vec a\rvert = \sqrt{3.13^2 + 1.5^2}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} = 3.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, at $\tan^{-1}(3.13/1.5) = 64^\circ$ from the tangent, inward and forward, since the particle is speeding up.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 4.12*
