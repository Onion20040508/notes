Every position and velocity so far was measured in some frame of reference ([[§A2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity#^def-a2-1-1|Def. §A2.1.1]]), usually Earth. A passenger walking in a train, a boat crossing a river and a plane flying in a wind each have one velocity relative to the moving medium and another relative to the ground. This section relates the descriptions of one motion in two frames that move relative to each other. It closes kinematics and prepares the notion of an inertial frame on which Newton's laws rest.

## Positions and velocities in two frames

> [!definition] Definition §A2.8.1: Relative Position and Relative Velocity
> For a particle $P$ and frames $S$ and $S'$:
> - $\vec r_{PS}$ is the position of $P$ measured in $S$, and $\vec v_{PS}$ its velocity relative to $S$;
> - $\vec r_{S'S}$ is the position of the origin of $S'$ measured in $S$, and $\vec v_{S'S}$ the velocity of $S'$ relative to $S$.
>
> The first subscript names what moves; the second names the frame it is observed from.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.5*

^def-a2-8-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.8.1: Addition of Relative Velocities
> $$
> \vec r_{PS} = \vec r_{PS'} + \vec r_{S'S}, \qquad \vec v_{PS} = \vec v_{PS'} + \vec v_{S'S} .
> $$
>
> The velocity of a particle relative to $S$ equals its velocity relative to $S'$ plus the velocity of $S'$ relative to $S$. Chaining frames, $\vec v_{PC} = \vec v_{PA} + \vec v_{AB} + \vec v_{BC}$. Taking $P$ to be the origin of $S$ itself gives $\vec v_{SS'} = -\vec v_{S'S}$. These relations hold for frames whose axes do not rotate relative to each other, and for speeds much less than $c$, where all observers share one time ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^thm-a1-3-4|REL Theorem §A1.3.4]]; relativistic form: [[§A2.3 Relativistic Velocity Transformation#^thm-a2-3-1|REL Theorem §A2.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.5, eqs. (4.33)–(4.36)*

^thm-a2-8-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The vector from the origin of $S$ to $P$ is the vector from the origin of $S$ to the origin of $S'$, followed by the vector from there to $P$: $\vec r_{PS} = \vec r_{S'S} + \vec r_{PS'}$ (tail-to-head addition). With one common time $t$ and non-rotating axes, differentiate both sides with respect to $t$ ([[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-a2-5-2|Def. §A2.5.2]]) to get the velocity relation. Applying it twice, through an intermediate frame, gives the chain.

^der-a2-8-1

*Uses:* [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^def-a2-8-1|Def. §A2.8.1]], [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^def-a1-3-4|Def. §A1.3.4]], [[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-a2-5-2|Def. §A2.5.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The subscript rule
> In $\vec v_{PE} = \vec v_{PT} + \vec v_{TE}$, the inner subscripts on the right (the connecting frame, T) match and "cancel", and the outer ones give the left side. A passenger (P) walking toward the back of a train (T) at $2\ \mathrm{m/s}$, in a train moving east at $10\ \mathrm{m/s}$ relative to Earth (E), has $\vec v_{PE} = (-2 + 10)\ \mathrm{m/s}\ \hat\imath = 8\ \mathrm{m/s}\ \hat\imath$, east. Write the equation with the subscripts first, then insert the vectors. Reversing the order of the subscripts reverses a vector.

^rem-a2-8-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.8.2: Accelerations in Two Frames
> $$
> \vec a_{PS} = \vec a_{PS'} + \vec a_{S'S} .
> $$
>
> If $S'$ moves at constant velocity relative to $S$, then $\vec a_{S'S} = \vec 0$ and $\vec a_{PS} = \vec a_{PS'}$: observers in uniform relative motion measure the same acceleration.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.5, eqs. (4.37)–(4.38)*

^thm-a2-8-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate $\vec v_{PS} = \vec v_{PS'} + \vec v_{S'S}$ with respect to the common time ([[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-a2-5-3|Def. §A2.5.3]]). If $\vec v_{S'S}$ is constant, its derivative vanishes.

^der-a2-8-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.8 Relative Motion#^thm-a2-8-1|Theorem §A2.8.1]], [[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-a2-5-3|Def. §A2.5.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why equal accelerations matter
> Velocities depend on the frame, but accelerations agree in all frames moving uniformly relative to one another. Newton's second law relates force to acceleration, not to velocity, so it holds equally in all such frames. These are the inertial frames of [[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]].

^rem-a2-8-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.8.1: A Car Seen from a Truck
> A truck drives south at $70$ km/h and a car drives east at $80$ km/h, both toward the same intersection. Take $\hat\imath$ east and $\hat\jmath$ north, with Earth (E) as the connecting frame: $\vec v_{CE} = 80\,\hat\imath$ km/h and $\vec v_{TE} = -70\,\hat\jmath$ km/h. Then
>
> $$
> \vec v_{CT} = \vec v_{CE} + \vec v_{ET} = \vec v_{CE} - \vec v_{TE} = (80\,\hat\imath + 70\,\hat\jmath)\ \mathrm{km/h},
> $$
>
> of magnitude $\sqrt{80^2 + 70^2} = 106$ km/h, at $\tan^{-1}(70/80) = 41.2^\circ$ north of east. From the truck, the car approaches from the southwest, moving partly *north*, although relative to the ground it never moves north at all.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 4.13*

^ex-a2-8-1

> [!example] Example §A2.8.2: Flying in a Wind
> A plane flies at $300$ km/h relative to the air, and the wind blows *from* the northeast at $90$ km/h. The pilot wants to travel due north over the ground. Take $\hat\imath$ east and $\hat\jmath$ north, with P the plane, A the air and G the ground: $\vec v_{PG} = \vec v_{PA} + \vec v_{AG}$. The wind blows toward the southwest, so $\vec v_{AG} = (-63.6\,\hat\imath - 63.6\,\hat\jmath)$ km/h. The ground velocity must be $\vec v_{PG} = V\,\hat\jmath$, so
>
> $$
> \vec v_{PA} = \vec v_{PG} - \vec v_{AG} = 63.6\,\hat\imath + (V + 63.6)\,\hat\jmath, \qquad \lvert\vec v_{PA}\rvert = 300 \ \Longrightarrow\ V + 63.6 = \sqrt{300^2 - 63.6^2} = 293.2 .
> $$
>
> The ground speed is $V = 230$ km/h. The heading is $\sin^{-1}(63.6/300) = 12^\circ$ east of north: the plane points partly into the wind so as to track due north.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 4.14*

^ex-a2-8-2

> [!example] Example §A2.8.3: Down the River and Back
> You paddle a distance $L$ downstream and back up, in a river flowing at speed $u$, with a boat that moves at speed $v$ relative to the water ($v > u$). Relative to the bank your speed is $v + u$ downstream and $v - u$ upstream, so the round trip takes
>
> $$
> T = \frac{L}{v + u} + \frac{L}{v - u} = \frac{2Lv}{v^2 - u^2} .
> $$
>
> *Checks:* in still water, $u = 0$ gives $T = 2L/v$, as expected. As $u \to v$, $T \to \infty$, because you can no longer make headway upstream. Since $v^2 - u^2 \le v^2$, always $T \ge 2L/v$: the shortest trip is in still water, $u = 0$. The time gained going downstream never makes up for the extra time spent going up.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 6*

^ex-a2-8-3
