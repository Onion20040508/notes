Most motion follows curved paths. Position, displacement, velocity and acceleration carry over from one dimension ([[§A2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity|§A2.1]]–[[§A2.4 Velocity and Displacement from Acceleration|§A2.4]]) as vectors ([[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components|§A1.3]]), and every vector equation splits into one-dimensional equations, one per axis. This is the key to projectile and circular motion in the sections that follow.

## Position, displacement and velocity

> [!definition] Definition §A2.5.1: Position Vector; Displacement Vector
> In a Cartesian frame, the **position vector** of a particle at $P(x(t), y(t), z(t))$ is the vector from the origin to $P$:
>
> $$
> \vec r(t) = x(t)\,\hat\imath + y(t)\,\hat\jmath + z(t)\,\hat k .
> $$
>
> The **displacement** from time $t_1$ to $t_2$ is $\Delta\vec r = \vec r(t_2) - \vec r(t_1)$, the vector from the first position to the second. The total displacement of a sequence of moves is the vector sum of the individual displacements.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.1, eqs. (4.1)–(4.3)*

^def-a2-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Displacement is the shortest route
> $\lvert\Delta\vec r\rvert$ is the straight-line distance between the end points, never more than the length of the path actually followed. A satellite in a circular polar orbit $400$ km up ($r = 6770$ km) that goes from above the North Pole to latitude $-45^\circ$ has $\Delta\vec r = (4787\,\hat\imath - 11\,557\,\hat\jmath)$ km, of magnitude $12\,509$ km. Along the orbit it actually covers an arc of $135^\circ$, about $15\,950$ km, and the route "$4787$ km east, then $11\,557$ km south" would be longer too.

^rem-a2-5-1

> [!definition] Definition §A2.5.2: Average and Instantaneous Velocity Vectors
> The **average velocity** between $t_1$ and $t_2$ and the **instantaneous velocity** are
>
> $$
> \vec v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\vec r(t_2) - \vec r(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1}, \qquad \vec v(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\vec r(t + \Delta t) - \vec r(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\vec r}{dt} .
> $$
>
> In components, $\vec v = v_x\hat\imath + v_y\hat\jmath + v_z\hat k$ with $v_x = dx/dt$, $v_y = dy/dt$, $v_z = dz/dt$. The speed is $\lvert\vec v\rvert$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.1, eqs. (4.4)–(4.7)*

^def-a2-5-2

> [!remark] Remark: The velocity is tangent to the path
> The chord $\Delta\vec r$ joins two nearby points of the path. As $\Delta t \to 0$ its direction turns into the direction of the tangent, so $\vec v$ is tangent to the trajectory at every instant and points the way the particle is going.

^rem-a2-5-2

## Acceleration

> [!definition] Definition §A2.5.3: Acceleration Vector
> The **acceleration** is the rate of change of the velocity vector:
>
> $$
> \vec a(t) = \frac{d\vec v}{dt} = \frac{dv_x}{dt}\,\hat\imath + \frac{dv_y}{dt}\,\hat\jmath + \frac{dv_z}{dt}\,\hat k = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}\,\hat\imath + \frac{d^2y}{dt^2}\,\hat\jmath + \frac{d^2z}{dt^2}\,\hat k .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.2, eqs. (4.8)–(4.10)*

^def-a2-5-3

> [!remark] Remark: Acceleration need not point along the velocity
> In two or three dimensions $\vec a$ can point anywhere relative to $\vec v$. Its part along $\vec v$ changes the speed, and its part perpendicular to $\vec v$ turns the direction of motion. Uniform circular motion ([[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]]) has only the second part: constant speed, nonzero acceleration.

^rem-a2-5-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.5.1: Constant Acceleration in Vector Form
> If $\vec a$ is constant, then
>
> $$
> \vec v(t) = \vec v_0 + \vec a\,t, \qquad \vec r(t) = \vec r_0 + \vec v_0\,t + \tfrac12\vec a\,t^2 .
> $$
>
> Each component obeys the one-dimensional equations of [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]] separately; for $x$:
>
> $$
> v_x = v_{0x} + a_x t, \qquad x = x_0 + v_{0x}t + \tfrac12 a_x t^2, \qquad v_x^2 = v_{0x}^2 + 2a_x(x - x_0),
> $$
>
> and likewise for $y$ and $z$, with the same $t$ in all three.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.2, eqs. (4.11)–(4.18)*

^thm-a2-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-a2-5-3|Def. §A2.5.3]], $dv_x/dt = a_x$, a constant, and similarly for $y$ and $z$. Each component is therefore a one-dimensional motion with constant acceleration, and [[§A2.4 Velocity and Displacement from Acceleration#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]] gives its velocity and position. Reassembling the components with $\hat\imath, \hat\jmath, \hat k$ gives the vector form ([[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]).

^der-a2-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-a2-5-2|Def. §A2.5.2]], [[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-a2-5-3|Def. §A2.5.3]], [[§A2.4 Velocity and Displacement from Acceleration#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]], [[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^thm-a1-4-1|Theorem §A1.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Independence of perpendicular motions
> Mathematically, the components $x(t)$, $y(t)$, $z(t)$ are separate functions, and a vector equation is three scalar ones ([[§A1.4 Vector Algebra#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]]). Physically, a ball dropped from rest and one thrown horizontally at the same instant from the same height are at the same height at every flash of a strobe light: the horizontal motion does not affect the vertical one. The thrown ball also covers equal horizontal distances between flashes, because nothing pushes it horizontally after release. Dynamics explains why: Newton's second law holds axis by axis ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]), and gravity is vertical. With air resistance, which depends on the full velocity, the two motions are coupled and the independence is only approximate.
>
> Summing the three component equations $v_i^2 = v_{0i}^2 + 2a_i\,\Delta x_i$ gives a coordinate-free form for constant $\vec a$, $\;v^2 = v_0^2 + 2\,\vec a \cdot \Delta\vec r$. This anticipates the work–energy theorem ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]]).

^rem-a2-5-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.5.1: Velocity from a Position Function
> A particle has $\vec r(t) = 2.0\,t^2\,\hat\imath + (2.0 + 3.0\,t)\,\hat\jmath + 5.0\,t\,\hat k$ (m, s). Differentiating component by component,
>
> $$
> \vec v(t) = 4.0\,t\,\hat\imath + 3.0\,\hat\jmath + 5.0\,\hat k\ \ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad \vec v(2.0\ \mathrm s) = (8.0\,\hat\imath + 3.0\,\hat\jmath + 5.0\,\hat k)\ \mathrm{m/s},
> $$
>
> with speed $\sqrt{8.0^2 + 3.0^2 + 5.0^2} = 9.9\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Between $1.0$ s and $3.0$ s,
>
> $$
> \vec v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{(18\,\hat\imath + 11\,\hat\jmath + 15\,\hat k) - (2\,\hat\imath + 5\,\hat\jmath + 5\,\hat k)}{2.0}\ \mathrm{m/s} = (8.0\,\hat\imath + 3.0\,\hat\jmath + 5.0\,\hat k)\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> The average over $[1, 3]$ s equals the velocity at the midpoint, because $\vec v$ is linear in $t$ (the acceleration is constant, $\vec a = 4.0\,\hat\imath\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$). In general the two differ.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 4.3*

^ex-a2-5-1

> [!example] Example §A2.5.2: A Skier
> A skier accelerates at $2.1\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ down a $15^\circ$ slope. With $x$ horizontal, $y$ up and the origin at the lodge, at $t = 0$ she is at $\vec r_0 = (75.0\,\hat\imath - 50.0\,\hat\jmath)$ m with $\vec v_0 = (4.1\,\hat\imath - 1.1\,\hat\jmath)$ m/s, already moving along the slope. Resolve the acceleration, keeping an extra digit: $a_x = (2.1)\cos 15^\circ = 2.03\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $a_y = -(2.1)\sin 15^\circ = -0.54\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. By [[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^thm-a2-5-1|Theorem §A2.5.1]], at $t = 10.0$ s,
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> x &= 75.0 + (4.1)(10.0) + \tfrac12(2.03)(10.0)^2 = 217\ \mathrm m, & v_x &= 4.1 + (2.03)(10.0) = 24.4\ \mathrm{m/s},\\
> y &= -50.0 - (1.1)(10.0) - \tfrac12(0.54)(10.0)^2 = -88\ \mathrm m, & v_y &= -1.1 - (0.54)(10.0) = -6.5\ \mathrm{m/s}.
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> So $\vec r = (217\,\hat\imath - 88\,\hat\jmath)$ m and $\vec v = (24.4\,\hat\imath - 6.5\,\hat\jmath)$ m/s, a speed of $25\ \mathrm{m/s}$, about $56$ mi/h. (Rounding $a_x$ to $2.0\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ first would give $216$ m and $24.1$ m/s; see [[§A1.2 Dimensional Analysis, Estimates, Significant Figures#^cau-a1-2-1|Caution: Round at the end]].) The two components were computed independently and share only the time.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 4.6*

^ex-a2-5-2
