A thrown ball, a fireworks shell and a dropped supply crate all move under gravity alone. Projectile motion is constant-acceleration motion in two dimensions ([[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^thm-a2-5-1|Theorem §A2.5.1]]) with the acceleration pointing straight down. It therefore splits into a horizontal motion at constant velocity and a vertical free fall ([[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^mod-a2-3-3|Model §A2.3.3]]), linked only by the time.

## The model and its equations

> [!model] Model §A2.6.1: Projectile
> A **projectile** is a body moving under gravity alone near Earth's surface, that is, in free fall ([[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^mod-a2-3-3|Model §A2.3.3]]) with a horizontal component of velocity. Its path is called the **trajectory**. The body is treated as a point with constant acceleration
>
> $$
> \vec a = -g\,\hat\jmath, \qquad a_x = 0, \quad a_y = -g ,
> $$
>
> with $x$ horizontal and $y$ vertically up.
>
> *Assumptions:* air resistance negligible; $g$ uniform in magnitude and direction, so heights and ranges are small compared with Earth's radius and the ground is flat. *Fails* when drag matters: real shells and balls fall short of the predictions, and the angle of maximum range drops below $45^\circ$. It also fails for ranges comparable to Earth's size. Earth then curves away beneath the projectile, the range exceeds the flat-Earth value, and at about $8\ \mathrm{km/s}$ horizontally the projectile never lands: it is in orbit ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.3*

^mod-a2-6-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.6.2: Equations of Projectile Motion
> For launch from $(x_0, y_0)$ with speed $v_0$ at angle $\theta_0$ above the horizontal, $v_{0x} = v_0\cos\theta_0$ and $v_{0y} = v_0\sin\theta_0$, and
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &\text{horizontal:} && v_x = v_{0x} \ (\text{constant}), \qquad x = x_0 + v_{0x}\,t,\\
> &\text{vertical:} && v_y = v_{0y} - gt, \qquad y = y_0 + v_{0y}\,t - \tfrac12 g t^2, \qquad v_y^2 = v_{0y}^2 - 2g(y - y_0).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> At any time, $v = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}$, and the velocity makes the angle $\tan^{-1}(v_y/v_x)$ with the horizontal.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.3, eqs. (4.19)–(4.23)*

^thm-a2-6-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $a_x = 0$ and $a_y = -g$ into the component equations of [[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^thm-a2-5-1|Theorem §A2.5.1]]. The initial components come from [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^thm-a1-3-2|Theorem §A1.3.2]], and the speed and angle from the same theorem applied to $\vec v$.

^der-a2-6-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.6 Projectile Motion#^mod-a2-6-1|Model §A2.6.1]], [[§A2.5 Motion in Two and Three Dimensions#^thm-a2-5-1|Theorem §A2.5.1]], [[§A1.3 Scalars, Vectors and Components#^thm-a1-3-2|Theorem §A1.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Strategy
> 1. Resolve the initial velocity into $v_{0x}$ and $v_{0y}$.
> 2. Treat the two motions separately. The vertical motion alone fixes the time: how long the projectile is in the air, when it reaches a given height. Time is the only variable the two motions share.
> 3. Use that time in the horizontal motion.
> 4. Recombine the components for the displacement and the velocity. A sketch shows which root of a quadratic is wanted.

## Level ground: height, time of flight, range

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.6.3: Maximum Height, Time of Flight, Trajectory and Range
> For a projectile launched from the origin with speed $v_0$ at angle $\theta_0$:
> - **Maximum height** above the launch point: $h = \dfrac{v_{0y}^2}{2g} = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin^2\theta_0}{2g}$.
> - **Trajectory**, a parabola: $\;y = (\tan\theta_0)\,x - \dfrac{g}{2(v_0\cos\theta_0)^2}\,x^2$.
>
> If it lands at the same height as it was launched:
> - **Time of flight:** $T_{\text{tof}} = \dfrac{2v_0\sin\theta_0}{g}$.
> - **Range:** $R = \dfrac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta_0}{g}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §4.3, eqs. (4.24)–(4.26)*

^thm-a2-6-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At the apex $v_y = 0$, so $0 = v_{0y}^2 - 2gh$. For level ground, set $y = 0$: $t\,(v_0\sin\theta_0 - \tfrac12 gt) = 0$. The root $t = 0$ is the launch and the other is $T_{\text{tof}}$. Eliminating time with $t = x/(v_0\cos\theta_0)$ in $y = (v_0\sin\theta_0)t - \tfrac12 gt^2$ gives the trajectory. Setting $y = 0$ in it gives $x = 0$ or $x = 2v_0^2\sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0/g$, and $2\sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0 = \sin 2\theta_0$. Equivalently, $R = v_{0x}\,T_{\text{tof}}$.

^der-a2-6-3

*Uses:* [[§A2.6 Projectile Motion#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the range formula
> - $R \propto v_0^2$: twice the launch speed, four times the range.
> - $\sin 2\theta_0$ is largest at $\theta_0 = 45^\circ$, which therefore gives the maximum range.
> - Complementary angles $\theta_0$ and $90^\circ - \theta_0$ give the **same range** at the same speed, because $\sin(180^\circ - 2\theta_0) = \sin 2\theta_0$. The steeper shot goes higher and stays up longer.
> - $T_{\text{tof}}$ and $R$ scale as $1/g$: on the Moon, where $g$ is about one sixth of Earth's, the same launch stays up six times as long and goes six times as far.
> - The apex is reached at half the time of flight, at horizontal distance $R/2$.

^rem-a2-6-2

> [!caution] Caution: Level ground only
> $T_{\text{tof}}$ and $R$ assume landing at the launch height. If the landing point is higher or lower (a ball caught in the stands, a crate dropped from a plane), solve $y(t) = y_{\text{landing}}$, a quadratic in $t$, and choose the root that belongs to the motion.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.6.1: A Fireworks Shell
> A shell is fired at $v_0 = 70.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $75.0^\circ$ above the horizontal, timed to explode at the apex. Here $v_{0y} = (70.0)\sin 75.0^\circ = 67.6\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $v_{0x} = (70.0)\cos 75.0^\circ = 18.1\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Then
>
> $$
> h = \frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g} = \frac{(67.6\ \mathrm{m/s})^2}{2(9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2})} = 233\ \mathrm m, \qquad t = \frac{v_{0y}}{g} = 6.90\ \mathrm s, \qquad x = v_{0x}\,t = 125\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> The displacement to the apex is $\vec s = (125\,\hat\imath + 233\,\hat\jmath)$ m, of magnitude $264$ m at $61.8^\circ$ above the horizontal. That is less than the launch angle, because the path has already bent over. With air resistance the real shell needs a larger initial speed to reach the same height.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 4.7*

^ex-a2-6-1

> [!example] Example §A2.6.2: Supply Crate from a Climbing Plane
> A plane climbing at $55^\circ$ above the horizontal with speed $75\ \mathrm{m/s}$ releases a supply crate $150$ m above the ground. At release the crate has the plane's velocity: $v_{0x} = (75)\cos 55^\circ = 43.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $v_{0y} = (75)\sin 55^\circ = 61.4\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Take $y$ up from the ground.
> - *Time in the air:* $0 = 150 + 61.4\,t - 4.90\,t^2$ gives $t = \dfrac{61.4 + \sqrt{61.4^2 + 4(4.90)(150)}}{2(4.90)}\ \mathrm s = 14.6\ \mathrm s$. The other root is negative.
> - *Horizontal distance:* $x = (43.0\ \mathrm{m/s})(14.6\ \mathrm s) = 629\ \mathrm m$. The crate must be released $629$ m before the target, measured horizontally.
> - *Impact velocity:* $v_x = 43.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $v_y = 61.4 - (9.80)(14.6) = -81.9\ \mathrm{m/s}$. As a check, $\lvert v_y\rvert = \sqrt{v_{0y}^2 + 2g(150\ \mathrm m)}$.
> - *Where is the plane?* Its horizontal velocity is the crate's, so it is directly above the impact point, at height $150 + (61.4)(14.6) = 1050$ m.
>
> The crate first rises for $6.3$ s before falling: it was never "dropped" from rest.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 5*

^ex-a2-6-2
