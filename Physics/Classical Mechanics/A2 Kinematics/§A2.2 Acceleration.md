Velocity is the rate of change of position ([[§A2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity|§A2.1]]); acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, obtained by the same limiting process one level up. It is the kinematic quantity that dynamics ties to force ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]), so getting its sign and direction right matters more than anything else in this chapter.

## Average and instantaneous acceleration

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.1: Average Acceleration
> The **average acceleration** over an interval is the change in velocity divided by the elapsed time:
>
> $$
> \bar a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v_f - v_0}{t_f - t_0} .
> $$
>
> Its SI unit is $\mathrm{m/s^2}$: meters per second, per second.
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.3, eq. (3.8)*

^def-a2-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.2: Instantaneous Acceleration
> The **instantaneous acceleration** is the limit of the average acceleration as $\Delta t \to 0$:
>
> $$
> a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} .
> $$
>
> On a $v$–$t$ graph it is the slope of the tangent line. Where $v(t)$ has a maximum or a minimum, $a = 0$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.3, eq. (3.9)*

^def-a2-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Direction of the acceleration
> Acceleration points along the *change* of velocity $\Delta\vec v$, not along the motion. A velocity can change in magnitude (speeding up, slowing down), in direction, or both. A runner who slows to a stop and runs back at the same speed has accelerated, although the speed is the same before and after. A subway train braking into a station accelerates opposite to its motion. The word "deceleration" names no direction, so it is avoided here: in a chosen coordinate system the acceleration is simply positive or negative.

^rem-a2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.1: Speeding Up and Slowing Down
> For motion along a line, with $v \ne 0$:
>
> $$
> \frac{d\lvert v\rvert}{dt} = \frac{v}{\lvert v\rvert}\,a .
> $$
>
> The object speeds up when $v$ and $a$ have the same sign and slows down when they have opposite signs. The sign of $a$ alone does not decide.
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.3, Example 3.6*

^thm-a2-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The text reads this off its examples; a short proof uses the chain rule. Differentiate $\lvert v\rvert^2 = v^2$: $\;2\lvert v\rvert\,\dfrac{d\lvert v\rvert}{dt} = 2v\,\dfrac{dv}{dt} = 2va$. Divide by $2\lvert v\rvert \ne 0$.

^der-a2-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Acceleration#^def-a2-2-2|Def. §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity#^def-a2-1-5|Def. §A2.1.5]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Zero velocity is not zero acceleration
> - At the top of a vertical throw, $v = 0$ for an instant, but $a$ is still $g = 9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ downward. If $a$ were zero there, the ball would stay up.
> - "Negative acceleration" does not mean "slowing down". With $v < 0$ and $a < 0$ the object speeds up in the negative direction ([[§A2.2 Acceleration#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]]).
> - Acceleration depends on how the velocity is changing *now*, not on how the motion began. A ball thrown downward accelerates at exactly $g$ the instant after release, the same as one dropped, if air resistance is negligible.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, Exam 1, F21 (first point); TBL 3 (third point)*

^cau-a2-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Average versus instantaneous
> Accelerations in nature range from about $0.25\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ (a high-speed train) through $9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ (free fall) to about $10^9\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ (a proton in the Large Hadron Collider). They need not be steady. If the acceleration barely varies over an interval, the average is close to the value at any instant. If it varies strongly (a package jolted back and forth on a conveyor), the motion must be cut into short intervals over which the acceleration is nearly constant.

^rem-a2-2-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.2.1: A Racehorse Leaves the Gate
> A racehorse goes from rest to $15.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ due west in $1.80$ s. Take east as positive. Then $v_0 = 0$ and $v_f = -15.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$, so
>
> $$
> \bar a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{-15.0\ \mathrm{m/s}}{1.80\ \mathrm s} = -8.33\ \mathrm{m/s^2} .
> $$
>
> The acceleration is $8.33\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ toward the west. The horse is *speeding up*, because $v$ and $a$ are both negative. The sign only reports the direction in the chosen axes.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 3.5*

^ex-a2-2-1

> [!example] Example §A2.2.2: Reading Velocity and Acceleration Together
> A particle has $v(t) = 20t - 5t^2$ (m/s, with $t$ in s). By [[§A2.2 Acceleration#^def-a2-2-2|Def. §A2.2.2]], $a(t) = 20 - 10t\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$.
>
> | $t$ (s) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $v$ (m/s) | 15 | 20 | 15 | $-25$ |
> | $a$ (m/s²) | 10 | 0 | $-10$ | $-30$ |
>
> At $t = 1$ s, $v$ and $a$ are both positive: speeding up. At $t = 2$ s, $a = 0$ and $v$ is at its maximum. At $t = 3$ s they have opposite signs: slowing down. The velocity vanishes at $t = 4$ s, where the particle reverses. At $t = 5$ s both are negative, so it is speeding up again, in the negative direction. A car that accelerates to a top speed, brakes to a stop and then backs up moves like this.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 3.6*

^ex-a2-2-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Acceleration is the second derivative of position ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^def-28-1|451 Def. §28.1]]). The rule "$a = 0$ at an extremum of $v$" is the Interior Extremum Theorem ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-1|451 Thm. §29.1]]).
> - The sign rule of [[§A2.2 Acceleration#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]] uses the chain rule ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]). Its three-dimensional form, $d\lvert\vec v\rvert/dt = \hat v\cdot\vec a$, is the tangential acceleration of [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^def-a2-7-3|Def. §A2.7.3]].
