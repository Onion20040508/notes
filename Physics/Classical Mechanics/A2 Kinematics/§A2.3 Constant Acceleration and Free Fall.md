When the acceleration ([[§A2.2 Acceleration|§A2.2]]) is constant, position, velocity, acceleration and time are tied together by four simple equations, and most one-dimensional problems reduce to choosing the right one. The most important case is free fall near Earth's surface, where every object, whatever its mass, has the same constant acceleration.

## The kinematic equations

> [!remark] Remark: Notation
> Start the clock at the beginning of the motion considered, $t_0 = 0$. A subscript 0 marks initial values ($x_0$, $v_0$); final values carry no subscript ($x$, $v$ at time $t$). Then $\Delta t = t$, $\Delta x = x - x_0$ and $\Delta v = v - v_0$. With the acceleration constant, average and instantaneous accelerations coincide and are written $a$.

^rem-a2-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.3.1: Kinematic Equations for Constant Acceleration
> If the acceleration $a$ is constant, then
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> v &= v_0 + at, \\
> x &= x_0 + \bar v\,t, \qquad \bar v = \tfrac12(v_0 + v), \\
> x &= x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2, \\
> v^2 &= v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.4, eqs. (3.10)–(3.14)*

^thm-a2-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Constant $a$ equals the average acceleration over $[0, t]$, so $a = (v - v_0)/t$, which is the first equation. The velocity is therefore a linear function of time, and the average of a linear function over an interval is the mean of its end values: $\bar v = \tfrac12(v_0 + v)$. Geometrically, the area under the straight $v$–$t$ line is a trapezoid of width $t$ and mean height $\tfrac12(v_0 + v)$; [[§A2.4 Velocity and Displacement from Acceleration#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]] shows that this area is the displacement. The definition of average velocity gives $x = x_0 + \bar v t$. Substituting $v = v_0 + at$ gives $\bar v = v_0 + \tfrac12 at$, hence the third equation. Finally, put $t = (v - v_0)/a$ into $x - x_0 = \tfrac12(v_0 + v)\,t$ to get $x - x_0 = (v^2 - v_0^2)/(2a)$, the fourth.

^der-a2-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A2.1 Position, Displacement, Velocity#^def-a2-1-3|Def. §A2.1.3]], [[§A2.2 Acceleration#^def-a2-2-1|Def. §A2.2.1]], [[§A2.4 Velocity and Displacement from Acceleration#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Choosing the equation
> The equations link five quantities, $\Delta x$, $v_0$, $v$, $a$, $t$, and each omits one of them: $v = v_0 + at$ has no $\Delta x$; $x = x_0 + \bar v t$ has no $a$; $x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12at^2$ has no $v$; $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x$ has no $t$. Pick the one that leaves out the quantity you neither know nor want. Only two of the equations are independent, so two unknowns need two equations solved together. The equations also carry insight:
> - Starting from rest, $\Delta x \propto t^2$: a dragster covers only a quarter of its distance in the first half of the time.
> - At fixed braking, the stopping distance $v_0^2/(2\lvert a\rvert)$ grows with the *square* of the speed. Twice as fast takes four times as far to stop.

^rem-a2-3-2

> [!caution] Caution: Only for constant acceleration
> These equations hold only while $a$ is constant. A motion with stages (accelerate, coast, brake) must be split into pieces, with the final values of one stage as the initial values of the next. When the equation is quadratic in $t$, it has two roots; check what each one means and discard the one outside the motion (for example, a negative time).

^cau-a2-3-1

> [!example] Example §A2.3.1: Stopping Distance
> A car moving at $v_0 = 30.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ brakes at $7.00\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ on dry concrete or $5.00\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ on wet. Take the direction of motion as positive, so $a < 0$ and the final velocity is $v = 0$. The time is not needed, so use $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x$:
>
> $$
> \Delta x = \frac{v^2 - v_0^2}{2a} = \frac{0 - (30.0\ \mathrm{m/s})^2}{2(-7.00\ \mathrm{m/s^2})} = 64.3\ \mathrm m \ \text{(dry)}, \qquad 90.0\ \mathrm m \ \text{(wet)}.
> $$
>
> During a reaction time of $0.500$ s before braking, the car keeps its speed and covers $(30.0\ \mathrm{m/s})(0.500\ \mathrm s) = 15.0\ \mathrm m$. The totals from the moment the light turns red are $79.3$ m and $105$ m. On dry concrete the reaction time adds almost a quarter to the braking distance.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 3.10*

^ex-a2-3-1

## Free fall

> [!law] Law §A2.3.2: Universality of Free Fall
> If air resistance and friction are negligible, all objects at a given location fall with the same constant acceleration, independent of their mass. It points toward the center of Earth, which defines "down", and its magnitude is the **acceleration due to gravity**, $g$:
>
> $$
> g \approx 9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \quad (\text{from } 9.78 \text{ to } 9.83\ \mathrm{m/s^2} \text{ with latitude, altitude and local geology}),
> $$
>
> rounded to $9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ in these notes.
>
> *Valid when: air resistance is negligible (dense, compact objects, moderate speeds); near a planet's surface, over heights small compared with its radius. A tennis ball reaches the ground after a baseball dropped with it; on the airless Moon, a hammer and a feather land together.*
> *Source: UP1 §3.5*

^law-a2-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Why this is surprising, and where it comes from
> Everyday experience, dominated by air resistance, suggests that heavy things fall faster, and this was believed until Galileo. The law is an experimental fact at this point. Dynamics explains it: weight is proportional to mass ([[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]), so the mass cancels from Newton's second law, and gravitation fixes the value of $g$ ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]]).

^rem-a2-3-3

> [!model] Model §A2.3.3: Free Fall near Earth's Surface
> An object is in **free fall** when gravity is the only influence on it: from the instant it leaves the hand or the carrier until it touches something. Near Earth's surface it is treated as a point with the constant acceleration
>
> $$
> \vec a = \vec g, \qquad \lvert\vec g\rvert = g, \quad \vec g \text{ pointing straight down,}
> $$
>
> the same while rising, at the top and while falling, whatever its mass ([[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^law-a2-3-2|Law §A2.3.2]]). For vertical motion, with $y$ measured **upward** ($a = -g$), the equations of [[§A2.3 Constant Acceleration and Free Fall#^thm-a2-3-1|Theorem §A2.3.1]] become
>
> $$
> v = v_0 - gt, \qquad y = y_0 + v_0 t - \tfrac12 g t^2, \qquad v^2 = v_0^2 - 2g(y - y_0).
> $$
>
> With a horizontal velocity as well, the same model gives projectile motion ([[§A2.6 Projectile Motion#^mod-a2-6-1|Model §A2.6.1]]).
>
> *Assumptions:* no air resistance or friction; $\vec g$ constant in magnitude and direction (heights and distances $\ll$ Earth's radius, the uniform-field model of [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^mod-a8-1-3|Model §A8.1.3]]); effects of Earth's rotation ignored. *Fails* for light or fast objects in air, where drag matters ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed|§A3.10]]), and over altitude changes that are not small compared with Earth's radius, where $g$ decreases ([[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-2|Theorem §A8.1.2]]). In terms of forces, free fall means that the net force is the weight alone ([[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^def-a3-4-2|Def. §A3.4.2]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 §3.5, eqs. (3.15)–(3.17)*

^mod-a2-3-3

> [!caution] Caution: The sign of g
> $g = 9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ is a *magnitude* and is always positive. The acceleration is $a = -g$ if up is positive and $a = +g$ if down is positive; choose one convention per problem and keep it. The acceleration stays $g$ downward at the top of the path, where $v = 0$.

^cau-a2-3-2

> [!example] Example §A2.3.2: A Sandbag Dropped from a Rising Balloon
> A hot-air balloon rises at constant speed $U$. At height $H$ a sandbag is released at rest *relative to the balloon*. Take $y$ up from the ground and $t = 0$ at release. The sandbag is in free fall with $v_0 = +U$, not zero:
>
> $$
> y_s = H + Ut - \tfrac12 g t^2, \qquad v_s = U - gt, \qquad y_b = H + Ut \ \text{(balloon)}.
> $$
>
> - *Highest point:* $v_s = 0$ at $t_1 = U/g$, so $y_{\max} = H + U^2/(2g)$. At that moment the balloon is at $H + U^2/g$, a further $U^2/(2g)$ above the bag.
> - *Landing:* $0 = H + Ut - \tfrac12 gt^2$ gives $t = \bigl(U + \sqrt{U^2 + 2gH}\bigr)/g$. The other root is negative and lies before the release.
> - *Checks:* for $U = 0$ this is $\sqrt{2H/g}$, a simple drop; for $H = 0$ it is $2U/g$, up and back down.
>
> On a $v$–$t$ graph the balloon is a horizontal line at $U$, and the sandbag is a straight line of slope $-g$ starting from the same point.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, Exam 1, F21*

## Two-body pursuit

> [!remark] Remark: Coupled motions
> In a **two-body pursuit problem** the unknown depends on the motion of both objects. Write the position of each as a function of time, in the same frame and with the same clock. Then impose the coupling condition, usually "same position at the same time", and solve the resulting equation. A cheetah starting from rest with $a = 4\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ as a gazelle passes at a steady $10\ \mathrm{m/s}$ catches it when $\tfrac12 at^2 = vt$, at $t = 2v/a = 5$ s, after $50$ m.

^rem-a2-3-4

> [!example] Example §A2.3.3: Catching the Bus
> A student runs at constant speed $v_0$ toward a bus at a stop. When she is a distance $D$ behind it, the bus pulls away from rest with constant acceleration $a$. Put the origin at her position at that moment:
>
> $$
> x_S(t) = v_0 t, \qquad x_B(t) = D + \tfrac12 a t^2 .
> $$
>
> She reaches the bus when $x_S = x_B$, that is $\tfrac12 a t^2 - v_0 t + D = 0$:
>
> $$
> t = \frac{v_0 \pm \sqrt{v_0^2 - 2aD}}{a} .
> $$
>
> For $D = 4$ m, $v_0 = 5\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $a = 2\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, $t = (5 \pm 3)/2$ s, so $t = 1$ s or $4$ s. She catches the bus after $1$ s, at $x = 5$ m. If she ran on, the bus would pass her again at $4$ s. If $v_0^2 < 2aD$ there is no real root and she never catches it. Checks: $D \to 0$ gives $t \to 0$, and $a \to 0$ gives $t \to D/v_0$ from the smaller root.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 3*
