Potential energy ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy|§A4.4]]) needs a force whose work does not depend on the path. This section names such forces, gives equivalent ways to recognise them, and shows how to get the force back from its potential energy. Friction and drag fail the test: the energy they take cannot be recovered later.

## Path independence

> [!definition] Definition §A4.5.1: Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces
> A force is **conservative** if the work it does between any two points is the same for every path joining them:
>
> $$
> \int_{AB,\ \text{path 1}} \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_{AB,\ \text{path 2}} \vec F \cdot d\vec r .
> $$
>
> Otherwise it is **non-conservative**. Dissipative forces such as kinetic friction and air resistance are non-conservative.
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.2, eq. (8.8)*

^def-a4-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.1: Closed-Path Criterion
> A force is conservative if and only if the work it does around every closed path is zero:
>
> $$
> \oint \vec F \cdot d\vec r = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.2, eq. (8.9)*

^thm-a4-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Choose two points $A$, $B$ on a closed path; it splits into path 1 from $A$ to $B$ and path 2 from $B$ back to $A$. Running a path backwards reverses every $d\vec r$ and so changes the sign of the work. Hence
>
> $$
> \oint \vec F\cdot d\vec r = \int_{AB,\ 1} \vec F\cdot d\vec r - \int_{AB,\ 2} \vec F\cdot d\vec r ,
> $$
>
> which vanishes for every closed path exactly when the work from $A$ to $B$ is the same along every pair of paths.

^der-a4-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]], [[§A4.1 Work#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Which forces are conservative
> Uniform gravity and the Hooke's-law spring are conservative: their work depends only on the endpoints ([[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-2|Theorem §A4.1.2]], [[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]]). Kinetic friction is not: its work is proportional to the path length ([[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]]). Every potential energy comes with a conservative force; non-conservative forces have no potential energy, because the energy they remove is not returned when the motion is reversed.

^rem-a4-5-1

## Force from potential energy

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.2: Force from Potential Energy
> The component of a conservative force in any direction $\vec l$ is minus the rate of change of its potential energy in that direction, $F_l = -dU/dl$. In Cartesian components
>
> $$
> \vec F = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\,\hat\imath - \frac{\partial U}{\partial y}\,\hat\jmath - \frac{\partial U}{\partial z}\,\hat k ,
> $$
>
> minus the gradient of $U$. In one dimension, $F_x = -dU/dx$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.2, eq. (8.11)*

^thm-a4-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]], over an infinitesimal displacement $d\vec l$ the potential energy changes by $dU = -\vec F \cdot d\vec l = -F_l\,dl$, where $F_l$ is the component of $\vec F$ along $d\vec l$. Dividing by $dl$ gives $F_l = -dU/dl$. Taking $d\vec l$ along $\hat\imath$, $\hat\jmath$, $\hat k$ in turn (the other coordinates held fixed) gives the three partial derivatives.

^der-a4-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]], [[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|Partial Derivatives (452 Def. §4.1)]], [[§7 Directional Derivatives#^rem-7-2|Gradient (452 §7)]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.3: Test for a Conservative Force in Two Dimensions
> If $\vec F = F_x\,\hat\imath + F_y\,\hat\jmath$ is conservative, with a potential energy whose second partial derivatives are continuous, then
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} .
> $$
>
> A force that violates this condition anywhere is non-conservative.
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.2, eq. (8.10)*

^thm-a4-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]], $F_x = -\partial U/\partial x$ and $F_y = -\partial U/\partial y$. Then $\partial F_x/\partial y = -\partial^2 U/\partial y\,\partial x$ and $\partial F_y/\partial x = -\partial^2 U/\partial x\,\partial y$, which are equal because mixed partial derivatives commute.

^der-a4-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]], [[§5 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-5-1|Schwarz–Clairaut (452 Thm. §5.1)]]

> [!remark] Remark: When the test is also sufficient
> The text uses the condition both ways, as the criterion for $F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy$ to be an exact differential. The converse direction (condition $\Rightarrow$ conservative) holds for a force that is smooth on the whole plane, or on any region without holes; for forces defined around a hole it can fail. In three dimensions there are three such conditions, one per pair of coordinates.

^rem-a4-5-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.5.1: Same Endpoints, Different Work
> Take the force $\vec F = (5\ \mathrm{N/m})\,y\,\hat\imath + (10\ \mathrm{N/m})\,x\,\hat\jmath$ of [[§A4.1 Work#^ex-a4-1-2|Example §A4.1.2]] from $(0,0)$ to $(2\ \mathrm m, 2\ \mathrm m)$ along three paths:
> - straight line $y = x$: $dW = (5x + 10x)\,dx$, $W = \tfrac{15}{2}(2)^2 = 30$ J;
> - parabola $y = x^2/(2\ \mathrm m)$: $W = 33.3$ J (Example §A4.1.2);
> - cubic $y = x^3/(4\ \mathrm{m^2})$: $dy = \tfrac34 x^2\,dx$, $dW = (1.25 + 7.5)\,x^3\,dx$, $W = 8.75\cdot\tfrac{(2)^4}{4} = 35$ J (units N/m³ · m⁴).
>
> The work depends on the path, so the force is non-conservative, as [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]] confirms at once: $\partial F_x/\partial y = 5\ \mathrm{N/m} \ne \partial F_y/\partial x = 10\ \mathrm{N/m}$. Changing $F_y$ to $(5\ \mathrm{N/m})\,x$ would make it conservative, with $U = -(5\ \mathrm{N/m})\,xy$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 7.4, §8.2*

^ex-a4-5-1

> [!example] Example §A4.5.2: Is Friction Conservative?
> A sled of mass $m$ on snow (kinetic friction coefficient $\mu_k$) is pulled slowly by a horizontal rope around a large circle of radius $R$ and back to its starting point. The normal force is $mg$, friction has constant magnitude $\mu_k mg$ and always opposes the motion, so by [[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-3|Theorem §A4.1.3]]
>
> $$
> W_{\text{fr}} = -\mu_k m g\,(2\pi R) \ne 0
> $$
>
> around a closed path. By [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-1|Theorem §A4.5.1]] friction is not conservative; the work grows with every lap.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 16*

^ex-a4-5-2

> [!example] Example §A4.5.3: Testing a Force and Finding Its Potential Energy
> Is $\vec F = a\bigl[(y^2/x)\,\hat\imath + 2y\ln(x/b)\,\hat\jmath\bigr]$, for $x > 0$ and constants $a$, $b$, conservative? The test gives
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y} = \frac{2ay}{x} = \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} ,
> $$
>
> so it passes. Find $U$ from $F_x = -\partial U/\partial x$: $U = -a\,y^2 \ln(x/b) + g(y)$. Then $-\partial U/\partial y = 2ay\ln(x/b) - g'(y)$ must equal $F_y$, so $g' = 0$ and $U = -a\,y^2\ln(x/b) + \text{const}$. Finding $U$ explicitly settles the question for any region.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 8.5*

^ex-a4-5-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Conservative forces are the gradient fields of [[§11 The Three Differential Operators꞉ Gradient, Curl, Divergence#^def-11-1|452 Def. §11.1]] (Multivariable Analysis), with potential $-U$; the test is "curl-free", and its converse on regions without holes is the [[Poincaré Lemma]].
> - Green's theorem ([[§16 Line Integrals and Green's Theorem#^thm-16-1|452 Thm. §16.1]]) turns the closed-path work into $\iint (\partial F_y/\partial x - \partial F_x/\partial y)\,dA$. In Example §A4.5.1 the integrand is $5\ \mathrm{N/m}$ and the region between the line and the parabola has area $\tfrac23\ \mathrm{m^2}$, giving $33.3 - 30 = 3.3$ J.
