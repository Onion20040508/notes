The work–energy theorem ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem|§A4.2]]) counts the work of every force. Splitting the forces into conservative ones, whose work is a drop in potential energy ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy|§A4.4]]), and the rest turns it into a statement about the sum $K + U$: it changes only through non-conservative work. Beyond mechanics, energy that seems lost reappears in other forms, and the general law of conservation of energy takes over.

## Mechanical energy

> [!definition] Definition §A4.6.1: Mechanical Energy
> The **mechanical energy** of a particle (or system) is the sum of its kinetic and potential energies, $E = K + U$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.3*

^def-a4-6-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.6.1: Work–Energy Theorem with Potential Energy
> The work done on a particle by the non-conservative forces equals the change of its mechanical energy:
>
> $$
> W_{\text{nc},AB} = \Delta(K + U)_{AB} = \Delta E_{AB} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.3, eq. (8.12)*

^thm-a4-6-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Split the net work into the work of the conservative forces and that of the rest: $W_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{c}} + W_{\text{nc}}$. By [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]], $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K$; by [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]], $W_{\text{c}} = -\Delta U$. Hence $\Delta K = -\Delta U + W_{\text{nc}}$, that is, $W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta K + \Delta U$.

^der-a4-6-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]], [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]], [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^def-a4-6-1|Def. §A4.6.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.6.2: Conservation of Mechanical Energy
> If the non-conservative forces on a particle do no work, its mechanical energy is constant:
>
> $$
> K_A + U_A = K_B + U_B .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.3, eq. (8.13)*

^thm-a4-6-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $W_{\text{nc},AB} = 0$ in [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-1|Theorem §A4.6.1]].

^der-a4-6-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-1|Theorem §A4.6.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Forces that do no work
> The condition is that non-conservative forces do no **work**, not that they are absent. A normal force from a fixed surface and the tension in a pendulum string are perpendicular to the motion and do no work. For a pendulum of length $L$ released from rest at angle $\theta$, $mgL(1 - \cos\theta) = \tfrac12 m v^2$ at the bottom: $v = 1.62$ m/s for $L = 1.0$ m and $\theta = 30^\circ$, found without the pendulum's equation of motion.

^rem-a4-6-1

> [!remark] Remark: Using energy conservation
> 1. Choose the system (often several bodies) and list the forces on it.
> 2. For each force that does work, decide whether it is conservative. If a non-conservative force does work, use [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-1|Theorem §A4.6.1]] instead of [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]].
> 3. Choose a reference point for each potential energy (they need not coincide) and write $K + U$ at the points of interest.
>
> The method gives speeds at chosen points without the time dependence; [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-3|Theorem §A4.7.3]] recovers even that in one dimension. For several bodies, include the kinetic and potential energies of all of them and the non-conservative work on all of them.

^rem-a4-6-2

> [!caution] Caution: Dissipated energy
> $W_{\text{nc}}$ is usually negative: friction and drag lower $E$. A 15 kg panel falling from rest from 1.0 km and hitting the ground at 45 m/s has $\Delta E = \tfrac12 (15)(45)^2\ \mathrm J - (15)(9.8)(1000)\ \mathrm J = -132$ kJ: almost all of its initial 147 kJ went into the air, found without knowing the drag force. Mechanical energy is also *not* conserved when bodies stick together in a collision ([[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^thm-a5-3-1|Theorem §A5.3.1]]).

^cau-a4-6-1

## Energy beyond mechanics

> [!principle] Principle §A4.6.3: Conservation of Energy
> The total energy of an isolated system is constant. Energy can be transferred between systems and converted from one form into another (kinetic, potential, thermal, chemical, electrical, radiant, nuclear), but it is never created or destroyed.
>
> In mechanics, Theorem §A4.6.1 is derived from Newton's laws; this general law is not. It cannot be proved from more basic principles, no exception has ever been found, and whenever measurements seemed to violate it a new form of energy was recognised. Here it is the bookkeeping that explains where dissipated mechanical energy goes.
>
> *Domain: all of physics as far as tested. In relativity the definition of energy is adjusted (rest energy; [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^def-a3-2-2|REL Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A3.2 Relativistic Energy#^pr-a3-2-3|REL Principle §A3.2.3]]).*
> *Source: UP1 §8.3, §8.5*

^pr-a4-6-3

> [!remark] Remark: Forms and sources of energy
> - **Thermal** energy is the kinetic energy of the random motion of atoms and molecules; friction and drag convert mechanical energy into it. **Chemical** energy (fuels, food) is potential energy of molecular structure, **nuclear** energy that of nuclear structure; **radiant** energy is carried by electromagnetic waves.
> - Fossil fuels supplied about 81% of world energy use in 2012, nuclear about 3%, renewable sources (hydro, wind, solar, geothermal, biomass) most of the rest.
> - The *law* of conservation of energy differs from *conserving* energy (using less). Energy is never used up; it is degraded, mostly into waste heat, from which little useful work can be recovered (Thermal and Statistical Physics, planned).

^rem-a4-6-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.6.1: Same Speed at the Ground
> From a platform at height $h$ you (a) drop a ball, (b) throw it straight up at speed $v_i$, (c) throw it straight down at $v_i$. Ignoring air resistance, only gravity does work and $\tfrac12 m v_f^2 = \tfrac12 m v_i^2 + mgh$ for each throw. So (b) and (c) land with the same speed $\sqrt{v_i^2 + 2gh}$, and (a) with the smaller $\sqrt{2gh}$. The same holds for balls fired at the same speed horizontally or at $45^\circ$: kinetic energy is a scalar, and the direction of launch changes the flight time and landing point, not the landing speed.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 14; Exam 2, F21*

^ex-a4-6-1

> [!example] Example §A4.6.2: Spring Launcher
> A 2.00 kg block is pushed against a spring ($k = 400$ N/m), compressing it by 0.220 m, and released; it slides along a frictionless floor and up a frictionless $37^\circ$ incline. The spring can push but not pull the block.
> - Only the spring and gravity do work; the normal forces are perpendicular to the motion. Mechanical energy is conserved.
> - Leaving the spring: $\tfrac12 k x^2 = \tfrac12 (400)(0.220)^2\ \mathrm J = 9.68\ \mathrm J = \tfrac12 m v^2$, so $v = 3.11$ m/s.
> - Highest point (at rest): $mgh = 9.68$ J, so $h = 9.68/(2.00 \times 9.8)\ \mathrm m = 0.494$ m. The slope of the incline does not matter.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 15–16*

^ex-a4-6-2

> [!example] Example §A4.6.3: Incline, Spring and Friction
> A package of mass $M$ is released from rest on a $53^\circ$ incline ($\sin 53^\circ = 0.8$, $\cos 53^\circ = 0.6$), a distance $D$ above the end of a spring of constant $K$; the coefficient of sliding friction is $\mu$.
>
> *Maximum compression $x$.* From release to rest at maximum compression the package slides $D + x$, drops $0.8(D + x)$ and feels friction $\mu N = 0.6\,\mu Mg$. Apply [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-1|Theorem §A4.6.1]]: the kinetic energy is zero at both ends (here $K$ denotes the spring constant), and $U = 0$ at the start, so
>
> $$
> -0.6\,\mu Mg\,(D + x) = \tfrac12 K x^2 - 0.8\,Mg\,(D + x)
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> \tfrac12 K x^2 = \beta\,(D + x), \qquad \beta = (0.8 - 0.6\mu)\,Mg ,
> $$
>
> whose positive root is $x = \bigl[\beta + \sqrt{\beta^2 + 2K\beta D}\,\bigr]/K$.
>
> *Rebound.* Going up, friction acts down the slope. If the package leaves the spring and stops a distance $s$ up the incline from the point of maximum compression, $\tfrac12 K x^2 = (0.8 + 0.6\mu)Mg\,s$, so
>
> $$
> s = \frac{0.8 - 0.6\mu}{0.8 + 0.6\mu}\,(D + x), \qquad \text{and it stops } (D + x) - s = \frac{1.2\,\mu}{0.8 + 0.6\mu}\,(D + x) \text{ below its starting point}.
> $$
>
> For $\mu = 0$ it returns exactly to the start, as it must.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, Exam 2, F21*

^ex-a4-6-3
