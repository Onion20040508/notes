Newton's second law relates force and acceleration at each instant, and following a motion with it means solving a differential equation, which is often hard or impossible in closed form. Work is the first of the quantities that summarise what a force does *along a path* instead of at an instant. Together with kinetic energy ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem|§A4.2]]) it answers many questions about speeds without solving for the motion.

## Definition

> [!definition] Definition §A4.1.1: Work Done by a Force
> When a force $\vec F$ acts on a particle that undergoes an infinitesimal displacement $d\vec r$, the **work** it does is
>
> $$
> dW = \vec F \cdot d\vec r = |\vec F|\,|d\vec r| \cos\theta = F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy + F_z\,dz ,
> $$
>
> with $\theta$ the angle between $\vec F$ and $d\vec r$. Along a path from $A$ to $B$ the work is the line integral
>
> $$
> W_{AB} = \int_{\text{path } AB} \vec F \cdot d\vec r .
> $$
>
> Work is a scalar. Its SI unit is the **joule**, $1\ \mathrm J = 1\ \mathrm{N\cdot m}$. In general it depends on the path, not only on $A$ and $B$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §7.1, eqs. (7.1)–(7.2)*

^def-a4-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: The sign of work
> - $W > 0$ when the force has a component along the displacement ($0 \le \theta < 90^\circ$), $W = 0$ when it is perpendicular ($\theta = 90^\circ$), $W < 0$ when it has a component against the displacement. A given force does the most work when it is along the displacement.
> - It does not matter whether one takes the component of the force along the displacement or the component of the displacement along the force: the dot product is symmetric.
> - The work done *against* a force is the negative of the work done *by* it.
> - A force can act without doing work: holding a briefcase at rest (no displacement), or carrying it horizontally at constant speed (force perpendicular to the displacement).

^rem-a4-1-1

## Constant forces and gravity

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.1: Work of a Constant Force
> If $\vec F$ is constant in magnitude and direction, its work from $A$ to $B$ depends only on the endpoints:
>
> $$
> W_{AB} = \vec F \cdot (\vec r_B - \vec r_A) = F d \cos\theta ,
> $$
>
> where $d = |\vec r_B - \vec r_A|$ and $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec F$ and the net displacement.
>
> *Source: UP1 §7.1*

^thm-a4-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A constant vector comes out of the integral: $W_{AB} = \vec F \cdot \int_A^B d\vec r = \vec F \cdot (\vec r_B - \vec r_A)$, since the infinitesimal displacements along any path add up to the net displacement. In components, $\int (F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy + F_z\,dz) = F_x(x_B - x_A) + F_y(y_B - y_A) + F_z(z_B - z_A)$.

^der-a4-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 Work#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-1|Dot Product (LADR Def. 6.1)]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.2: Work Done by Gravity Near Earth
> With the $y$-axis vertically up, the work done by the weight of a body of mass $m$ near Earth's surface, along **any** path from $A$ to $B$, is
>
> $$
> W_{\text{grav},AB} = -mg\,(y_B - y_A) .
> $$
>
> Gravity does negative work on a body that rises and positive work on one that descends.
>
> *Source: UP1 §7.1, eq. (7.4)*

^thm-a4-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Near the surface the weight $m\vec g = -mg\,\hat\jmath$ is constant ([[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]; uniform field, [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^mod-a8-1-3|Model §A8.1.3]]). By [[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]], $W = -mg\,\hat\jmath \cdot (\vec r_B - \vec r_A) = -mg\,(y_B - y_A)$.

^der-a4-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]], [[§A3.4 Mass and Weight#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]], [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^mod-a8-1-3|Model §A8.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Gravity gives back what it takes
> Lowering a 20 N book 1 m from a shelf, carrying it 3 m horizontally to a table and moving it in a straight line back to its place on the shelf, gravity does $+20$ J, $0$ J and $-20$ J: zero in total around the closed path. Forces with this property are **conservative** ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]); they are the ones that have a potential energy ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]]).

^rem-a4-1-2

## Contact forces

> [!remark] Remark: Normal force and static friction
> - For a body sliding on a fixed surface, $d\vec r$ is tangent to the surface and the normal force $\vec N$ is perpendicular to it, so $dW_N = \vec N \cdot d\vec r = 0$: the normal force does no work. (A body with internal structure can have work done on it by a normal force that deforms it; compare [[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^ex-a5-3-1|Example §A5.3.1]].)
> - Static friction does no work in the frame of the two surfaces, since they do not slip. In another frame it can: static friction from an accelerating sled moves a rider along with it and does positive work on the rider. Likewise, kinetic friction from a rug pulled out from under an object can point along the object's displacement relative to the floor and do positive work. Each case has to be analysed.

^rem-a4-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.3: Work Done by Kinetic Friction
> If kinetic friction from a fixed surface has constant magnitude $f_k$ and always opposes the motion along the surface, then
>
> $$
> W_{\text{fr}} = -f_k\,\ell_{AB} ,
> $$
>
> where $\ell_{AB}$ is the **length of the path** travelled, not the net displacement.
>
> *Source: UP1 §7.1, eq. (7.3)*

^thm-a4-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At each point $\vec f_k = -f_k\,d\vec r/|d\vec r|$, so $\vec f_k \cdot d\vec r = -f_k\,|d\vec r|$. Summing along the path, $W_{\text{fr}} = -f_k \int_A^B |d\vec r| = -f_k\,\ell_{AB}$.

^der-a4-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 Work#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A3.8 Friction#^law-a3-8-1|Law §A3.8.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Path length, not displacement
> Push a couch (normal force 1 kN, $\mu_k = 0.6$) 3 m along a wall, then 1 m away from it, then straight back to the start ($\sqrt{10}\ \mathrm m = 3.16$ m). The displacement is zero, yet the work done against friction is $(0.6)(1\ \mathrm{kN})(3 + 1 + 3.16)\ \mathrm m = 4.3$ kJ. Unlike gravity, friction does not give the work back.

^cau-a4-1-1

## Springs

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.4: Work Done by a Spring
> Let a spring obey Hooke's law $F_x = -kx$, with $x$ the displacement from its unstretched length. When $x$ changes from $x_A$ to $x_B$, the spring does work
>
> $$
> W_{\text{spring},AB} = -\tfrac12 k\,(x_B^2 - x_A^2) ,
> $$
>
> which depends only on the endpoints (the spring may stretch and compress back and forth in between).
>
> *Source: UP1 §7.1, eq. (7.5)*

^thm-a4-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The force has only an $x$-component, so $W_{AB} = \int_{x_A}^{x_B} (-kx)\,dx = -\tfrac12 k x^2 \Big|_{x_A}^{x_B} = -\tfrac12 k\,(x_B^2 - x_A^2)$.

^der-a4-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 Work#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A3.6 Common Forces#^law-a3-6-3|Law §A3.6.3]] (Hooke's law), [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|FTC I (451 Thm. §34.1)]]

> [!remark] Remark: Work as an area
> In one dimension $W = \int F_x\,dx$ is the signed area under the graph of $F_x$ against $x$: area above the axis counts positive, area below negative. For a spring the regions are triangles and trapezoids, whose "areas" carry units of N·m.

^rem-a4-1-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.1.1: Who Does Positive Work?
> An elevator is lowered at constant speed by a steel cable from an electric motor. The cable pulls up on the elevator, which moves down: the cable does **negative** work on the elevator. By the third law the elevator pulls down on the end of the cable, which also moves down: the elevator does **positive** work on the cable. Gravity does positive work on the elevator, equal in size to the cable's negative work, since at constant speed the net work is zero ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]]). The motor, which lets cable out against its pull, does negative work: it acts as a brake.
>
> A third-law pair is equal and opposite, but its works are opposite only because here the two points of application move together.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 13*

^ex-a4-1-1

> [!example] Example §A4.1.2: Variable Force on a Curved Path
> A particle moves along the parabola $y = (0.5\ \mathrm m^{-1})x^2$ from $A = (0, 0)$ to $B = (2\ \mathrm m, 2\ \mathrm m)$ under $\vec F = (5\ \mathrm{N/m})\,y\,\hat\imath + (10\ \mathrm{N/m})\,x\,\hat\jmath$. Use the path to write everything in terms of $x$: $dy = (1\ \mathrm m^{-1})\,x\,dx$, so
>
> $$
> dW = F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy = (5)(0.5)\,x^2\,dx + (10)(1)\,x^2\,dx = (12.5\ \mathrm{N/m^2})\,x^2\,dx ,
> \qquad
> W = (12.5\ \mathrm{N/m^2})\,\frac{(2\ \mathrm m)^3}{3} = 33.3\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> Parametrising by $y$ instead gives the same 33.3 J. Along other paths between the same points this force does different work ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^ex-a4-5-1|Example §A4.5.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 7.4*

^ex-a4-1-2

> [!example] Example §A4.1.3: Stretching a Spring
> Stretching a spring 6 cm from its unstretched length takes 0.54 J. The work *required* is the work done against the spring, $+\tfrac12 k(x_B^2 - x_A^2)$, so $k = 2(0.54\ \mathrm J)/(0.06\ \mathrm m)^2 = 300\ \mathrm{N/m}$. A further 6 cm takes
>
> $$
> W = \tfrac12 (300\ \mathrm{N/m})\bigl[(0.12\ \mathrm m)^2 - (0.06\ \mathrm m)^2\bigr] = 1.62\ \mathrm J ,
> $$
>
> three times as much as the first 6 cm: the work grows with the square of the stretch, so stretching from 0 to 12 cm costs four times the first step.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 7.5*

^ex-a4-1-3
