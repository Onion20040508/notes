[[§A4.1 Work|§A4.1]] defined the work a force does along a path. What does that work do to the motion? Integrating Newton's second law along the path answers it: the net work equals the change of a single number built from mass and speed, the kinetic energy. The resulting work–energy theorem gives speeds at different points of a trajectory without solving for the trajectory.

## Kinetic energy

> [!definition] Definition §A4.2.1: Kinetic Energy
> The **kinetic energy** of a particle of mass $m$ moving with speed $v$ (much less than the speed of light) is
>
> $$
> K = \tfrac12 m v^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m},
> $$
>
> where $p = mv$ is the magnitude of its momentum. The kinetic energy of a system of particles is the sum $K = \sum_j \tfrac12 m_j v_j^2$. Its unit is $\mathrm{kg\cdot m^2/s^2} = \mathrm J$, the same as that of work.
>
> *Source: UP1 §7.2, eqs. (7.6)–(7.7)*

^def-a4-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Properties of kinetic energy
> - It is a scalar and never negative; it depends on the speed, not on the direction of motion.
> - It depends on the frame, because velocity does. A 75.0 kg passenger walking at 1.50 m/s in a train moving at 15.0 m/s has $K = 84.4$ J relative to the car, $6.83$ kJ or $10.2$ kJ relative to the tracks (walking backward or forward), and $0$ in a frame moving with him.
> - Translational, rotational, vibrational and thermal (random molecular) kinetic energy are names for the same quantity in different kinds of motion.
> - At speeds comparable to that of light, special relativity replaces $\tfrac12 mv^2$ (Relativity, planned).

^rem-a4-2-1

## The theorem

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.1: Work–Energy Theorem
> The net work done on a particle between two points $A$ and $B$ of its path equals the change of its kinetic energy:
>
> $$
> W_{\text{net},AB} = \int_A^B \vec F_{\text{net}} \cdot d\vec r = K_B - K_A .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §7.3, eqs. (7.8)–(7.9)*

^thm-a4-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the second law, $\vec F_{\text{net}} = m\,d\vec v/dt$. Along the path $d\vec r = \vec v\,dt$, so
>
> $$
> dW_{\text{net}} = m\,\frac{d\vec v}{dt} \cdot \vec v\,dt = m\,\vec v \cdot d\vec v = m\,(v_x\,dv_x + v_y\,dv_y + v_z\,dv_z) = d\bigl(\tfrac12 m v^2\bigr),
> $$
>
> using $v^2 = \vec v \cdot \vec v = v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2$. Integrating from $A$ to $B$ gives $W_{\text{net},AB} = \tfrac12 m v_B^2 - \tfrac12 m v_A^2$.

^der-a4-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A4.1 Work#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]], [[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-1|Dot Product (LADR Def. 6.1)]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|FTC I (Theorem §34.1)]]

> [!caution] Caution: All the forces, and only those
> - The net work is the work of the net force, which for a particle equals the sum of the works of all the forces on it ($\vec F_{\text{net}} = \sum \vec F_i$ and the dot product distributes). Leave out a force, or include one that does not act on the particle, and the answer is wrong.
> - The theorem is a statement about a particle. For an extended or deformable body different forces act at points that move differently, and the bookkeeping needs more care ([[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^ex-a5-3-1|Example §A5.3.1]]).
> - Checks: constant speed means zero net work; a body that speeds up has positive net work; one that slows down, negative.

^cau-a4-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: What the theorem buys
> On a frictionless *curved* track the normal force changes from point to point, and Newton's law along the track is a differential equation that may be impossible to solve in closed form. But the normal force is perpendicular to the motion and does no work, so only gravity counts ([[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-2|Theorem §A4.1.2]]): $\tfrac12 m v_f^2 - \tfrac12 m v_i^2 = -mg(y_f - y_i)$ for **any** shape of track. Conversely, if the motion is known, the theorem gives information about the forces, through the product of force and distance.

^rem-a4-2-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.2.1: Pulling a Block up a Frictionless Incline
> A block of weight $w$ starts from rest on a frictionless incline of angle $\theta$; a force of constant magnitude $F$, parallel to the incline, pulls it a distance $L$ up the slope. Work of each force:
>
> $$
> W_F = FL, \qquad W_N = 0, \qquad W_{\text{grav}} = -w L \sin\theta \quad(\text{the block rises } L\sin\theta), \qquad W_{\text{net}} = (F - w\sin\theta)\,L .
> $$
>
> By the theorem $K_f = (F - w\sin\theta)L$, so $v_f = \sqrt{2gL\,(F - w\sin\theta)/w}$, using $m = w/g$. Newton's law gives the same: $a = (F - w\sin\theta)/m$ along the incline and $v_f^2 = 2aL$. The block moves up from rest only if $F > w\sin\theta$.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 13–14*

^ex-a4-2-1

> [!example] Example §A4.2.2: Loop-the-Loop
> A toy car starts from rest at height $y_1$ on a frictionless track that ends in a vertical loop of radius $R$. How high must it start to go all the way round? Only gravity does work, so at the top of the loop ($y_2 = 2R$)
>
> $$
> \tfrac12 m v_2^2 = mg\,(y_1 - 2R) .
> $$
>
> At the top both $\vec N$ and $m\vec g$ point down, toward the centre, and supply the centripetal force ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion|§A3.9]]): $N + mg = m v_2^2/R$. The car keeps contact if $N \ge 0$:
>
> $$
> N = \frac{m v_2^2}{R} - mg = \frac{2mg\,(y_1 - 2R)}{R} - mg \ge 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y_1 \ge \tfrac52 R .
> $$
>
> The normal force varies all around the loop, but it never does work, so the theorem sidesteps it.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 7.9; PHY 181, TBL 16–17*

^ex-a4-2-2

> [!example] Example §A4.2.3: Average Stopping Force
> A 2.60 g bullet leaves the muzzle at 335 m/s and stops after penetrating eight 0.75-inch pine boards, 15.2 cm in all. The wood's force does all the work: $W_{\text{net}} = -F_{\text{ave}}\,\Delta s = 0 - K_i$, so
>
> $$
> F_{\text{ave}} = \frac{\tfrac12 (2.60\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg})(335\ \mathrm{m/s})^2}{0.152\ \mathrm m} = 960\ \mathrm N .
> $$
>
> This is the force averaged over the *distance* travelled; impulse gives the average over the time ([[§A5.1 Momentum and Impulse|§A5.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 7.10*

^ex-a4-2-3
