In [[§A4.1 Work|§A4.1]] the work done by gravity near Earth and by a spring turned out to depend only on where the motion starts and ends. Such work can be booked in advance as the decrease of a function of position, the potential energy. A football rising against gravity loses kinetic energy and the football–Earth system gains potential energy; on the way down the potential energy turns back into kinetic energy.

## Definition

> [!definition] Definition §A4.4.1: Potential Energy
> Let $\vec F$ be a force whose work between two points does not depend on the path (a conservative force, [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces|§A4.5]]). The **potential energy difference** between $A$ and $B$ is minus the work done by $\vec F$:
>
> $$
> \Delta U_{AB} = U_B - U_A = -W_{AB} .
> $$
>
> Fixing a value $U(\vec r_0)$ at a reference point $\vec r_0$ defines the **potential energy function** $U(\vec r) = U(\vec r_0) - \int_{\vec r_0}^{\vec r} \vec F \cdot d\vec r$. Only differences of $U$ are physical; the reference point and its value are a convention, to be stated and kept throughout a problem.
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.1, eqs. (8.1)–(8.2)*

^def-a4-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Potential energy belongs to the system
> Potential energy is a property of the **interaction** between the bodies of a system, not of one body: the football–Earth system has gravitational potential energy, not the football. One speaks of "the ball's potential energy" only because the other partner is so massive (Earth) or so light (a massless spring) that its own motion can be neglected. With several kinds of interaction, the total potential energy is the sum of one term per kind (the works of the individual forces add).

^rem-a4-4-1

## Gravity and springs

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.1: Gravitational Potential Energy Near Earth
> With the $y$-axis vertically up, the potential energy of a body of mass $m$ in Earth's uniform gravity near the surface is
>
> $$
> U(y) = mgy + \text{const}.
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.1, eqs. (8.4)–(8.5)*

^thm-a4-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-2|Theorem §A4.1.2]] the work of gravity from $A$ to $B$ is $-mg(y_B - y_A)$ on every path, so by [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]] $\Delta U_{AB} = mg(y_B - y_A)$, the difference of $mgy$ at the endpoints.

^der-a4-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-2|Theorem §A4.1.2]], [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.2: Elastic Potential Energy
> A spring obeying Hooke's law, stretched or compressed by $x$ from its unstretched length, has potential energy
>
> $$
> U(x) = \tfrac12 k x^2 + \text{const}.
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.1, eqs. (8.6)–(8.7)*

^thm-a4-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]] the spring does work $-\tfrac12 k(x_B^2 - x_A^2)$ from $x_A$ to $x_B$, so $\Delta U = \tfrac12 k(x_B^2 - x_A^2)$, the difference of $\tfrac12 kx^2$.

^der-a4-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 Work#^thm-a4-1-4|Theorem §A4.1.4]], [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Choosing the zero
> The usual choices are $U = 0$ at the lowest point of the problem for gravity and at the unstretched length for a spring. Other choices change every value of $U$ by the same constant and no difference. A 75 kg hiker on a hill whose summit is 147 m above its base and 195 m above sea level has $U = 108$ kJ at the summit and $-35.3$ kJ at sea level if $U = 0$ at the base; with $U = 0$ at sea level the numbers change, the differences do not. A compressed spring stores as much energy as one stretched by the same amount, since $U \propto x^2$.

^rem-a4-4-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.4.1: Potential Energy from a Force
> A particle moves along the $x$-axis under $F = -ax^2$ with $a = 3\ \mathrm{N/m^2}$. From $x = 1$ m to $x = 2$ m,
>
> $$
> \Delta U = -W = -\int_{1\ \mathrm m}^{2\ \mathrm m} (-ax^2)\,dx = \frac{a}{3}\bigl(8 - 1\bigr)\ \mathrm{m^3} = 7\ \mathrm J .
> $$
>
> The function is $U(x) = \tfrac13 a x^3 + C$. If $U(0) = 0.5$ J, then $C = 0.5$ J, $U(1\ \mathrm m) = 1.5$ J and $U(2\ \mathrm m) = 8.5$ J; another choice of $C$ shifts both values and leaves the 7 J difference.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 8.1*

^ex-a4-4-1

> [!example] Example §A4.4.2: Raising a Ball
> You lift a ball at constant velocity from height $h$ to $H$. Take the **ball** as the system. Its kinetic energy is constant. Earth's gravity does work $-mg(H - h) < 0$ on it, and you do $+mg(H - h)$; the net work is zero, as it must be. Of the statements "the kinetic energy decreases", "Earth does negative work", "you do negative work", only the second is true.
>
> Take instead **ball + Earth** as the system: gravity is now internal and its work is replaced by the increase $\Delta U = mg(H - h)$ of potential energy, which equals the work you do. Either description is correct, but never both at once: counting the work of gravity *and* the change of $U$ counts gravity twice.
>
> *Source: PHY 181, TBL 14–15*

^ex-a4-4-2

> [!example] Example §A4.4.3: Vertical Mass–Spring System
> A block of weight $mg = 1.2$ N hangs from a spring with $k = 6.0$ N/m and is released from rest at the spring's unstretched length. Take $y$ up, with $y = 0$ and both potential energies zero at the release point $A$. Anticipating energy conservation ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy|§A4.6]]), $\tfrac12 mv^2 + mgy + \tfrac12 k y^2 = 0$ throughout.
> - Lowest point $C$ ($v = 0$, $y \ne 0$): $y_C = -2mg/k = -0.40$ m.
> - Halfway, $y_B = -0.20$ m: $U_B = (1.2\ \mathrm N)(-0.20\ \mathrm m) + \tfrac12 (6.0\ \mathrm{N/m})(0.20\ \mathrm m)^2 = -0.24\ \mathrm J + 0.12\ \mathrm J = -0.12\ \mathrm J$, so $K_B = 0.12$ J. With $m = 1.2\ \mathrm N/9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2} = 0.122$ kg, $v_B = \sqrt{2K_B/m} = 1.4$ m/s.
>
> $B$ is where the spring force $k|y_B| = 1.2$ N balances the weight: the minimum of the total $U$, where the block moves fastest ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability|§A4.7]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 8.4*

^ex-a4-4-3
