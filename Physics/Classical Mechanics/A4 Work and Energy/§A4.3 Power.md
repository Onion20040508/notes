Work and the work–energy theorem ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem|§A4.2]]) say nothing about time. Yet how fast work is done often matters as much as how much: two sprinters reaching the same speed do the same work, and the faster one wins. Power is the rate of doing work.

## Definition

> [!definition] Definition §A4.3.1: Average and Instantaneous Power
> The **average power** over a time interval $\Delta t$ in which work $\Delta W$ is done is $P_{\text{ave}} = \Delta W/\Delta t$. The (instantaneous) **power** is its limit,
>
> $$
> P = \frac{dW}{dt}, \qquad\text{so that}\qquad W = \int P\,dt
> $$
>
> ($W = P\,\Delta t$ when $P$ is constant). Since work transfers energy, power is also the rate of energy transfer. The SI unit is the **watt**, $1\ \mathrm W = 1\ \mathrm{J/s}$; the horsepower is $1\ \mathrm{hp} = 746\ \mathrm W$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §7.4, eqs. (7.10)–(7.11)*

^def-a4-3-1

## Power of a force

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.3.1: Power Delivered by a Force
> A force $\vec F$ acting on a body moving with velocity $\vec v$ delivers power
>
> $$
> P = \vec F \cdot \vec v .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §7.4, eq. (7.12)*

^thm-a4-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a time $dt$ the body is displaced by $d\vec r$ and the force does $dW = \vec F \cdot d\vec r$. Dividing by $dt$, $P = \vec F \cdot d\vec r/dt = \vec F \cdot \vec v$.

^der-a4-3-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.3 Power#^def-a4-3-1|Def. §A4.3.1]], [[§A4.1 Work#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], [[§19 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-1|Dot Product (LADR Def. 6.1)]]

> [!remark] Remark: The work–energy theorem as a rate
> For the net force on a particle, $P_{\text{net}} = \vec F_{\text{net}} \cdot \vec v = m\,\vec v \cdot d\vec v/dt = dK/dt$: the net power is the rate of change of kinetic energy, which is [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^thm-a4-2-1|Theorem §A4.2.1]] per unit time.

^rem-a4-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Whose power?
> Power belongs to a particular force. At constant velocity the *net* power is zero, but an individual force, such as the engine's push on a car climbing a hill, can deliver a large power that is balanced by negative power from gravity and drag. Power can be negative: a force against the velocity takes energy out at the rate $|\vec F\cdot\vec v|$.

^cau-a4-3-1

## Example

> [!example] Example §A4.3.1: Car Climbing a Hill
> A 1200 kg car drives up a 15% grade at a steady 90 km/h = 25 m/s; a quarter of the engine's power goes into overcoming air resistance and friction. What power must the engine deliver?
>
> The speed is constant, so the kinetic energy does not change and the engine's power is used up against gravity and the losses. A 15% grade means $\tan\theta = 0.15$, $\theta = 8.53^\circ$. Gravity's power is $m\vec g \cdot \vec v = -mgv\sin\theta$, so the engine supplies $0.75\,P = mgv\sin\theta$:
>
> $$
> P = \frac{(1200\ \mathrm{kg})(9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2})(25\ \mathrm{m/s})\sin 8.53^\circ}{0.75} = 58\ \mathrm{kW} \approx 78\ \mathrm{hp},
> $$
>
> a reasonable figure for a small car. Extra power would accelerate the car; much of an engine's output is lost as waste heat besides.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 7.12*

^ex-a4-3-1
