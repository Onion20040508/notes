For a particle moving in one dimension with conserved mechanical energy ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy|§A4.6]]), the graph of $U(x)$ alone tells where the particle can go, how fast it moves there, where it can rest, and whether a small push sends it back or away. With one integration it even gives the motion $x(t)$.

## Potential energy diagrams

> [!definition] Definition §A4.7.1: Potential Energy Diagram; Turning Point
> A **potential energy diagram** is the graph of $U(x)$ for a one-dimensional system, drawn together with the horizontal line at the (constant) mechanical energy $E$. A **turning point** is a position where $U(x) = E$: there the kinetic energy is zero and the velocity changes sign.
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.4*

^def-a4-7-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.7.1: Allowed Region and Speed
> If only conservative forces do work on a particle in one dimension, its motion is confined to the region where $U(x) \le E$, and at each position its speed is
>
> $$
> |v| = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\bigl(E - U(x)\bigr)}{m}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.4*

^thm-a4-7-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]], $K = E - U(x)$ at every position. Kinetic energy is never negative ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]]), so $U(x) \le E$; solving $\tfrac12 mv^2 = E - U(x)$ for $v$ gives the speed.

^der-a4-7-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|Theorem §A4.6.2]], [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two standard diagrams
> - **Free fall**, $U = mgy$: a straight line. The object cannot rise above $y_{\max} = E/mg$, a turning point; at the ground ($U = 0$) its speed is largest, $v_0 = \sqrt{2E/m}$.
> - **Mass on a spring**, $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$: a parabola, an *infinite potential well*. For every $E > 0$ the motion lies between the turning points $\pm x_{\max} = \pm\sqrt{2E/k}$, and the speed is largest, $\sqrt{2E/m}$, at the bottom $x = 0$.

^rem-a4-7-1

## Equilibrium and stability

> [!definition] Definition §A4.7.2: Equilibrium Point; Stable and Unstable
> An **equilibrium point** is a position $x_0$ where the force vanishes, $dU/dx = 0$ (the slope of the diagram is zero). It is **stable** if, on either side, the force points back toward $x_0$ (a restoring force), and **unstable** if it points away from $x_0$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.4*

^def-a4-7-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.7.2: Stability from the Shape of the Potential Energy
> An equilibrium at a local minimum of $U$ is stable, one at a local maximum is unstable. In particular, $d^2U/dx^2 > 0$ at $x_0$ means stable and $d^2U/dx^2 < 0$ unstable. Near a stable equilibrium the force is approximately that of a spring,
>
> $$
> F(x) \approx -U''(x_0)\,(x - x_0) ,
> $$
>
> with effective spring constant $U''(x_0)$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.4; §7.1 (small displacements from stable equilibrium act like a spring force)*

^thm-a4-7-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The force is $F = -dU/dx$ ([[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]]). Near a local minimum $U$ falls toward $x_0$ from both sides: $dU/dx < 0$ just left of $x_0$ and $> 0$ just right, so $F > 0$ on the left and $F < 0$ on the right, pointing back to $x_0$. At a local maximum all signs reverse and the force points away. For the second-derivative form, expand about $x_0$, where $U'(x_0) = 0$: $U(x) \approx U(x_0) + \tfrac12 U''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2$, so $F = -dU/dx \approx -U''(x_0)(x - x_0)$, restoring exactly when $U''(x_0) > 0$.

^der-a4-7-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]], [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^def-a4-7-2|Def. §A4.7.2]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-1|Interior Extremum Theorem (Theorem §29.1)]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|Taylor's Theorem (Theorem §31.2)]]

## Motion from the energy

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.7.3: Time from the Energy
> In one-dimensional motion with constant mechanical energy $E$, a particle moving in the $+x$ direction from $x_0$ at $t = 0$ reaches $x$ at time
>
> $$
> t = \int_{x_0}^{x} \frac{dx'}{\sqrt{2\,\bigl(E - U(x')\bigr)/m}} ,
> $$
>
> as long as it does not pass a turning point (for motion toward $-x$, put a minus sign in front). Inverting $t(x)$ gives $x(t)$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §8.3, eq. (8.14)*

^thm-a4-7-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|Theorem §A4.7.1]], $dx/dt = \sqrt{2(E - U(x))/m}$ while the particle moves toward $+x$. Separate the variables, $dt = dx/\sqrt{2(E - U)/m}$, and integrate from $t = 0$, $x = x_0$.

^der-a4-7-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|Theorem §A4.7.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|FTC I (Theorem §34.1)]]

> [!remark] Remark: Energy as the model
> Often a system is better described by the form of its kinetic and potential energies than by its forces; this is the starting point of advanced mechanics (Lagrangian mechanics, Chapter B6, planned) and of quantum mechanics. Theorem §A4.7.3 is the simplest instance: the potential energy determines the whole motion.

^rem-a4-7-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.7.1: A Double Well
> A particle moves on the $x$-axis with $U(x) = 2(x^4 - x^2)$ ($U$ in J, $x$ in m), no non-conservative forces, and $E = -0.25$ J.
> - *Allowed regions.* $K = E - U = -\tfrac14 - 2x^4 + 2x^2 \ge 0$. Completing the square in $x^2$ gives $2\bigl(x^2 - \tfrac12\bigr)^2 \le \tfrac14$, so $\tfrac12 - \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt2} \le x^2 \le \tfrac12 + \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt2}$: two separate regions, $0.38 \le x \le 0.92$ and $-0.92 \le x \le -0.38$.
> - *Equilibria.* $dU/dx = 8x^3 - 4x = 0$ at $x = 0$ and $x = \pm 1/\sqrt2 = \pm 0.707$. Since $d^2U/dx^2 = 24x^2 - 4$ is $-4 < 0$ at $0$ and $+8 > 0$ at $\pm 0.707$, the centre is unstable and the two others are stable ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|Theorem §A4.7.2]]).
>
> The particle oscillates in whichever well it starts in; it cannot cross the barrier at $x = 0$, where $U = 0 > E$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 8.10*

> [!example] Example §A4.7.2: Turning Points and Forces in a Quartic Well
> A particle has $U(x) = \tfrac14 c x^4$ with $c = 8\ \mathrm{N/m^3}$ and mechanical energy $E = 2$ J. Its turning points satisfy $\tfrac14 (8\ \mathrm{N/m^3})\,x^4 = 2$ J, so $x = \pm 1$ m. There the force is $F_x = -dU/dx = -cx^3 = \mp 8$ N: magnitude 8 N, pointing toward the origin in both cases. It is a restoring force that turns the particle round.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 8.6*

^ex-a4-7-2

> [!example] Example §A4.7.3: Harmonic Oscillation from the Energy
> Let $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$ and let the particle start from rest at $x_0 = A = \sqrt{2E/k}$, the turning point, and move toward $-x$. Theorem §A4.7.3 with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ gives
>
> $$
> t = -\int_{A}^{x} \frac{dx'}{\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x'^2}} = \frac{1}{\omega}\Bigl[\arcsin 1 - \arcsin\frac{x}{A}\Bigr],
> \qquad\text{so}\qquad
> x(t) = A\sin\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{2} - \omega t\Bigr) = A\cos\omega t .
> $$
>
> The particle oscillates sinusoidally with amplitude $A = \sqrt{2E/k}$ and frequency $\omega/2\pi = \sqrt{k/m}/2\pi$, the harmonic oscillator (Oscillations, Waves and Optics, planned). Here the formula is applied for $0 \le \omega t \le \pi$; the next half-cycle follows in the same way.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 8.11*

^ex-a4-7-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - At an equilibrium inside an interval, $U'(x_0) = 0$ is the [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-1|Interior Extremum Theorem]]; the spring approximation is the second-order case of [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|Taylor's theorem]] (Single Variable Analysis).
> - In several dimensions equilibria are critical points of $U(x, y, z)$ and stability is decided by the [[Second Derivative Test in Several Variables]].
