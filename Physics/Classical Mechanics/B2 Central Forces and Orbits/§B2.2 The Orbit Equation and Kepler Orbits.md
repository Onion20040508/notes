The effective potential tells which distances an orbit reaches ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]]); this section finds the **shape** $r(\varphi)$. Eliminating time and writing the orbit in $u = 1/r$ gives an equation that is linear for the inverse-square force, so the Kepler problem can be solved completely. Level A stated Kepler's first law without proof ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-1|Theorem §A8.3.1]]) and derived the second and third for a fixed Sun ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]], [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-4|Theorem §A8.3.4]]). Here all three become theorems for two bodies of any masses, with the eccentricity and the size of the orbit expressed through $E$ and $L$, a further conserved vector that exists only for $1/r$ potentials, and the position as a function of time. Notation as in [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential]]: one particle of reduced mass $\mu$ at the relative position $\vec r$, polar angle $\varphi$ in the orbit plane, $L = \mu r^2\dot\varphi > 0$; in this section $T$ is the period of an orbit, as at level A, not kinetic energy.

## The orbit equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.1: Orbit Equation in $u = 1/r$
> For motion in a central force $F(r)\hat r$ with angular momentum $L \neq 0$, the function $u(\varphi) = 1/r(\varphi)$ satisfies
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2u}{d\varphi^2} + u = -\frac{\mu}{L^2u^2}\,F\!\left(\frac1u\right),
> $$
>
> and its first integral, the energy equation,
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{du}{d\varphi}\Bigr)^2 + u^2 = \frac{2\mu}{L^2}\Bigl(E - U\!\left(\frac1u\right)\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Source: H&S §7.4.2, eqs. (7.45)–(7.48) · Morin §7.3.2, eq. (7.16), §7.4.1*

^thm-b2-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Since $\dot\varphi = L/(\mu r^2) = Lu^2/\mu$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]), time derivatives along the orbit become angle derivatives by the chain rule ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]), $\frac{d}{dt} = \frac{Lu^2}{\mu}\frac{d}{d\varphi}$. Hence, with primes for $d/d\varphi$,
>
> $$
> \dot r = \frac{d}{dt}\frac1u = -\frac{u'}{u^2}\,\frac{Lu^2}{\mu} = -\frac L\mu\,u', \qquad \ddot r = -\frac L\mu\,u''\,\frac{Lu^2}{\mu} = -\frac{L^2u^2}{\mu^2}\,u'' .
> $$
>
> The first derivative of $r$ loses its dependence on $u$; that is why $1/r$ is the right variable. Insert into the radial equation $\mu\ddot r - L^2/(\mu r^3) = F(r)$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]): $-\frac{L^2u^2}{\mu}(u'' + u) = F(1/u)$, which is the orbit equation. Inserting $\dot r$ into $\tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 + \frac{L^2u^2}{2\mu} + U = E$ gives $\frac{L^2}{2\mu}(u'^2 + u^2) = E - U$, the first integral. (H&S obtain the second-order equation the other way round, by differentiating the first-order one and cancelling $u'$, which is not allowed on a circular orbit, where $u' \equiv 0$.)

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the orbit equation
> - Without force the right side vanishes and $u = C\cos(\varphi - \varphi_0)$, i.e. $r\cos(\varphi - \varphi_0) = 1/C$: a straight line at distance $1/C$ from the origin (Morin Exercise 7.19).
> - The right side is $-\mu F/(L^2u^2) = -\mu r^2F(r)/L^2$. It is a constant exactly for the inverse-square force, which makes the equation linear with constant coefficients: the Kepler problem below. For other forces it is nonlinear, but stays useful near a circular orbit ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession]]).
> - It also runs backwards: a given orbit $r(\varphi)$ determines the force law that produces it ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^ex-b2-2-1|Example §B2.2.1]]). The equation is often called Binet's equation.

^rem-b2-2-1

> [!example] Example §B2.2.1: Which Force Makes an Exponential Spiral?
> Find the central potential in which a particle with angular momentum $L$ moves on $r = r_0e^{a\varphi}$, with $E = 0$.
>
> Here $u = e^{-a\varphi}/r_0$, so $u'' = a^2u$ and the orbit equation gives $(1 + a^2)u = -\mu F/(L^2u^2)$:
>
> $$
> F(r) = -\frac{(1 + a^2)L^2}{\mu r^3}, \qquad U(r) = -\frac{(1 + a^2)L^2}{2\mu r^2}\quad (U(\infty) = 0) .
> $$
>
> The first integral fixes the energy: $u'^2 + u^2 = (1 + a^2)u^2$ must equal $\frac{2\mu}{L^2}\bigl(E + \frac{(1 + a^2)L^2}{2\mu}u^2\bigr)$, so indeed $E = 0$. This is an attractive $-c/r^2$ potential stronger than the barrier, $c = (1 + a^2)L^2/(2\mu) > L^2/(2\mu)$, the case in which a particle can fall to the centre ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]] (4)): for $a < 0$ it spirals inward. It winds round infinitely often but arrives in finite time, since $\dot r = ar\dot\varphi = aL/(\mu r)$ gives $\frac{d}{dt}r^2 = 2aL/\mu$, a constant.
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 7.1 (solved there with the radial energy equation)*

## Kepler orbits

From now on the force is the attractive inverse-square force, $U = -\alpha/r$ with $\alpha > 0$: $\alpha = Gm_1m_2$ for gravity, $\alpha = \lvert q_1q_2\rvert/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ for attracting charges.

> [!definition] Definition §B2.2.1: Semi-Latus Rectum and Eccentricity of a Kepler Orbit
> For motion in $U = -\alpha/r$ with energy $E$ and angular momentum $L$, the **semi-latus rectum** $p$ and the **eccentricity** $e$ are
>
> $$
> p = \frac{L^2}{\mu\alpha}, \qquad e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{\mu\alpha^2}} .
> $$
>
> The point of the orbit nearest the centre of force is the **periapsis** (perihelion for the Sun, perigee for Earth), the farthest point of a bound orbit the **apoapsis** (aphelion, apogee); they are the turning points of [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]].
>
> *Source: Landau §15, eq. (15.4) · Morin §7.4.1, eqs. (7.25)–(7.26), (7.30) · H&S §7.4.2, eqs. (7.41), (7.51)*

^def-b2-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Two uses of $\alpha$
> Level A writes the focal form of an ellipse as $r = \alpha/(1 + e\cos\theta)$ ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^def-a8-3-1|Def. §A8.3.1]]), with $\alpha$ the semi-latus rectum. Here, following Morin and Landau, $\alpha$ is the strength of the force and the semi-latus rectum is $p$; Morin calls it $k$. H&S keep $GMm$ and write $\ell^2/(GMm^2)$ for $p$.

^cau-b2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.2: Kepler Orbits Are Conic Sections (Kepler's First Law)
> In $U = -\alpha/r$ with $L \neq 0$, the orbit is
>
> $$
> r(\varphi) = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos(\varphi - \varphi_0)},
> $$
>
> a conic section with a focus at the centre of force and its periapsis at $\varphi = \varphi_0$, at distance $r_{\min} = p/(1 + e)$. The energy decides the type:
>
> | Energy | Eccentricity | Orbit |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | $E = -\mu\alpha^2/2L^2 = U_{\text{eff,min}}$ | $e = 0$ | circle of radius $p$ |
> | $-\mu\alpha^2/2L^2 < E < 0$ | $0 < e < 1$ | ellipse, $r_{\max} = p/(1 - e)$ |
> | $E = 0$ | $e = 1$ | parabola |
> | $E > 0$ | $e > 1$ | one branch of a hyperbola, the one curving round the focus |
>
> For two bodies, $\vec r$ is the relative position; each body moves on a conic of the same eccentricity with the centre of mass at a focus, scaled by $m_2/M$ and $m_1/M$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: Morin §7.4.1–7.4.3 · H&S §7.4.2 · Landau §15, eq. (15.5)*

^thm-b2-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Solving.* With $F = -\alpha/r^2 = -\alpha u^2$, the orbit equation ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]) becomes
>
> $$
> u'' + u = \frac{\mu\alpha}{L^2} = \frac1p ,
> $$
>
> linear with constant coefficients. Its solutions are a particular solution $1/p$ plus the general solution $A\cos\varphi + B\sin\varphi$ of $u'' + u = 0$, which can be written $C\cos(\varphi - \varphi_0)$ with $C \ge 0$. Put $C = e/p$: $u = (1 + e\cos(\varphi - \varphi_0))/p$, the stated $r(\varphi)$.
>
> *The constant $e$.* At $\varphi = \varphi_0$, $u' = 0$ and $u = (1 + e)/p$. The first integral there reads $\frac{(1 + e)^2}{p^2} = \frac{2\mu}{L^2}\bigl(E + \alpha\frac{1 + e}{p}\bigr)$; with $2\mu\alpha/L^2 = 2/p$ this is $\frac{(1 + e)^2 - 2(1 + e)}{p^2} = \frac{e^2 - 1}{p^2} = \frac{2\mu E}{L^2}$, so $e^2 = 1 + 2\mu Ep^2/L^2 = 1 + 2EL^2/(\mu\alpha^2)$, as in [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]]. Since $e \ge 0$ is real, $E \ge -\mu\alpha^2/(2L^2)$, the minimum of $U_{\text{eff}} = -\alpha/r + L^2/(2\mu r^2)$, reached at $r = p$. The sign of $E$ decides whether $e <, =, > 1$.
>
> *The shape.* Take $\varphi_0 = 0$ and $x = r\cos\varphi$, $y = r\sin\varphi$. Then $r + ex = p$, and squaring $r = p - ex$ gives $x^2 + y^2 = p^2 - 2pex + e^2x^2$:
> - $e = 0$: $x^2 + y^2 = p^2$, a circle about the origin;
> - $0 < e < 1$: completing the square, $\dfrac{(x + ae)^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ with $a = \dfrac{p}{1 - e^2}$, $b = \dfrac{p}{\sqrt{1 - e^2}}$; the centre is at $(-ae, 0)$ and the foci at distance $c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = ae$ from it, so one focus is the origin ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^def-a8-3-1|Def. §A8.3.1]]);
> - $e = 1$: $y^2 = p^2 - 2px$, a parabola opening toward $-x$ with vertex $(p/2, 0)$ and focal length $p/2$, so its focus is the origin;
> - $e > 1$: $\dfrac{(x - ae)^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ with $a = \dfrac{p}{e^2 - 1}$, $b = \dfrac{p}{\sqrt{e^2 - 1}}$, focal distance $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = ae$, so again a focus is the origin. Squaring added the other branch, on which $p - ex < 0$; only the branch that curves round the origin solves $r = p - ex$, and $r \to \infty$ as $\cos\varphi \to -1/e$.

> [!remark] Remark: Kepler's laws, now proved
> Kepler found his three laws in Brahe's data, as empirical rules. Level A already files them as theorems, consequences of the inverse-square law, but the first was stated without proof ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^der-a8-3-1|its derivation]] defers to this chapter) and all three assumed a Sun that does not move. The layer stays *theorem*; what changes is that the proofs are now complete. They are theorems of Newtonian mechanics with $U = -\alpha/r$, proved for two bodies of any masses: the first is [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]], the second [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]] (true for any central force), the third [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]. What changes is small but real: the Sun sits not at the focus but near it (the centre of mass is the focus), and the constant in the third law depends on the planet's mass. Also new is what the theorem *excludes*: no spirals, no decaying orbits, no precession. H&S note that the circle and the parabola are special values never met exactly in nature, and that a radial fall ($L = 0$) is the limit $e \to 1$ of the other conics.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.3: Size and Speed in Terms of Energy and Angular Momentum
> For a bound Kepler orbit ($E < 0$) with semi-major axis $a$ and semi-minor axis $b$:
>
> $$
> a = \frac{p}{1 - e^2} = \frac{\alpha}{2\lvert E\rvert}, \qquad b = \frac{p}{\sqrt{1 - e^2}} = \frac{L}{\sqrt{2\mu\lvert E\rvert}}, \qquad r_{\min} = a(1 - e), \quad r_{\max} = a(1 + e) .
> $$
>
> Inversely $E = -\alpha/(2a)$ and $L = \sqrt{\mu\alpha a(1 - e^2)}$: the energy fixes only the size, the angular momentum then fixes the shape. The relative speed at distance $r$ obeys
>
> $$
> v^2 = \frac{\alpha}{\mu}\Bigl(\frac2r - \frac1a\Bigr) = G(m_1 + m_2)\Bigl(\frac2r - \frac1a\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> For a hyperbola ($E > 0$), $a = p/(e^2 - 1) = \alpha/(2E)$, $r_{\min} = a(e - 1)$, and the speed at infinity is $\sqrt{2E/\mu}$.
>
> *Source: Landau §15, eqs. (15.6)–(15.9) · H&S Example 7.1, eqs. (7.55)–(7.60) · Morin §7.4.3*

^thm-b2-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], $1 - e^2 = -2EL^2/(\mu\alpha^2) = 2\lvert E\rvert L^2/(\mu\alpha^2)$ and $p = L^2/(\mu\alpha)$, so
>
> $$
> a = \frac{p}{1 - e^2} = \frac{L^2}{\mu\alpha}\cdot\frac{\mu\alpha^2}{2\lvert E\rvert L^2} = \frac{\alpha}{2\lvert E\rvert}, \qquad b = \frac{p}{\sqrt{1 - e^2}} = \frac{L^2}{\mu\alpha}\cdot\frac{\sqrt\mu\,\alpha}{\sqrt{2\lvert E\rvert}\,L} = \frac{L}{\sqrt{2\mu\lvert E\rvert}} .
> $$
>
> $r_{\min,\max} = p/(1 \pm e) = a(1 \mp e)$ since $p = a(1 - e)(1 + e)$; and $L^2 = \mu\alpha p = \mu\alpha a(1 - e^2)$. Then $E = \tfrac12\mu v^2 - \alpha/r = -\alpha/(2a)$ gives $v^2$, with $\alpha/\mu = Gm_1m_2(m_1 + m_2)/(m_1m_2) = G(m_1 + m_2)$. The hyperbola is the same algebra with $e^2 - 1 = 2EL^2/(\mu\alpha^2)$; far away $U \to 0$, so $\tfrac12\mu v_\infty^2 = E$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.4: Kepler's Third Law for Two Bodies
> The period of a bound Kepler orbit is
>
> $$
> T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\mu a^3}{\alpha}}, \qquad\text{for gravity}\qquad T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G(m_1 + m_2)}\,a^3 ,
> $$
>
> where $a$ is the semi-major axis of the **relative** orbit; equivalently $T = \pi\alpha\sqrt{\mu/(2\lvert E\rvert^3)}$, a function of the energy alone. In terms of the semi-major axis $a_2 = (m_1/M)\,a$ of body 2's own ellipse about the centre of mass,
>
> $$
> T^2 = \frac{M^2}{m_1^2}\cdot\frac{4\pi^2a_2^3}{Gm_1} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Morin §7.4.4–7.4.5, eqs. (7.36)–(7.49) · H&S §7.6.2, eq. (7.64) · Landau §15, eq. (15.8)*

^thm-b2-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The radius vector sweeps area at the rate $L/(2\mu)$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]), and in one period it sweeps the whole ellipse, of area $\pi ab$. So $T = 2\mu\pi ab/L$. With $b = a\sqrt{1 - e^2}$ and $L = \sqrt{\mu\alpha a(1 - e^2)}$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]),
>
> $$
> T = \frac{2\pi\mu a^2\sqrt{1 - e^2}}{\sqrt{\mu\alpha a(1 - e^2)}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\mu a^3}{\alpha}} ,
> $$
>
> and $\alpha/\mu = G(m_1 + m_2)$. Substituting $a = \alpha/(2\lvert E\rvert)$ gives the energy form; substituting $a = (M/m_1)a_2$ gives the last form. (Morin's check: two equal masses $m$ on one circle of radius $r$ about their centre of mass have $a = 2r$, $M = 2m$, so $T^2 = 4\pi^2(2r)^3/(2Gm) = 16\pi^2r^3/(Gm)$, which is $m v^2/r = Gm^2/(2r)^2$ with $v = 2\pi r/T$.)

^der-b2-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]], [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^der-a8-3-4|the derivation of Theorem §A8.3.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the reduced mass changes in the third law
> Level A's $T^2 = 4\pi^2a^3/(GM_{\text{Sun}})$ ([[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-4|Theorem §A8.3.4]]) has the same constant for every planet. Exactly, the constant is $4\pi^2/G(M_{\text{Sun}} + m)$, smaller for Jupiter than for Mercury by about one part in a thousand. Morin's quick view of the factor $M^2/m_1^2$: replace the slightly moving Sun by a fixed mass $(m_1/M)^2m_1$ at the centre of mass; at the planet's distance from that point it exerts the same inverse-square force, so it gives the same orbit. Landau gets $T \propto a^{3/2}$ with no integration at all, from scaling: for a potential homogeneous of degree $k$ in the coordinates, geometrically similar orbits are traversed in times scaling as (length)$^{1 - k/2}$, and $k = -1$ gives the exponent $3/2$ (Landau §10).

## The Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.5: Laplace–Runge–Lenz Vector
> In $U = -\alpha/r$ the vector
>
> $$
> \vec A = \vec v \times \vec L - \alpha\,\hat r \qquad (\vec v = \dot{\vec r}, \ \vec L = \mu\,\vec r\times\vec v)
> $$
>
> is conserved. It lies in the orbit plane, points from the centre of force to the periapsis, and has magnitude $\lvert\vec A\rvert = \alpha e$, so $\lvert\vec A\rvert^2 = \alpha^2 + 2EL^2/\mu$. Its constancy alone gives the orbit: with $\varphi$ measured from $\vec A$,
>
> $$
> \vec r\cdot\vec A = \frac{L^2}{\mu} - \alpha r = \alpha e\,r\cos\varphi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{p}{1 + e\cos\varphi} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Landau §15, eq. (15.17)*

^thm-b2-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Conservation.* $\vec L$ is constant and $\mu\dot{\vec v} = -\alpha\hat r/r^2$, so with $\hat r\cdot\vec v = \dot r$ and $\vec a\times(\vec b\times\vec c) = \vec b(\vec a\cdot\vec c) - \vec c(\vec a\cdot\vec b)$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{dt}(\vec v\times\vec L) = -\frac{\alpha}{\mu r^2}\,\hat r\times\mu(\vec r\times\vec v) = -\frac{\alpha}{r^2}\bigl(\vec r\,\dot r - \vec v\,r\bigr) = \frac{\alpha}{r}\bigl(\vec v - \dot r\,\hat r\bigr) .
> $$
>
> And $\frac{d}{dt}\hat r = \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\vec r}{r} = \frac{\vec v}{r} - \frac{\vec r\,\dot r}{r^2} = \frac1r(\vec v - \dot r\,\hat r)$. The two rates are equal, so $\dot{\vec A} = \vec 0$.
>
> *Direction and size.* $\vec v\times\vec L$ and $\hat r$ are both perpendicular to $\vec L$, so $\vec A$ lies in the plane. At periapsis $\vec v = v\hat\varphi$ and $\vec L = L\hat z$, so $\vec v\times\vec L = vL\,\hat r$ and $\vec A = (vL - \alpha)\hat r$ with $vL = L^2/(\mu r_{\min}) = \alpha p/r_{\min} = \alpha(1 + e)$. Hence $\vec A = \alpha e\,\hat r_{\text{periapsis}}$, and $\alpha^2e^2 = \alpha^2 + 2EL^2/\mu$ by [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]].
>
> *The orbit.* $\vec r\cdot(\vec v\times\vec L) = \vec L\cdot(\vec r\times\vec v) = L^2/\mu$ (cyclic triple product), so $\vec r\cdot\vec A = L^2/\mu - \alpha r$. Setting this equal to $\alpha e\,r\cos\varphi$ and solving for $r$ gives the conic with $p = L^2/(\mu\alpha)$. (The second step used the conic; the third does not, and gives an independent proof of [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]] once $\lvert\vec A\rvert$ is computed directly from $E$ and $L$.)

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]] (cross product)

> [!remark] Remark: Why Kepler orbits close
> A conserved vector pointing at the periapsis means the periapsis cannot move: every Kepler ellipse is closed. Landau stresses that $\vec A$, like $E$ and $\vec L$, is a single-valued function of the state, and that it exists only for $U \propto 1/r$ (of either sign; Landau writes it for $U = \alpha/r$, hence his $+\alpha\vec r/r$). Add any other term to the potential and $\vec A$ is no longer constant; it turns slowly, and with it the periapsis: this is the precession of [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession]]. Morin and H&S do not use the vector.

## Position in time

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.6: Kepler's Equation
> On a bound Kepler orbit, with $t = 0$ at periapsis and $x$ along the major axis toward periapsis (focus at the origin), the motion is given in terms of the **eccentric anomaly** $\xi$ by
>
> $$
> r = a(1 - e\cos\xi), \qquad t = \sqrt{\frac{\mu a^3}{\alpha}}\,(\xi - e\sin\xi), \qquad x = a(\cos\xi - e), \quad y = b\sin\xi .
> $$
>
> One revolution is $0 \le \xi \le 2\pi$. To find the position at a given time, solve $\xi - e\sin\xi = 2\pi t/T$ for $\xi$ (Kepler's equation). On a hyperbola, $r = a(e\cosh\xi - 1)$, $t = \sqrt{\mu a^3/\alpha}\,(e\sinh\xi - \xi)$, $-\infty < \xi < \infty$.
>
> *Source: Landau §15, eqs. (15.10)–(15.12)*

^thm-b2-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-6|Theorem §B2.1.6]], $t = \int\frac{dr}{\sqrt{(2/\mu)(E + \alpha/r) - L^2/\mu^2r^2}} = \sqrt{\frac\mu2}\int\frac{r\,dr}{\sqrt{Er^2 + \alpha r - L^2/2\mu}}$. With $E = -\alpha/(2a)$ and $L^2/(\mu\alpha) = a(1 - e^2)$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]]) the quadratic under the root is
>
> $$
> -\frac{\alpha}{2a}\bigl(r^2 - 2ar + a^2(1 - e^2)\bigr) = \frac{\alpha}{2a}\bigl(a^2e^2 - (r - a)^2\bigr) ,
> $$
>
> so $t = \sqrt{\mu a/\alpha}\int\frac{r\,dr}{\sqrt{a^2e^2 - (r - a)^2}}$. Substitute $r - a = -ae\cos\xi$, $dr = ae\sin\xi\,d\xi$, $\sqrt{\cdots} = ae\sin\xi$: the integrand becomes $a(1 - e\cos\xi)\,d\xi$, and integrating from periapsis ($\xi = 0$, $r = a(1 - e)$) gives $t = \sqrt{\mu a^3/\alpha}\,(\xi - e\sin\xi)$. For the coordinates, the conic reads $ex = p - r = a(1 - e^2) - a(1 - e\cos\xi) = ae(\cos\xi - e)$, and $y^2 = r^2 - x^2 = a^2\bigl[(1 - e\cos\xi)^2 - (\cos\xi - e)^2\bigr] = a^2(1 - e^2)\sin^2\xi$. At $\xi = 2\pi$, $t = 2\pi\sqrt{\mu a^3/\alpha} = T$, as in [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]. The hyperbola is the same with $r - a = \ldots$ replaced by $r + a = ae\cosh\xi$.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.2.2: Two Stars Stopped in Their Orbit
> Two stars circle each other with period $P$. If both are suddenly stopped and fall together, how long until they collide (treating them as points)?
>
> The relative motion is that of a particle of mass $\mu$ in $-\alpha/r$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]). Before, the relative orbit is a circle of radius $r_0$ (the separation), so $P = 2\pi\sqrt{\mu r_0^3/\alpha}$ ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]] with $a = r_0$). After the stop, $\vec r$ starts from rest at $r_0$, so $L = 0$ and $E = -\alpha/r_0$; by [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]] this is the degenerate "ellipse" $e = 1$ of semi-major axis $a = \alpha/(2\lvert E\rvert) = r_0/2$. Kepler's equation still holds ($L$ entered only through $a(1 - e^2) = 0$): $r = a(1 - \cos\xi)$ runs from $2a = r_0$ at $\xi = \pi$ to $0$ at $\xi = 2\pi$, taking $\pi\sqrt{\mu a^3/\alpha}$, half a period of the degenerate orbit. Hence
>
> $$
> t_{\text{fall}} = \pi\sqrt{\frac{\mu(r_0/2)^3}{\alpha}} = \frac12\cdot\frac{1}{2^{3/2}}\cdot 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\mu r_0^3}{\alpha}} = \frac{P}{4\sqrt2} .
> $$
>
> The masses drop out, so the same number answers Morin's Exercise 7.12: Earth stopped in its orbit would fall into the Sun in $365.25\ \mathrm d/4\sqrt2 \approx 65$ days.
>
> *Source: H&S Problem 7.18 · Morin Exercise 7.12*

> [!example] Example §B2.2.3: Reading an Orbit off One Snapshot
> After engine cut-off, a $100\ \mathrm{kg}$ spacecraft is $r = 1.00\times10^7\ \mathrm m$ from Earth's centre, moving at $v = 7000\ \mathrm{m/s}$ at $45^\circ$ to the line from Earth. Find $E$, $L$, $a$ and $e$. Use $GM_E = 3.986\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}$; the craft is so light that $\mu = m$.
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac12 mv^2 - \frac{GM_Em}{r} = 2.45\times10^9\ \mathrm J - 3.99\times10^9\ \mathrm J = -1.54\times10^9\ \mathrm J, \qquad L = mrv\sin45^\circ = 4.95\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2/s} .
> $$
>
> The orbit is bound. By [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]] and [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^def-b2-2-1|Def. §B2.2.1]], with $\alpha = GM_Em$,
>
> $$
> a = \frac{\alpha}{2\lvert E\rvert} = 1.30\times10^7\ \mathrm m, \qquad e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{2EL^2}{m\alpha^2}} = \sqrt{1 - 0.474} = 0.725 .
> $$
>
> So $r_{\min} = a(1 - e) = 3.6\times10^6\ \mathrm m$, less than Earth's radius $6.37\times10^6\ \mathrm m$: whether the craft is now moving outward or inward, this orbit ends on the ground, after at most one more pass through apogee at $a(1 + e) = 2.2\times10^7\ \mathrm m$. Only $E$ and $L$ were needed, not the direction of motion along the line.
>
> *Source: H&S Problem 7.31 (the question about the ground is added here)*
