A circular orbit sits at an extremum of the effective potential ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]]). Two questions follow. Does a slightly disturbed circular orbit stay nearly circular? And if it does, does the disturbed orbit close, as every Kepler ellipse does ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]])? The first is the one-dimensional stability test of [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|Theorem §A4.7.2]] applied to $U_{\text{eff}}$, and it gives a condition on how fast the force may fall off. The second compares two frequencies, the radial oscillation and the revolution, and leads to the apsidal angle, to Bertrand's theorem (only $1/r$ and $r^2$ potentials have all bounded orbits closed), and to the slow turning of the periapsis when a Kepler potential is perturbed. Notation as in [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential]].

## Stability of circular orbits

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.1: Stability of Circular Orbits
> Let $r_0$ be a circular orbit for angular momentum $L$, so $U_{\text{eff}}'(r_0) = 0$, i.e. $L^2 = \mu r_0^3U'(r_0)$. Against small radial disturbances at fixed $L$ it is **stable** if $U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0) > 0$ and **unstable** if $U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0) < 0$. Since
>
> $$
> U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0) = U''(r_0) + \frac{3L^2}{\mu r_0^4} = U''(r_0) + \frac{3}{r_0}\,U'(r_0),
> $$
>
> in terms of the (attractive, $F(r_0) < 0$) force $F = -U'$ the orbit is stable exactly when
>
> $$
> F'(r_0) + \frac{3F(r_0)}{r_0} < 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{d\ln\lvert F\rvert}{d\ln r}\bigg|_{r_0} > -3 :
> $$
>
> the attraction must fall off more slowly than $1/r^3$. For a power law $F = -c\,r^n$ ($c > 0$) every circular orbit is stable if $n > -3$ and unstable if $n < -3$; in Morin's form $U = \beta r^k$ (with $\beta k > 0$), stable for $k > -2$.
>
> *Source: H&S §7.3, eqs. (7.22)–(7.23), Problem 7.25 · Morin Problem 7.4*

^thm-b2-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The radial motion is one-dimensional motion in $U_{\text{eff}}$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]), and an equilibrium of one-dimensional motion is stable at a minimum and unstable at a maximum of the potential ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|Theorem §A4.7.2]], [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]). Differentiating $U_{\text{eff}} = U + L^2/(2\mu r^2)$ twice gives $U'' + 3L^2/(\mu r^4)$; at $r_0$ replace $L^2$ by $\mu r_0^3U'(r_0)$. With $U' = -F$ the condition $U'' + 3U'/r_0 > 0$ is $-F' - 3F/r_0 > 0$; dividing by $-F(r_0) = \lvert F(r_0)\rvert > 0$ gives $r_0F'/F > -3$, which is the logarithmic derivative. For $F = -cr^n$, $r F'/F = n$. For $U = \beta r^k$, $F \propto r^{k-1}$, so $n = k - 1 > -3$ means $k > -2$; and a circular orbit needs $U' = \beta kr^{k-1} > 0$, i.e. $\beta k > 0$.

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]], [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|Theorem §A4.7.2]], [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why $1/r^3$ is the borderline
> Push the particle slightly outward at fixed $L$. The centrifugal term $L^2/(\mu r^3)$, which pushed outward and balanced the attraction at $r_0$, weakens like $1/r^3$. If the attraction weakens more slowly, it wins and pulls the particle back; if faster, the particle keeps going. For $U = -c/r^2$ (force $-2c/r^3$) both terms scale alike, $U_{\text{eff}} = (L^2 - 2\mu c)/(2\mu r^2)$ has no minimum at all, and the particle either escapes or spirals into the centre (Morin Problem 7.6, Landau §15 Problem 2). Gravity ($n = -2$), the constant force of [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^ex-b2-1-2|Example §B2.1.2]] ($n = 0$) and the spring ($n = 1$) all give stable circles. A disturbance that also changes $L$ moves the particle to the circular orbit of the new $L$, nearby when $r_0$ depends continuously on $L$, and the statement applies there.

## Radial oscillations and the apsidal angle

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.2: Small Oscillations about a Circular Orbit
> Near a stable circular orbit of radius $r_0$, the distance oscillates harmonically, $r(t) \approx r_0 + \delta\cos(\omega_rt + \psi)$, with
>
> $$
> \omega_r^2 = \frac{U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0)}{\mu} = \frac1\mu\Bigl(U''(r_0) + \frac3{r_0}U'(r_0)\Bigr),
> $$
>
> while the particle revolves at the mean angular velocity $\omega_\varphi = L/(\mu r_0^2)$, with $\omega_\varphi^2 = U'(r_0)/(\mu r_0)$. Their ratio depends only on the local shape of the force law:
>
> $$
> \frac{\omega_r}{\omega_\varphi} = \sqrt{3 + \frac{r_0U''(r_0)}{U'(r_0)}} = \sqrt{3 + \frac{d\ln\lvert F\rvert}{d\ln r}\bigg|_{r_0}} \ ; \qquad \text{for } U = \beta r^k:\quad \frac{\omega_r}{\omega_\varphi} = \sqrt{k + 2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 7.4, eqs. (7.67)–(7.70) · H&S Problems 7.26–7.27*

^thm-b2-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Expand $U_{\text{eff}}$ about its minimum to second order ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]): with $U_{\text{eff}}'(r_0) = 0$, the radial equation $\mu\ddot r = -U_{\text{eff}}'(r)$ becomes $\mu\ddot x \approx -U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0)\,x$ for $x = r - r_0$, simple harmonic motion with $\omega_r^2 = U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0)/\mu$ ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-3|WO Theorem §A1.2.3]], [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]). Morin notes that substituting $r = r_0 + \epsilon(t)$ into the radial equation and linearising does the same calculation the long way. The angular velocity $\dot\varphi = L/(\mu r^2) = \omega_\varphi\bigl(1 - 2x/r_0 + \ldots\bigr)$ wobbles at first order in $\delta$ about $\omega_\varphi = L/(\mu r_0^2)$; and $\omega_\varphi^2 = L^2/(\mu^2r_0^4) = U'(r_0)/(\mu r_0)$ by the circular-orbit condition. Dividing, $\omega_r^2/\omega_\varphi^2 = (U'' + 3U'/r_0)\,r_0/U' = 3 + r_0U''/U'$, and $r_0U''/U' = r_0F'/F$. For $U = \beta r^k$, $rU''/U' = k - 1$, so the ratio is $\sqrt{k + 2}$.

^der-b2-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]], [[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-3|WO Theorem §A1.2.3]], [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method#^thm-b1-3-4|Theorem §B1.3.4]]

> [!definition] Definition §B2.3.1: Apsidal Angle
> For a bounded orbit, the **apsidal angle** $\Phi$ is the angle through which the radius vector turns between a periapsis ($r_{\min}$) and the next apoapsis ($r_{\max}$):
>
> $$
> \Phi = \int_{r_{\min}}^{r_{\max}}\frac{L\,dr/r^2}{\sqrt{2\mu\bigl(E - U_{\text{eff}}(r)\bigr)}} .
> $$
>
> By the reflection symmetry of orbits about each apsis line ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]] (5)) every apoapsis-to-periapsis leg turns by the same $\Phi$, so successive periapses are separated by $2\Phi$. Landau's $\Delta\varphi$ is this full angle $2\Phi$. The **precession** per radial period is $2\Phi - 2\pi$: positive means the periapsis advances in the sense of the motion.
>
> *Source: H&S Problem 7.41 · Landau §14, eq. (14.10)*

^def-b2-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.3: Closed and Open Orbits; the Apsidal Angle near a Circle
> 1. A bounded orbit is closed exactly when $\Phi/\pi$ is rational, $2\Phi = 2\pi m/n$: after $n$ radial periods the radius vector has made $m$ turns and the orbit repeats. Otherwise it never closes and, as time goes on, it comes arbitrarily close to every point of the annulus $r_{\min} \le r \le r_{\max}$.
> 2. For a nearly circular orbit about a stable circle of radius $r_0$,
>
> $$
> \Phi = \pi\,\frac{\omega_\varphi}{\omega_r} = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3 + r_0U''(r_0)/U'(r_0)}}, \qquad \text{for } U = \beta r^k:\quad \Phi = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{k + 2}} .
> $$
>
> Kepler ($k = -1$): $\Phi = \pi$, one radial oscillation per revolution. Isotropic oscillator ($k = 2$): $\Phi = \pi/2$, two per revolution, the centred ellipses of Morin Problem 7.5 and H&S §7.4.1. Morin's further examples, $k = 7$ ($\Phi = \pi/3$) and $k = -7/4$ ($\Phi = 2\pi$, closing after two revolutions), close only to the accuracy of the small-oscillation approximation.
>
> *Source: Landau §14 · Morin Problem 7.4(c) · H&S Problem 7.41, eqs. (7.93)–(7.96)*

^thm-b2-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) The orbit is built from identical arcs, each turning by $\Phi$ ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]]). After $n$ radial periods the radius vector is back at $r_{\min}$ having turned by $2n\Phi$; the orbit repeats exactly when this is a multiple $2\pi m$ of a full turn. If $\Phi/\pi$ is irrational, the periapsis directions $2n\Phi$ (mod $2\pi$) never repeat and are dense in the circle, which is Landau's statement that the path covers the annulus.
>
> (2) Start at periapsis at $t = 0$: $r = r_0 - \delta\cos\omega_rt$ ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-2|Theorem §B2.3.2]]); the next apoapsis comes half a radial period later, at $t = \pi/\omega_r$. Meanwhile $\dot\varphi = \omega_\varphi(1 + (2\delta/r_0)\cos\omega_rt + O(\delta^2))$, and the first-order term integrates to zero over $0 \le t \le \pi/\omega_r$. So $\Phi = \omega_\varphi\pi/\omega_r + O(\delta^2)$; insert the ratio from Theorem §B2.3.2.

*Uses:* [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]], [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-2|Theorem §B2.3.2]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.4: Bertrand's Theorem
> The only central potentials in which **all** bounded orbits are closed are
>
> $$
> U(r) = -\frac{\alpha}{r}\ (\alpha > 0) \qquad\text{and}\qquad U(r) = \tfrac12 Kr^2\ (K > 0) .
> $$
>
> In the first, bounded orbits are ellipses with the centre of force at a focus ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]); in the second, ellipses centred on it.
>
> *Source: H&S §7.5 (proof as Problem 7.41) · Landau §14 · Morin Problem 7.4 (solution; cites Brown 1978)*

^thm-b2-3-4

> [!derivation]- Derivation (outline, following H&S Problem 7.41)
> None of the sources proves the theorem in the text: H&S set it as a guided problem, Morin cites the literature, Landau states it. The steps below fill in that outline. They are not a complete proof: step 1 assumes that the bounded orbits form a connected family on which $\Phi$ varies continuously, and the limits in steps 2 and 3 are taken under the integral sign without the justification a full proof needs.
>
> *1. Near-circular orbits force a power law.* Suppose there are stable circular orbits for every radius $R$ in some interval (each with its own $L$). Nearly circular orbits about them have $\Phi(R) = \pi/\sqrt{3 + RU''(R)/U'(R)}$ ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]]). If all of them are closed, $\Phi(R)/\pi$ is rational for every $R$; a continuous function with only rational values on an interval is constant. So $RU''/U' = s$, a constant, i.e. $U' = CR^s$ and $U = C'R^{k}$ with $k = s + 1$, or $U = C'\ln R$ when $s = -1$. Moreover $\Phi$ depends continuously on $E$ and $L$ and takes only values in $\pi\mathbb Q$, so it is the same for all bounded orbits: $\Phi = \pi/\sqrt{k + 2}$ for **every** bounded orbit, near-circular or not (with $k = 0$ for the logarithm). Stability needs $k > -2$.
>
> *2. Potentials rising to infinity ($k > 0$, or the logarithm).* Let $E \to \infty$. In $u = 1/r$, $\Phi = \int_{u_1}^{u_2}\frac{L\,du}{\sqrt{2\mu(E - U(1/u)) - L^2u^2}}$. Scale $u = u_2s$ with $u_2^2 \approx 2\mu E/L^2$: the root becomes $\sqrt{2\mu E}\,\sqrt{1 - s^2 - \varepsilon(s)}$, where $\varepsilon = U/E$ matters only near the tiny $s_1 = u_1/u_2 \to 0$. In the limit $\Phi \to \int_0^1 ds/\sqrt{1 - s^2} = \pi/2$: near the centre the particle passes almost freely, turning by $\pi/2$ from periapsis to infinity, and the far wall turns it back adding nothing. So $\pi/\sqrt{k + 2} = \pi/2$, and $k = 2$; the logarithm ($\pi/\sqrt2 \neq \pi/2$) is excluded.
>
> *3. Potentials tending to zero ($-2 < k < 0$, $U = -cr^k$, $c > 0$).* Let $E \to 0^-$. At $E = 0$, with $\gamma = -k \in (0, 2)$,
>
> $$
> \Phi = \int_0^{u_{\max}}\frac{L\,du}{\sqrt{2\mu cu^\gamma - L^2u^2}} = \int_0^1\frac{ds}{\sqrt{s^\gamma - s^2}} = \int_0^1\frac{s^{-\gamma/2}\,ds}{\sqrt{1 - s^{2-\gamma}}} = \frac{2}{2 - \gamma}\int_0^1\frac{dw}{\sqrt{1 - w^2}} = \frac{\pi}{2 + k},
> $$
>
> using $u = u_{\max}s$ with $u_{\max}^{2-\gamma} = 2\mu c/L^2$, then $w = s^{(2-\gamma)/2}$. So $\pi/\sqrt{k + 2} = \pi/(k + 2)$, i.e. $k + 2 = 1$: $k = -1$.
>
> Conversely both survivors do close all bounded orbits: Kepler by [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]], the oscillator because $\vec F = -K\vec r$ separates into three identical oscillators in Cartesian coordinates (H&S Problem 7.19).

## Precession of perturbed Kepler orbits

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.5: Periapsis Shift for a Perturbed Kepler Potential
> Let $U = -\alpha/r + \delta U(r)$ with $\delta U$ a small correction. To first order in $\delta U$, the periapsis advances per radial period by
>
> $$
> \delta\varphi = 2\Phi - 2\pi = \frac{\partial}{\partial L}\Bigl(\frac{2\mu}{L}\int_0^\pi r^2\,\delta U\,d\varphi\Bigr),
> $$
>
> the integral taken along the unperturbed ellipse $r = p/(1 + e\cos\varphi)$ and the derivative at fixed $E$. In particular
>
> $$
> \delta U = \frac{\beta}{r^2}:\quad \delta\varphi = -\frac{2\pi\mu\beta}{L^2} = -\frac{2\pi\beta}{\alpha p}; \qquad\qquad \delta U = \frac{\gamma}{r^3}:\quad \delta\varphi = -\frac{6\pi\mu^2\alpha\gamma}{L^4} = -\frac{6\pi\gamma}{\alpha p^2} .
> $$
>
> An extra attraction ($\beta < 0$ or $\gamma < 0$) makes the periapsis advance; an extra repulsion makes it regress. The second shift does not depend on the eccentricity.
>
> *Source: Landau §15, Problem 3*

^thm-b2-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $X(r) = 2\mu(E - U) - L^2/r^2$. By [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]],
>
> $$
> 2\Phi = 2\int_{r_{\min}}^{r_{\max}}\frac{L\,dr}{r^2\sqrt{X}} = -2\,\frac{\partial}{\partial L}\int_{r_{\min}}^{r_{\max}}\sqrt{X}\,dr ,
> $$
>
> since $\partial\sqrt X/\partial L = -L/(r^2\sqrt X)$, and the limits contribute nothing because $\sqrt X = 0$ there (Landau writes it this way to avoid spurious divergences in the expansion). Now $X = X_0 - 2\mu\,\delta U$ with $X_0$ the Kepler expression, and $\sqrt X \approx \sqrt{X_0} - \mu\,\delta U/\sqrt{X_0}$. The zeroth-order term gives $2\pi$, the closed Kepler orbit. The first-order term gives
>
> $$
> \delta\varphi = 2\,\frac{\partial}{\partial L}\int_{r_{\min}}^{r_{\max}}\frac{\mu\,\delta U\,dr}{\sqrt{X_0}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial L}\Bigl(\frac{2\mu}{L}\int_0^\pi r^2\,\delta U\,d\varphi\Bigr),
> $$
>
> because along the unperturbed orbit $d\varphi = L\,dr/(r^2\sqrt{X_0})$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-6|Theorem §B2.1.6]]), with $\varphi$ running from $0$ at periapsis to $\pi$ at apoapsis.
>
> For $\delta U = \beta/r^2$ the integral is $\pi\beta$, so $\delta\varphi = \partial_L(2\pi\mu\beta/L) = -2\pi\mu\beta/L^2$. For $\delta U = \gamma/r^3$, $r^2\delta U = \gamma/r = \gamma(1 + e\cos\varphi)/p = \gamma\mu\alpha(1 + e\cos\varphi)/L^2$, whose integral over $[0, \pi]$ is $\pi\gamma\mu\alpha/L^2$ (the $e$ term integrates to zero). Then $\delta\varphi = \partial_L(2\pi\gamma\mu^2\alpha/L^3) = -6\pi\gamma\mu^2\alpha/L^4$. With $p = L^2/(\mu\alpha)$ these are the forms in $p$.

^der-b2-3-5

*Uses:* [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^def-b2-3-1|Def. §B2.3.1]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-6|Theorem §B2.1.6]], [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Precession as a turning Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector
> In the pure Kepler potential the periapsis is fixed because the Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector points at it and is conserved ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-5|Theorem §B2.2.5]]). A small $\delta U$ leaves the orbit nearly an ellipse over one revolution, but that vector now turns slowly, by $\delta\varphi$ per revolution: the orbit is a slowly rotating ellipse, a rosette. Mercury's perihelion advances in this way, mostly because of the pull of the other planets, which acts as such a perturbation, with a small remainder that Newtonian gravity cannot explain ([[§A8.5 Beyond Newton#^rem-a8-5-3|Remark: Curved space-time]]). General relativity corrects the effective potential by a term of the $\gamma/r^3$ form with $\gamma < 0$, and the formula above then gives the observed remainder; that calculation belongs to Relativity C5 (planned).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.3.1: A $1/r^3$ Correction: Stable and Unstable Circles
> A particle of mass $m$ (fixed centre, $\mu = m$) moves in $U(r) = -\dfrac\alpha r - \dfrac{\alpha r_0^2}{r^3}$, with $\alpha, r_0 > 0$. Write $p = L^2/(m\alpha)$, the radius of the circular orbit when $r_0 = 0$.
>
> *Circular orbits.* $U$ is negative, increasing, and tends to $-\infty$ like $-1/r^3$ at the centre. $U_{\text{eff}}' = \alpha/r^2 + 3\alpha r_0^2/r^4 - L^2/(mr^3) = 0$ becomes, after multiplying by $r^4/\alpha$,
>
> $$
> r^2 - pr + 3r_0^2 = 0, \qquad r_\pm = \frac p2\Bigl(1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 12r_0^2/p^2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Circular orbits exist only if $p \ge 2\sqrt3\,r_0$, i.e. $L^2 \ge 2\sqrt3\,m\alpha r_0$. Since $U_{\text{eff}} \to -\infty$ at $r \to 0$ and $\to 0^-$ at $r \to \infty$, the inner extremum $r_-$ is a maximum (unstable) and the outer $r_+$ a minimum (stable); they merge at $p = 2\sqrt3\,r_0$. For $r_0 \ll p$ ($p$ is the length the exam asks for, against which $r_0$ must be small),
>
> $$
> r_+ \approx p - \frac{3r_0^2}{p}, \qquad r_- \approx \frac{3r_0^2}{p} .
> $$
>
> *Orbits near the circles.* The orbit equation ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]) with $F = -\alpha u^2 - 3\alpha r_0^2u^4$ reads $u'' + u = (1 + 3r_0^2u^2)/p$; the circles are the roots $u = 1/r_\pm$ of $3r_0^2u^2 - pu + 1 = 0$. Put $u = 1/r_\pm + w$ and keep first order in $w$:
>
> $$
> w'' = \Bigl(\frac{6r_0^2}{p\,r_\pm} - 1\Bigr)w = \mp\kappa^2\,w, \qquad \kappa^2 = \sqrt{1 - \frac{12r_0^2}{p^2}} ,
> $$
>
> with $+\kappa^2$ at the unstable circle $r_-$ and $-\kappa^2$ at the stable circle $r_+$. Near $r_-$, $w = Ae^{\kappa\varphi} + Be^{-\kappa\varphi}$ with $\kappa \approx 1$: the orbit leaves the unstable circle exponentially in the angle, spiralling inward toward the centre or outward, and only the special orbits with $A = 0$ approach it. Near $r_+$, $w = C\cos(\kappa\varphi + \psi)$, so the apsidal angle is ([[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]])
>
> $$
> \Phi = \frac\pi\kappa = \pi\Bigl(1 - \frac{12r_0^2}{p^2}\Bigr)^{-1/4} \approx \pi\Bigl(1 + \frac{3r_0^2}{p^2}\Bigr), \qquad 2\Phi - 2\pi \approx \frac{6\pi r_0^2}{p^2} .
> $$
>
> This agrees with [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-5|Theorem §B2.3.5]] for $\gamma = -\alpha r_0^2$: $-6\pi\gamma/(\alpha p^2) = 6\pi r_0^2/p^2$, a forward advance, now for orbits of any eccentricity.
>
> *Source: PHY 421, Midterm 2 (Fall 2015), Problem 3 (the last part, the orbit near the unstable circle, was a bonus); the stable-circle and precession parts are added here*

> [!example] Example §B2.3.2: An Exactly Precessing Orbit
> A particle of mass $m$ feels $F(r) = -\dfrac{\alpha}{r^2} - \dfrac{\lambda}{r^3}$, gravity plus a weak inverse-cube attraction ($\lambda > 0$). The orbit equation gives
>
> $$
> u'' + u = \frac{m}{L^2}(\alpha + \lambda u) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad u'' + \nu^2u = \frac{m\alpha}{L^2}, \qquad \nu^2 = 1 - \frac{m\lambda}{L^2} .
> $$
>
> For $\lambda < L^2/m$ the solution is
>
> $$
> r(\varphi) = \frac{p'}{1 + e\cos\nu\varphi}, \qquad p' = \frac{\nu^2L^2}{m\alpha} ,
> $$
>
> a conic in the variable $\nu\varphi$. The periapsis returns when $\nu\varphi$ grows by $2\pi$, so for $e < 1$ the orbit is what H&S call a precessing ellipse: each loop is close to an ellipse, and the periapsis advances by $2\pi(1/\nu - 1) > 0$ per radial period; it closes only if $\nu$ is rational. For small $\lambda$, $2\pi(1/\nu - 1) \approx \pi m\lambda/L^2$, which is [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession#^thm-b2-3-5|Theorem §B2.3.5]] with $\delta U = -\lambda/(2r^2)$, $\beta = -\lambda/2$: here the first-order formula is the leading term of an exact result.
>
> The limiting value is $\lambda = L^2/m$. At it, $u'' = m\alpha/L^2$ makes $u$ grow like $\varphi^2$; for $\lambda > L^2/m$, $u'' - \lvert\nu\rvert^2u = m\alpha/L^2$ has growing exponential solutions. In both cases the particle falls into the centre: $r^2U \to -\lambda/2 \le -L^2/(2m)$, the fall condition of [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]] (4). The inverse-cube force is special because it only renormalises the centrifugal barrier: $U_{\text{eff}} = -\alpha/r + (L^2 - m\lambda)/(2mr^2)$.
>
> *Source: H&S Problem 7.23 (Morin Problem 7.6 and Landau §15, Problem 2 treat the pure $1/r^2$ potential)*
