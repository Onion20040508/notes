Level A solved orbits only with the attracting body held fixed ($m \ll M$: [[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-4|Theorem §A8.2.4]], [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-1|Theorem §A8.3.1]]), and treated angular momentum ([[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-1|Theorem §A6.6.1]]) and energy diagrams ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|Theorem §A4.7.1]]) separately. This section takes two bodies of any masses interacting through any central force and cuts the problem from six coordinates down to one. Separating the centre of mass leaves one particle of **reduced mass** $\mu$ in a fixed central field; conservation of angular momentum makes its motion planar and fixes its angular speed; energy conservation then turns the distance $r(t)$ into one-dimensional motion in an **effective potential**, to which the energy method of [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method]] applies. Notation for the chapter: $U$ is potential energy, as at level A (Morin writes $V$); $\vec L$ is angular momentum (H&S write $\ell$, Landau $M$); $\varphi$ is the polar angle in the plane of the orbit.

## Central forces and the two-body system

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.1: Central Force
> A force on a particle is **central** about a point $O$ if it points along the line through $O$ and its magnitude depends only on the distance $r$ from $O$:
>
> $$
> \vec F = F(r)\,\hat r, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \vec F = -\nabla U(r) = -U'(r)\,\hat r ,
> $$
>
> with $U(r) = -\int F(r)\,dr$; $F < 0$ is attractive. Two bodies have a **central interaction** if the force on body 2 due to body 1 is $F(r)\,\hat r$ with $\vec r$ the vector from 1 to 2, and the force on body 1 is its negative. Examples: gravity and the Coulomb force ($U \propto 1/r$), and the three-dimensional spring with relaxed length zero ($U = \tfrac12 kr^2$).
>
> *Source: Morin §7 (introduction) · H&S §7.1 · Landau §14*

^def-b2-1-1

> [!model] Model §B2.1.1: Isolated Two-Body System
> Two particles of masses $m_1, m_2$, at $\vec r_1, \vec r_2$ in an inertial frame ([[§A3.2 Newton's First Law#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]), with a central interaction of potential energy $U(\lvert\vec r_2 - \vec r_1\rvert)$ and no external forces. Assumptions:
> - the bodies are points, or spherically symmetric bodies that never overlap (shell theorem, [[§A8.1 Universal Gravitation#^thm-a8-1-5|Theorem §A8.1.5]]); no tides, no spin;
> - nonrelativistic speeds, and the force acts instantaneously along the line joining the bodies;
> - every other body is ignored (for a planet: the other planets).
>
> *Source: Morin §7.4.5 · H&S §7.2 · Landau §13*

^mod-b2-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.2: Centre-of-Mass and Relative Coordinates; Reduced Mass
> With $M = m_1 + m_2$, the **centre of mass** ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^def-a5-4-1|Def. §A5.4.1]]) and the **relative position** are
>
> $$
> \vec R = \frac{m_1\vec r_1 + m_2\vec r_2}{M}, \qquad \vec r = \vec r_2 - \vec r_1 ,
> $$
>
> so that $\vec r_1 = \vec R - \dfrac{m_2}{M}\,\vec r$ and $\vec r_2 = \vec R + \dfrac{m_1}{M}\,\vec r$. The **reduced mass** is
>
> $$
> \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}, \qquad \frac1\mu = \frac1{m_1} + \frac1{m_2} .
> $$
>
> $\vec r$ points from body 1 to body 2 (H&S); Morin and Landau use $\vec r_1 - \vec r_2$, which flips the signs in the inverse formulas.
>
> *Source: H&S §7.2, eqs. (7.7)–(7.12) · Morin §7.4.5, eqs. (7.41)–(7.44) · Landau §13*

^def-b2-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.2: Reduction of the Two-Body Problem
> For the system of [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^mod-b2-1-1|Model §B2.1.1]]:
> 1. the centre of mass moves with constant velocity, $\ddot{\vec R} = \vec 0$;
> 2. the relative position obeys the equation of a single particle of mass $\mu$ in the **fixed** central field $U(r)$:
>
> $$
> \mu\,\ddot{\vec r} = F(r)\,\hat r = -\nabla U(r) ;
> $$
>
> 3. the kinetic energy, and the energy and angular momentum in the centre-of-mass frame, are those of that particle:
>
> $$
> T = \tfrac12 M\dot{\vec R}^2 + \tfrac12\mu\dot{\vec r}^2, \qquad E_{\text{cm}} = \tfrac12\mu\dot{\vec r}^2 + U(r), \qquad \vec L_{\text{cm}} = \mu\,\vec r \times \dot{\vec r} .
> $$
>
> The bodies' positions relative to the centre of mass, $-\tfrac{m_2}{M}\vec r$ and $+\tfrac{m_1}{M}\vec r$, are copies of the path $\vec r(t)$ scaled by $m_2/M$ and $m_1/M$ (the first one also reflected through the centre of mass).
>
> *Source: Morin §7.4.5 · H&S §7.2 · Landau §13*

^thm-b2-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The force on body 2 is $F(r)\hat r$ and, by the third law ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|Principle §A3.5.1]]), the force on body 1 is $-F(r)\hat r$. The second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]]) for each body gives $m_1\ddot{\vec r}_1 = -F\hat r$ and $m_2\ddot{\vec r}_2 = F\hat r$.
>
> (1) Adding, $M\ddot{\vec R} = m_1\ddot{\vec r}_1 + m_2\ddot{\vec r}_2 = \vec 0$ ([[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]] with zero external force).
>
> (2) Dividing each equation by its mass and subtracting, $\ddot{\vec r} = \ddot{\vec r}_2 - \ddot{\vec r}_1 = \bigl(\tfrac1{m_2} + \tfrac1{m_1}\bigr)F\hat r = F\hat r/\mu$. The force derives from $U(r)$ by [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]], with $\nabla r = \vec r/r$.
>
> (3) Insert $\dot{\vec r}_1 = \dot{\vec R} - \tfrac{m_2}{M}\dot{\vec r}$ and $\dot{\vec r}_2 = \dot{\vec R} + \tfrac{m_1}{M}\dot{\vec r}$ into $T = \tfrac12 m_1\dot{\vec r}_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2\dot{\vec r}_2^2$. The cross terms $\mp\tfrac{m_1m_2}{M}\dot{\vec R}\cdot\dot{\vec r}$ cancel, and
>
> $$
> T = \tfrac12 M\dot{\vec R}^2 + \tfrac12\Bigl(\frac{m_1m_2^2}{M^2} + \frac{m_2m_1^2}{M^2}\Bigr)\dot{\vec r}^2 = \tfrac12 M\dot{\vec R}^2 + \tfrac12\mu\dot{\vec r}^2 .
> $$
>
> In the centre-of-mass frame $\dot{\vec R} = \vec 0$, which gives $E_{\text{cm}}$. The angular momentum about the centre of mass is $\sum_i m_i(\vec r_i - \vec R)\times(\dot{\vec r}_i - \dot{\vec R}) = \bigl[m_1(m_2/M)^2 + m_2(m_1/M)^2\bigr]\,\vec r\times\dot{\vec r} = \mu\,\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}$. The last statement is the inverse formulas of [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-2|Def. §B2.1.2]] with $\vec R$ as origin.

^der-b2-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^mod-b2-1-1|Model §B2.1.1]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-2|Def. §B2.1.2]], [[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|Principle §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A5.4 Centre of Mass#^thm-a5-4-1|Theorem §A5.4.1]], [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]], [[§4 Partial Derivatives#^ex-4-2|452 Ex. §4.2]] ($\partial r/\partial x = x/r$)

> [!remark] Remark: The Lagrangian route, and why no fictitious force appears
> Morin, H&S and Landau reach the same result by writing the Lagrangian in the new coordinates, $\tfrac12 M\dot{\vec R}^2 + \tfrac12\mu\dot{\vec r}^2 - U(r)$: $\vec R$ does not appear, so it is cyclic and the total momentum $M\dot{\vec R}$ is conserved ([[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-1|Theorem §B6.4.1]]), and what is left is the Lagrangian of one particle of mass $\mu$ ([[§B6.1 Generalized Coordinates and Lagrange's Equations#^thm-b6-1-4|Theorem §B6.1.4]]). H&S stress a point that looks paradoxical: $\vec r$ is measured from body 1, which accelerates, yet no fictitious force ([[§B9.1 Accelerating and Rotating Frames#^thm-b9-1-5|Theorem §B9.1.5]]) enters. The equation was derived in an inertial frame; the acceleration of body 1 is absorbed into the single parameter $\mu$ in place of $m_2$.

^rem-b2-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: What the reduced mass replaces
> - $\mu$ replaces the *inertia* only. The interaction is unchanged: for gravity $U = -Gm_1m_2/r = -\alpha/r$ with $\alpha = Gm_1m_2$, not $G\mu M$ (Morin §7.4.5). Note $\alpha/\mu = GM$.
> - $\mu$ is smaller than either mass. For $m_2 \ll m_1$, $\mu \approx m_2(1 - m_2/m_1)$: for Earth and Sun $\mu$ is below Earth's mass by one part in $3\times10^5$, and the centre of mass sits about $450\ \mathrm{km}$ from the Sun's centre. For equal masses $\mu = m/2$.
> - $\vec r$ is the separation, not either body's distance from the centre of mass, and $E_{\text{cm}}$, $\vec L_{\text{cm}}$ are the totals for both bodies in the centre-of-mass frame.

## Angular momentum: planar motion and equal areas

From here on the problem is that of [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]: one particle of mass $\mu$ at $\vec r$ in a fixed central field (for a single particle in a field from a fixed source, read $\mu = m$).

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.3: Angular Momentum in a Central Field; Kepler's Second Law
> Let $\mu\ddot{\vec r} = F(r)\hat r$. Then:
> 1. the angular momentum $\vec L = \mu\,\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}$ is constant;
> 2. if $\vec L \neq \vec 0$ the motion stays in the fixed plane through the origin perpendicular to $\vec L$; if $\vec L = \vec 0$ it stays on a fixed line through the origin;
> 3. with polar coordinates $(r, \varphi)$ in that plane, oriented so that $\vec L = L\hat z$ with $L > 0$,
>
> $$
> L = \mu r^2\dot\varphi ,
> $$
>
> so $\varphi$ increases monotonically;
> 4. the radius vector sweeps area at the constant rate
>
> $$
> \frac{dA}{dt} = \tfrac12 r^2\dot\varphi = \frac{L}{2\mu} \qquad\text{(Kepler's second law)} .
> $$
>
> For two bodies the same holds for each body's position vector from the centre of mass, with the rates scaled by $(m_2/M)^2$ and $(m_1/M)^2$.
>
> *Source: Morin §7.1 (Theorems 7.1–7.2), §7.4.4 · H&S §7.1, §7.6.1 · Landau §14, eqs. (14.2)–(14.3)*

^thm-b2-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $\dot{\vec L} = \mu\,\dot{\vec r}\times\dot{\vec r} + \vec r\times F(r)\hat r = \vec 0$: the first product has equal factors, the second parallel ones. This is [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-1|Theorem §A6.6.1]] with zero torque about the centre.
>
> (2) $\vec r\cdot\vec L = \mu\,\vec r\cdot(\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}) = 0$ at all times, and $\vec L$ is fixed, so $\vec r$ stays in the plane $\perp\vec L$ through the origin. If $\vec L = \vec 0$ the velocity is always along $\vec r$; then $d\hat r/dt = (\dot{\vec r} - \hat r(\hat r\cdot\dot{\vec r}))/r = \vec 0$, and the direction $\hat r$ never changes.
>
> (3) In plane polar coordinates $\dot{\vec r} = \dot r\,\hat r + r\dot\varphi\,\hat\varphi$ ([[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]), so $\vec r\times\dot{\vec r} = r^2\dot\varphi\,\hat z$ and $L = \mu r^2\dot\varphi$. With $L > 0$, $\dot\varphi > 0$ always.
>
> (4) In time $dt$ the radius vector turns by $d\varphi$ and sweeps a thin sector of area $dA = \tfrac12 r\cdot r\,d\varphi$ (the polar area element, [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-7|452 Thm. §15.7]]), so $dA/dt = \tfrac12 r^2\dot\varphi = L/(2\mu)$. For the two bodies, $\vec r_i - \vec R$ is $\vec r$ scaled by $m_2/M$ or $m_1/M$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]), and areas scale with the square.

^der-b2-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^def-a6-6-1|Def. §A6.6.1]], [[§A6.6 Angular Momentum and Its Conservation#^thm-a6-6-1|Theorem §A6.6.1]], [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]], [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^thm-15-7|452 Thm. §15.7]]

> [!remark] Remark: What level B adds to Kepler's second law
> [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^thm-a8-3-2|Theorem §A8.3.2]] derived equal areas for a planet about a fixed Sun, and [[§A8.3 Kepler's Laws#^cau-a8-3-1|its caution]] noted that only the central direction of the force matters. Here the law holds exactly for two bodies of any masses, for the relative vector and for each body about the centre of mass, and it comes with planarity: a central-force problem is two-dimensional from the start. Landau calls $L = \mu r^2\dot\varphi$ the *area integral*. In Lagrangian language $\varphi$ is cyclic and $L$ its conjugate momentum, and the conservation law is the consequence of rotational symmetry (Noether's theorem, [[§B6.4 Symmetries, Conservation Laws and Noether's Theorem#^thm-b6-4-2|Theorem §B6.4.2]]; H&S note that Kepler had found a symmetry without knowing it).

## The radial equation and the effective potential

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.3: Effective Potential
> For a particle of mass $\mu$ with angular momentum $L$ in a central potential $U(r)$, the **effective potential** is
>
> $$
> U_{\text{eff}}(r) = U(r) + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2} .
> $$
>
> The added term is the **centrifugal energy** (Landau), centrifugal potential (H&S) or angular-momentum barrier (Morin). It depends on the orbit through $L$: each value of $L$ has its own curve $U_{\text{eff}}$.
>
> *Source: Morin §7.2, eq. (7.10) · H&S §7.3, eqs. (7.20)–(7.21) · Landau §14, eq. (14.8)*

^def-b2-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.4: Radial Motion as One-Dimensional Motion
> In a central field, with $L$ fixed, the distance $r(t)$ obeys
>
> $$
> \mu\ddot r = -U_{\text{eff}}'(r) = F(r) + \frac{L^2}{\mu r^3}, \qquad \tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 + U_{\text{eff}}(r) = E ,
> $$
>
> where $E = \tfrac12\mu(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2) + U(r)$ is the conserved energy. So $r$ moves exactly like a particle of mass $\mu$ on the half-line $r > 0$ in the potential $U_{\text{eff}}$; once $r(t)$ is known, $\dot\varphi = L/(\mu r^2)$ gives $\varphi(t)$.
>
> *Source: Morin §7.2, eqs. (7.6)–(7.11) · H&S §7.3, eq. (7.19) · Landau §14, eqs. (14.4), (14.8)*

^thm-b2-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The radial component of the acceleration in polar coordinates is $\ddot r - r\dot\varphi^2$ ([[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]; the second term is the centripetal acceleration of [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]]). The force is purely radial, so the radial component of $\mu\ddot{\vec r} = F\hat r$ reads $\mu(\ddot r - r\dot\varphi^2) = F(r)$. Eliminate $\dot\varphi$ with $\dot\varphi = L/(\mu r^2)$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]):
>
> $$
> \mu\ddot r = F(r) + \frac{L^2}{\mu r^3} = -\frac{d}{dr}\Bigl(U(r) + \frac{L^2}{2\mu r^2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> Multiplying by $\dot r$ makes both sides exact derivatives, $\frac{d}{dt}\bigl[\tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 + U_{\text{eff}}(r)\bigr] = 0$, which gives the energy equation with some constant $E$. It is the energy: since $v^2 = \dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2$ and $\tfrac12\mu r^2\dot\varphi^2 = L^2/(2\mu r^2)$, the expression $\tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 + U_{\text{eff}}$ is $\tfrac12\mu v^2 + U$.

^der-b2-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^def-b2-1-3|Def. §B2.1.3]], [[§B1.1 Newton's Laws in Vector and Curvilinear Form#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]], [[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-1|Theorem §A2.7.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: The centrifugal term is not a new force
> $L^2/(2\mu r^2)$ is the kinetic energy of the angular motion, $\tfrac12\mu r^2\dot\varphi^2$, rewritten as a function of $r$ through the conservation of $L$. Its "force" $+L^2/(\mu r^3)$ is the term $\mu r\dot\varphi^2$ moved to the right-hand side of the radial equation: it appears only because $r$ is measured along a rotating direction. No body exerts it, and it does not belong on a free-body diagram in an inertial frame ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^cau-a3-9-2|Caution: Centrifugal is not centripetal]]). It keeps the particle away from the centre because, at fixed $L$, the angular speed $\dot\varphi \propto 1/r^2$ grows faster than the energy can supply as $r \to 0$.

^cau-b2-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.5: Turning Points, Bounded and Unbounded Orbits, Circular Orbits
> Fix $L \neq 0$ and $E$.
> 1. The motion is confined to $\{r : U_{\text{eff}}(r) \le E\}$. At a **turning point** (apsis), $U_{\text{eff}}(r) = E$, the radial velocity vanishes but $\dot\varphi = L/(\mu r^2) \neq 0$: the particle moves tangentially there, it does not stop.
> 2. If the allowed interval is $[r_{\min}, r_{\max}]$ with both ends finite, $r$ oscillates periodically between them and the orbit lies in the annulus $r_{\min} \le r \le r_{\max}$ (**bounded**, or finite, motion). If it is $[r_{\min}, \infty)$, the particle comes in from infinity, turns at $r_{\min}$ and leaves (**unbounded** motion).
> 3. A **circular orbit** of radius $r_0$ exists exactly when $U_{\text{eff}}'(r_0) = 0$, that is
>
> $$
> L^2 = \mu r_0^3\,U'(r_0) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad -F(r_0) = \mu r_0\dot\varphi^2 ,
> $$
>
> and then $E = U_{\text{eff}}(r_0)$. It requires an attractive force at $r_0$, which supplies exactly the centripetal force ([[§A3.9 Dynamics of Circular Motion#^thm-a3-9-1|Theorem §A3.9.1]]). Its stability is the subject of [[§B2.3 Stability of Orbits and Precession]].
> 4. **Fall to the centre.** With $L \neq 0$ the particle can reach $r \to 0$ only if $r^2U(r)$ tends to a limit below $-L^2/(2\mu)$, i.e. $U \to -\infty$ at least as fast as $-a/r^2$ with $a > L^2/(2\mu)$, or as $-1/r^n$ with $n > 2$.
> 5. **Symmetry.** The orbit is symmetric under reflection in the line from the centre through any turning point; a bounded orbit is made of identical arcs from $r_{\min}$ to $r_{\max}$, each reflected copy of the last.
>
> *Source: Morin §7.2 · H&S §7.3, eqs. (7.22)–(7.23) · Landau §14, eqs. (14.9)–(14.11)*

^thm-b2-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $\tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 = E - U_{\text{eff}}(r) \ge 0$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]), as for any one-dimensional motion ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|Theorem §A4.7.1]], [[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method]]).
>
> (2) Between two simple turning points one-dimensional motion is periodic ([[§B1.3 One-Dimensional Motion and the Energy Method]]); $\dot\varphi > 0$ meanwhile, so the particle circles the centre while $r$ oscillates. With only an inner turning point, $\dot r$ keeps one sign after the turn.
>
> (3) $r \equiv r_0$ forces $\ddot r = 0$, hence $U_{\text{eff}}'(r_0) = U'(r_0) - L^2/(\mu r_0^3) = 0$, and $\dot r = 0$, hence $E = U_{\text{eff}}(r_0)$. Conversely, starting at an extremum of $U_{\text{eff}}$ with $\dot r = 0$, $r \equiv r_0$ solves the radial equation, and by uniqueness of solutions it is the motion. With $\dot\varphi = L/(\mu r_0^2)$ the condition reads $U'(r_0) = \mu r_0\dot\varphi^2$, and $U' > 0$ means $F < 0$.
>
> (4) Multiply $\tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 = E - U - L^2/(2\mu r^2) \ge 0$ by $r^2$: $r^2U(r) + L^2/(2\mu) \le Er^2 \to 0$ as $r \to 0$. So small $r$ is reachable only if $\lim_{r\to0} r^2U(r) < -L^2/(2\mu)$ (Landau's eq. (14.11)).
>
> (5) Take $t = 0$ at a turning point $r_a$ and put $\varphi(0) = 0$. If $(r(t), \varphi(t))$ solves $\mu\ddot r = -U_{\text{eff}}'(r)$, $\dot\varphi = L/(\mu r^2)$, so does $(\tilde r, \tilde\varphi)(t) = (r(-t), -\varphi(-t))$: the radial equation contains only the second derivative, and $\dot{\tilde\varphi}(t) = \dot\varphi(-t) = L/(\mu\tilde r(t)^2)$. Both solutions start at $r_a$ with $\dot r = 0$ and $\varphi = 0$, so by uniqueness they coincide: $r(-t) = r(t)$ and $\varphi(-t) = -\varphi(t)$, a reflection in the line $\varphi = 0$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.6: Solution by Quadratures
> The motion in a central field is given by two integrals:
>
> $$
> t = \int\frac{dr}{\sqrt{\dfrac2\mu\bigl(E - U_{\text{eff}}(r)\bigr)}} + \text{const}, \qquad \varphi = \int\frac{L\,dr/r^2}{\sqrt{2\mu\bigl(E - U_{\text{eff}}(r)\bigr)}} + \text{const},
> $$
>
> taken with the sign of $\dot r$ on each leg between turning points. The first gives $r(t)$ implicitly, the second the shape of the orbit $\varphi(r)$.
>
> *Source: Landau §14, eqs. (14.6)–(14.7) · Morin §7.3.1, eq. (7.15) · H&S §7.3–7.4, eqs. (7.29), (7.32)*

^thm-b2-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Solve the energy equation of [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]] for $dr/dt = \pm\sqrt{2(E - U_{\text{eff}})/\mu}$ and separate variables; this is [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-3|Theorem §A4.7.3]] with $U_{\text{eff}}$ for $U$. Then $d\varphi = \dot\varphi\,dt = \dfrac{L}{\mu r^2}\,dt$ and $dt = dr\big/\sqrt{2(E - U_{\text{eff}})/\mu}$ give the second integral.

^der-b2-1-6

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]], [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-3|Theorem §A4.7.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Counting constants
> Two bodies in space need twelve initial data. The reduction uses all of them: six fix the uniform motion of the centre of mass, two the direction of $\vec L$ (the orbit plane), and the four constants of the plane problem are $E$, $L$ and the two integration constants in [[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-6|Theorem §B2.1.6]] (the time of a perihelion passage and the direction of the perihelion). H&S call the problem *integrable*: in the plane, $E$ and $L$ are two conserved quantities for two degrees of freedom. The integrals can be done in closed form only for a few potentials; the Kepler problem is the most important ([[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.1.1: A Satellite Kicked Outward
> A satellite of mass $m$ circles Earth (potential $U = -\alpha/r$; Earth taken as fixed, so $\mu = m$) with speed $v$. A rocket fires briefly and adds an outward *radial* velocity equal to $v$. Express everything through $m$, $\alpha$ and the angular momentum $L$.
>
> *Before.* $U_{\text{eff}} = -\alpha/r + L^2/(2mr^2)$ has its minimum where $\alpha/r^2 = L^2/(mr^3)$ ([[§B2.1 The Two-Body Problem and the Effective Potential#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]]):
>
> $$
> R = \frac{L^2}{m\alpha}, \qquad v = \frac{L}{mR} = \frac{\alpha}{L}, \qquad E_0 = U_{\text{eff}}(R) = -\frac{m\alpha^2}{L^2} + \frac{m\alpha^2}{2L^2} = -\frac{m\alpha^2}{2L^2} .
> $$
>
> *The kick.* The thrust is radial, so its torque about Earth's centre is zero and $L$ does not change; equivalently the tangential velocity $L/(mR)$ at the same $r = R$ is untouched. *After.* Only the radial kinetic energy is new:
>
> $$
> E_1 = E_0 + \tfrac12 mv^2 = -\frac{m\alpha^2}{2L^2} + \frac{m\alpha^2}{2L^2} = 0 .
> $$
>
> With $E = 0$, $U_{\text{eff}}(r) \le 0$ exactly for $r \ge L^2/(2m\alpha) = R/2$: the allowed region is unbounded and the satellite escapes. It arrives at infinity with zero speed; [[§B2.2 The Orbit Equation and Kepler Orbits#^thm-b2-2-2|Theorem §B2.2.2]] says the orbit is a parabola ($e = 1$) with perihelion $R/2$. Since $\dot r > 0$ at the kick, the satellite is already past that perihelion and never comes closer than $R$ again. A radial kick of the orbital speed is exactly the escape kick, because it doubles the kinetic energy, and $K = \lvert E_0\rvert$ on a circular orbit ([[§A8.2 Gravitational Energy and Orbits#^thm-a8-2-5|Theorem §A8.2.5]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 421, Midterm 2 (Fall 2021), Problem 1 (the exam writes $V = -k/r$ and $l$)*

^ex-b2-1-1

> [!example] Example §B2.1.2: A Force of Constant Magnitude
> A particle of mass $m$ feels $\vec F = -k\hat r$ with $k > 0$. In Cartesian components $\vec F = -k\,(x, y, z)/\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$. With $U(0) = 0$, $U(r) = kr$ and
>
> $$
> U_{\text{eff}}(r) = kr + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2} .
> $$
>
> *Circular orbit.* $U_{\text{eff}}' = k - L^2/(mr^3) = 0$ gives $r_c = (L^2/mk)^{1/3}$, speed $v_c = L/(mr_c) = (Lk/m^2)^{1/3}$ (check: $mv_c^2/r_c = k$), and, using $L^2 = mkr_c^3$,
>
> $$
> E_c = kr_c + \tfrac12 kr_c = \tfrac32\,kr_c = \tfrac32\Bigl(\frac{k^2L^2}{m}\Bigr)^{1/3} .
> $$
>
> *High energy, $E \gg E_c$.* Near the outer turning point the linear term dominates, $E \approx kr_{\max}$; near the inner one the barrier does, $E \approx L^2/(2mr_{\min}^2)$. So
>
> $$
> r_{\max} \approx \frac Ek, \quad v_{\text{aphelion}} = \frac{L}{mr_{\max}} \approx \frac{Lk}{mE}; \qquad r_{\min} \approx \frac{L}{\sqrt{2mE}}, \quad v_{\text{perihelion}} = \frac{L}{mr_{\min}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m}} .
> $$
>
> At perihelion almost all the energy is kinetic, at aphelion almost all potential. Each approximation neglects a term smaller by a factor of order $(E_c/E)^{3/2}$ or $(E_c/E)^3$, as substituting back shows.
>
> *Source: PHY 421, Midterm 2 (Fall 2015), Problem 1*

^ex-b2-1-2

> [!example] Example §B2.1.3: How Much Angular Momentum a Gaussian Well Can Hold
> A particle of mass $m$ moves in $U(r) = -V_0e^{-\lambda^2r^2}$. For which $L$ is there a circular orbit?
>
> $U_{\text{eff}}'(r) = 2V_0\lambda^2re^{-\lambda^2r^2} - L^2/(mr^3) = 0$ gives the radius implicitly:
>
> $$
> L^2 = 2mV_0\lambda^2\,r^4e^{-\lambda^2r^2} .
> $$
>
> The right side vanishes at $r \to 0$ and $r \to \infty$ and is largest where $4r^3 - 2\lambda^2r^5 = 0$, at $r_0^2 = 2/\lambda^2$. So circular orbits exist only for
>
> $$
> L^2 \le L_{\max}^2 = \frac{8mV_0}{\lambda^2e^2} ;
> $$
>
> below it there are two, the inner one at a minimum of $U_{\text{eff}}$ (stable) and the outer at a maximum (unstable). At $L = L_{\max}$ the two merge at $r_0$, where $U_{\text{eff}}(r_0) = V_0/e^2 > 0$.
>
> The tempting criterion "there is a well with $U_{\text{eff}} < 0$" is wrong: it gives $L^2 < 2mV_0/(\lambda^2e)$, too small, because for $L$ just below $L_{\max}$ the dip of $U_{\text{eff}}$ lies entirely above zero. A circular orbit needs a local extremum of $U_{\text{eff}}$, not a negative value. Beyond $L_{\max}$ the barrier wins at every $r$ and $U_{\text{eff}}$ decreases monotonically.
>
> *Source: Morin Problem 7.3*
