Level A derived the mirror equation, refraction at a spherical surface and the thin-lens equation one element at a time ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-5|Theorem §A5.1.5]], [[§A5.2 Images Formed by Refraction#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]], [[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^thm-a5-3-3|Theorem §A5.3.3]]), with a table of signs to keep straight ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]]), and noted that in the paraxial model ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^mod-a5-1-2|Model §A5.1.2]]) every element acts linearly on a ray ([[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^rem-a5-3-4|Remark: Ray matrices]]). This section builds that observation into a calculus. A ray is a two-component vector, each element a $2\times2$ matrix, a system the product of its matrices; imaging is the vanishing of one matrix element, and the level-A equations, with their signs, come out as corollaries. The sign convention of level A turns out to be exactly the one the matrices need, once distances are read as signed separations along the light.

## Ray vectors and ray matrices

> [!definition] Definition §B12.1.1: Ray Vector
> Let light travel along an optical axis $z$ in the direction of increasing $z$. At a reference plane $z = \text{const}$, a paraxial ray in a plane containing the axis is described by its **ray vector**
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix}\rho\\ \theta\end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> where $\rho$ is the height of the ray above the axis and $\theta$ the angle it makes with the axis, positive when the ray rises in the direction of travel ($\theta \approx d\rho/dz$). The paraxial model of [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^mod-a5-1-2|Model §A5.1.2]] is assumed: $\lvert\theta\rvert \ll 1$ and $\rho$ small compared with the radii of curvature.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.3, Fig. 10.5 · PHY 300, Worksheet 12*

^def-b12-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B12.1.2: Ray-Transfer Matrix
> An optical element or system between an input plane and an output plane is described by its **ray-transfer matrix** (ray matrix, ABCD matrix) $M$:
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix}\rho_2\\ \theta_2\end{pmatrix} = M\begin{pmatrix}\rho_1\\ \theta_1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad M = \begin{pmatrix}A & B\\ C & D\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> $A$ is a ratio of heights, $D$ of angles, $B$ a length and $C$ an inverse length.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.4, eq. (10.22) · PHY 300, Worksheet 12*

^def-b12-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §B12.1.3: Sign Convention for Ray Matrices
> - Heights $\rho$ are positive above the axis, angles $\theta$ positive for rising rays.
> - A distance $d$ in a translation is the signed separation $z_{\text{out}} - z_{\text{in}}$ along the direction of the light; a negative $d$ means going back against the light (used for virtual images and virtual objects).
> - The radius $r$ of a spherical surface is positive when its centre of curvature lies on the outgoing side, i.e. when the surface is convex toward the incoming light.
> - Mirrors are treated by **unfolding** the path: after each reflection the $z$ axis is reversed, so that the light again travels toward $+z$, and heights keep their sign. A concave mirror (centre of curvature on the side to which the light returns) then has $r > 0$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.1 ("Sign convention"), §10.4 (Table 10.1) and §10.5 (mirror as a lens)*

^def-b12-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Translating the sign convention of level A
> The University Physics convention of [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]] is the matrix convention, read as follows:
>
> | Level A | Ray matrices |
> | --- | --- |
> | object distance $d_o$ ($> 0$ for a real object) | the element is $d_o$ beyond the object plane: translation $T(d_o)$ from object to element |
> | image distance $d_i$ ($> 0$ for a real image beyond a lens or surface, in front of a mirror) | translation $T(d_i)$ from element to image along the outgoing light (unfolded for a mirror) |
> | $d_i < 0$, virtual image; $d_o < 0$, virtual object | the same translations with negative length: back along the light |
> | $R > 0$ if the centre of curvature is on the outgoing side | $r > 0$ in the same case |
> | $f > 0$ converging, $f < 0$ diverging | $C = -1/f$ |
> | heights $h_o$, $h_i$ above the axis | $\rho$ |
> | magnification $m = h_i/h_o$ | $m = A$ of the object-to-image matrix |
>
> So nothing has to be translated, only made explicit: level A's distances are the signed lengths of the translation matrices, and its rule "positive where the light really is" ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^rem-a5-1-3|Remark: One rule behind the table]]) is the statement that the translations run along the light. What the matrices add are signed angles, which level A never needed, and the unfolding of mirrors. Fowles uses the same convention for $s$, $s'$ and $r$. The "Cartesian" conventions of other texts ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^cau-a5-1-1|Caution: Signs in practice]]) instead give the object a coordinate $z_o < 0$ and write $1/s' - 1/s = 1/f$; in matrix terms their object distance is $-d_o$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.1–10.4 · PHY 300, Worksheet 12; the comparison table is assembled here*

^rem-b12-1-1

## Elementary matrices

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.1.1: Translation and Refraction Matrices
> In the paraxial approximation:
>
> $$
> T(d) = \begin{pmatrix}1 & d\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\ \text{(travel a distance } d\text{ in a homogeneous medium)}, \qquad P = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & \dfrac{n_1}{n_2}\end{pmatrix}\ \text{(plane interface)},
> $$
>
> $$
> R(r) = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\[2pt] \dfrac{1}{r}\Bigl(\dfrac{n_1}{n_2} - 1\Bigr) & \dfrac{n_1}{n_2}\end{pmatrix}\ \text{(spherical interface of radius } r\text{ from index } n_1 \text{ into } n_2) .
> $$
>
> The plane interface is the limit $r \to \infty$. The translation matrix is the same in every medium, since $\theta$ is the true angle.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.3–10.4, eqs. (10.18)–(10.20) and Table 10.1 · PHY 300, Worksheet 12 (questions 1–2)*

^thm-b12-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Translation.* A straight ray keeps its angle, and its height grows by $d\tan\theta_1 \approx d\,\theta_1$: $\rho_2 = \rho_1 + d\,\theta_1$, $\theta_2 = \theta_1$.
>
> *Spherical interface.* Put the vertex at $z = 0$ and the centre of curvature at $z = r$. In the paraxial approximation the ray meets the surface at its height $\rho$ (the sag $\rho^2/2r$ is second order), so $\rho_2 = \rho_1 = \rho$. The normal at that point runs toward the centre, along the direction $(r, -\rho)$, so it makes the angle $-\rho/r$ with the axis. The angles of incidence and refraction, measured from the normal with the same orientation as $\theta$, are $\theta_1 + \rho/r$ and $\theta_2 + \rho/r$. Snell's law for small angles ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|Law §A4.2.4]] with $\sin x \approx x$) gives
>
> $$
> n_1\Bigl(\theta_1 + \frac\rho r\Bigr) = n_2\Bigl(\theta_2 + \frac\rho r\Bigr) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_2 = \frac{n_1}{n_2}\,\theta_1 + \frac1r\Bigl(\frac{n_1}{n_2} - 1\Bigr)\rho .
> $$
>
> For $r \to \infty$ this is $\theta_2 = (n_1/n_2)\theta_1$, the plane interface.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.1.2: Thin Lens and Spherical Mirror
> A thin lens of index $n$ in air, with surface radii $r_1$ (met first) and $r_2$, and a spherical mirror of radius $r$ (unfolded) have the matrices
>
> $$
> L(f) = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ -\dfrac1f & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \frac1f = (n - 1)\Bigl(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\Bigr) \ \text{(lens)}, \qquad f = \frac r2 \ \text{(mirror)} .
> $$
>
> A thin lens or mirror leaves the height unchanged and bends every ray by $-\rho/f$, proportional to its height. In a medium of index $n_m$ the lens has $1/f = (n/n_m - 1)(1/r_1 - 1/r_2)$, the lensmaker's equation of [[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^thm-a5-3-3|Theorem §A5.3.3]]; the mirror's $f = r/2$ is [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-3|Theorem §A5.1.3]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.3–10.4, eq. (10.21) and Table 10.1 · PHY 300, Worksheet 12 (question 2b)*

^thm-b12-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Lens.* The light meets the first surface (air into glass), then, after a negligible thickness, the second (glass into air). By [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-3|Theorem §B12.1.3]] the lens is the product in reverse order:
>
> $$
> R_2R_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ \frac{n - 1}{r_2} & n\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ \frac{1}{r_1}\bigl(\frac1n - 1\bigr) & \frac1n\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ \frac{n - 1}{r_2} - \frac{n - 1}{r_1} & 1\end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> using [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-1|Theorem §B12.1.1]] with $(n_1, n_2) = (1, n)$ and $(n, 1)$. The lower-left element is $-(n - 1)(1/r_1 - 1/r_2) = -1/f$. In a medium, replace $1$ by $n_m$ throughout; the ratios $n/n_m$ appear.
>
> *Mirror.* Put the vertex at $z = 0$, the centre of a concave mirror at $z = -r$ (on the side of the incoming light). At height $\rho$ the unit normal pointing back toward the light is $\hat n \approx (-1, -\rho/r)$. The reflected direction of $(1, \theta_1)$ is $(1, \theta_1) - 2\bigl[(1, \theta_1)\cdot\hat n\bigr]\hat n \approx (1, \theta_1) + 2\hat n = (-1, \theta_1 - 2\rho/r)$ to first order ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]). The reflected ray travels toward $-z$ and changes height by $\theta_1 - 2\rho/r$ per unit distance travelled; after unfolding ($z \to -z$, heights kept) its angle is $\theta_2 = \theta_1 - 2\rho/r$, while $\rho_2 = \rho$. This is $L(f)$ with $f = r/2$.

## Systems

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.1.3: Composition and Determinant
> A system of elements with matrices $M_1, M_2, \dots, M_N$, met by the light in this order, has the matrix
>
> $$
> M = M_N\cdots M_2M_1 ,
> $$
>
> and its determinant is the ratio of the refractive indices of the first and last media:
>
> $$
> \det M = AD - BC = \frac{n_{\text{in}}}{n_{\text{out}}} .
> $$
>
> In particular $\det M = 1$ for any system that begins and ends in the same medium.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.4, eq. (10.23)*

^thm-b12-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The output vector of each element is the input of the next, so the vector after the system is $M_N(\cdots(M_2(M_1v)))$, which is $(M_N\cdots M_1)v$ because composition of linear maps is matrix multiplication ([[§9 Matrices#^ladr-3-41|LADR Def. 3.41]]). The determinant is multiplicative ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]). Each elementary matrix has determinant $1$ (translation, thin lens, mirror) or $n_1/n_2$ (an interface from $n_1$ to $n_2$); along the system the interfaces form a chain $n_{\text{in}} \to n' \to n'' \to \dots \to n_{\text{out}}$, and the product of the ratios telescopes to $n_{\text{in}}/n_{\text{out}}$.

^der-b12-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-1|Theorem §B12.1.1]], [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-2|Theorem §B12.1.2]], [[§9 Matrices#^ladr-3-41|LADR Def. 3.41]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]

> [!caution] Caution: Order, and other conventions
> - The matrices multiply from right to left in the order the light meets them; $M_1M_2 \neq M_2M_1$ in general (a lens followed by a gap is not a gap followed by a lens).
> - Some texts use the **reduced angle** $n\theta$ in place of $\theta$. Then every interface matrix has determinant $1$, a translation through a medium of index $n$ becomes $\begin{pmatrix}1 & d/n\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, and $\det M = 1$ always. The PHY 300 formula sheet mixes the two: its "inside a dielectric, $\begin{pmatrix}1 & t/n\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$" belongs to the reduced-angle convention, or is the matrix of a whole plane-parallel plate including both of its faces ([[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^ex-b12-1-1|Example §B12.1.1]]). With true angles and the interface matrices above, propagation inside glass is $T(t)$. The same sheet writes the ray vector as $(\theta, \rho)$, which does not match its own lens matrix; the order is $(\rho, \theta)$, as in Worksheet 12 and Fowles.
> - Fowles's Table 10.1 says "convex $r > 0$": convex as seen by the incoming light, in agreement with [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^def-b12-1-3|Def. §B12.1.3]].

^cau-b12-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.1.4: Imaging Condition and Magnification
> Let $M$ be the matrix from a plane 1 to a plane 2. All rays leaving a point of plane 1 pass through a single point of plane 2, so that plane 2 carries an image of plane 1, if and only if
>
> $$
> B = 0 .
> $$
>
> Then the lateral magnification is $m = \rho_2/\rho_1 = A$, rays from an axial object point leave with $\theta_2 = D\,\theta_1$, and $AD = n_{\text{in}}/n_{\text{out}}$: the angular magnification is $D = (n_{\text{in}}/n_{\text{out}})/m$. A magnified image is seen through a narrower cone of rays.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 12 (question 3: "the position of the rays coming from the object should be independent of their initial angle") · Fowles §10.4*

^thm-b12-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\rho_2 = A\rho_1 + B\theta_1$ is independent of the angle $\theta_1$ for every $\rho_1$ exactly when $B = 0$; then $\rho_2 = A\rho_1$. For $\rho_1 = 0$, $\theta_2 = C\cdot0 + D\theta_1$. With $B = 0$, $\det M = AD$, which is $n_{\text{in}}/n_{\text{out}}$ by [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-3|Theorem §B12.1.3]].

^der-b12-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^def-b12-1-2|Def. §B12.1.2]], [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-3|Theorem §B12.1.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.1.5: The Image Equations of Level A
> With object distance $s$ and image distance $s'$ in the sense of [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^rem-b12-1-1|Remark: Translating the sign convention of level A]]:
> - a thin lens images when $\dfrac1s + \dfrac1{s'} = \dfrac1f$, with $m = -\dfrac{s'}{s}$;
> - a spherical mirror when $\dfrac1s + \dfrac1{s'} = \dfrac2r$, with $m = -\dfrac{s'}{s}$;
> - a spherical refracting surface when $\dfrac{n_1}{s} + \dfrac{n_2}{s'} = \dfrac{n_2 - n_1}{r}$, with $m = -\dfrac{n_1s'}{n_2s}$.
>
> These are [[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^thm-a5-3-3|Theorem §A5.3.3]], [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-5|Theorem §A5.1.5]] and [[§A5.2 Images Formed by Refraction#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]], with the signs of [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]]: the sign rules of level A are consequences of the algebra.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.1–10.2, eqs. (10.3)–(10.7) · PHY 300, Worksheet 12 (question 3)*

^thm-b12-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Lens or mirror.* With $L(f)$ of [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-2|Theorem §B12.1.2]],
>
> $$
> T(s')\,L(f)\,T(s) = \begin{pmatrix}1 - \frac{s'}{f} & s + s' - \frac{ss'}{f}\\[2pt] -\frac1f & 1 - \frac sf\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> By [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-4|Theorem §B12.1.4]] the image condition $B = 0$ is $s + s' = ss'/f$; dividing by $ss'$ gives $1/s + 1/s' = 1/f$ ($= 2/r$ for the mirror). Then $m = A = 1 - s'/f = 1 - s'(1/s + 1/s') = -s'/s$.
>
> *Refracting surface.* With $c = (n_1/n_2 - 1)/r$ and $R(r)$ of [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-1|Theorem §B12.1.1]],
>
> $$
> T(s')\,R(r)\,T(s) = \begin{pmatrix}1 + cs' & s + \frac{n_1}{n_2}s' + css'\\[2pt] c & cs + \frac{n_1}{n_2}\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> $B = 0$ divided by $ss'$ reads $\frac1{s'} + \frac{n_1}{n_2}\frac1s + c = 0$; multiplying by $n_2$ gives $\frac{n_1}{s} + \frac{n_2}{s'} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{r}$. Then $m = A = 1 + cs' = 1 + s'\bigl(-\frac1{s'} - \frac{n_1}{n_2s}\bigr) = -\frac{n_1s'}{n_2s}$.

^der-b12-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-1|Theorem §B12.1.1]], [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-2|Theorem §B12.1.2]], [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-4|Theorem §B12.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: The layer change
> At level A the thin-lens equation was derived by stacking two surfaces and letting the thickness go to zero, and the signs were a convention to be learned. Here the equation is the single condition $B = 0$ on a product of matrices, the magnification is a matrix element, and the signs need no separate rule: a negative $s'$ is a translation backward, a negative $f$ a matrix that bends rays away from the axis. The same three lines handle any system, however many elements, which is where the method pays: [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators|§B12.2]]. The limits are those of the paraxial model: no aberrations ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^rem-a5-1-2|Remark: Aberrations]]), which Fowles treats separately (spherical aberration growing as $h^2$, chromatic aberration from the dispersion of $n$).
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.1–10.2*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B12.1.1: A Plane-Parallel Plate
> A glass plate of index $n$ and thickness $t$ in air, crossed from left to right: by [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-3|Theorem §B12.1.3]] and [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-1|Theorem §B12.1.1]],
>
> $$
> M = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & n\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & t\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & \frac1n\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & \frac tn\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} = T\Bigl(\frac tn\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> The plate acts like a gap of air of thickness $t/n$: seen through it, an object appears closer by $t - t/n = t(1 - 1/n)$. For an object lying on the bottom of the plate (or of a pool of depth $t$), the ray vectors leaving the top surface are those of an object at depth $t/n$ in air, the apparent depth of [[§A5.2 Images Formed by Refraction#^thm-a5-2-1|Theorem §A5.2.1]]. A $6\ \mathrm{mm}$ window of glass with $n = 1.5$ shifts what is behind it by $2\ \mathrm{mm}$ toward the viewer. This net matrix is the "inside a dielectric" entry of the PHY 300 formula sheet ([[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^cau-b12-1-1|Caution: Order, and other conventions]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.3–10.4 (plane interface and translation matrices) · PHY 300, Final practice exam (formula sheet); the product is computed here*

^ex-b12-1-1

> [!example] Example §B12.1.2: Imaging into a Glass Rod
> A long glass rod ($n_2 = 1.50$) ends in a convex hemispherical surface of radius $r = 2.0\ \mathrm{cm}$; an object stands $s = 8.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ in front of it in air. The surface matrix ([[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-1|Theorem §B12.1.1]]) has $c = (1/1.5 - 1)/2.0\ \mathrm{cm} = -\frac16\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ and $D$-element $\frac23$. The image condition of [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-5|Theorem §B12.1.5]], $\frac{1}{8.0} + \frac{1.5}{s'} = \frac{0.5}{2.0}$, gives $s' = 12\ \mathrm{cm}$ inside the rod, and then
>
> $$
> R = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ -\frac16 & \frac23\end{pmatrix}, \qquad T(12)\,R\,T(8) = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 12\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 8\\ -\frac16 & -\frac23\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-1 & 0\\ -\frac16 & -\frac23\end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{lengths in cm}).
> $$
>
> $B = 0$ confirms the image; $m = A = -1$, a real, inverted image of the same size (level A: $m = -n_1s'/(n_2s) = -12/12$); $D = -\frac23$, so the cone of rays from the object point is narrowed to two thirds inside the glass; and $\det M = AD = \frac23 = n_1/n_2$, as [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-3|Theorem §B12.1.3]] requires. Equal size does not mean equal angles: entering a denser medium compresses the angles by $n_1/n_2$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.1 and §10.4 (refracting surface and its matrix); numbers chosen here*

^ex-b12-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Matrix multiplication and its associativity: [[§9 Matrices#^ladr-3-41|LADR Def. 3.41]]; the determinant: [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]].
> - The same $2\times2$ calculus describes polarization (Jones matrices, [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus|§B8.2]]) and thin-film stacks (transfer matrices, [[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^def-b10-5-1|Def. §B10.5.1]]): in each case a linear map per element and a product for the system, taken in the reverse of the order in which the light meets the elements.
> - $\det M = 1$ in a uniform medium is the paraxial form of the conservation of étendue (phase-space area of a bundle of rays), the optical counterpart of Liouville's theorem in mechanics ([[§B7.2 Phase Space and Liouville's Theorem#^thm-b7-2-3|CM Theorem §B7.2.3]]).
