*Sources: Fowles, Introduction to Modern Optics, §10.2, §10.4–10.5 · PHY 300 (Mihaly, Fall 2024).*

Level A handled systems of several lenses one image at a time ([[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^rem-a5-3-2|Remark: Systems of several elements]]) and quoted the focal length of two separated lenses, deferring the question of where to measure distances from ([[§A5.4 The Eye and the Camera#^rem-a5-4-1|Remark: Lenses with a gap]]). With ray matrices ([[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses|§B12.1]]) any system is one matrix, and its four elements fix everything an observer outside can measure: the focal length, the focal points and the principal planes, from which the thin-lens equation holds again. The section applies this to the thick lens, to separated lenses and to the telescope, and then to systems that repeat a unit many times: a row of lenses guiding a beam, or two mirrors bouncing light back and forth. There the question is whether rays stay near the axis forever, and the answer, an eigenvalue condition on the unit matrix, is the stability criterion of laser resonators.

## Cardinal points of a system

> [!definition] Definition §B12.2.1: Focal Points, Principal Planes, Effective Focal Length
> Consider a system in a single medium (same index before and after), with input and output reference planes. Rays entering parallel to the axis leave crossing the axis at the **second focal point** $F'$; extend each such exiting ray backward and the incoming ray forward: they meet in a plane perpendicular to the axis, the **second principal plane** $H'$. Rays leaving parallel to the axis came from the **first focal point** $F$; the corresponding construction defines the **first principal plane** $H$. The **effective focal length** $f$ is the distance from $H'$ to $F'$. $F, F', H, H'$ are the **cardinal points** of the system.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.2 (thick lens, Fig. 10.3) · PHY 300, Worksheet 12 (question 4) and Final practice exam (problem 6)*

^def-b12-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.2.1: Cardinal Points from the System Matrix
> Let $M = \begin{pmatrix}A & B\\ C & D\end{pmatrix}$ be the matrix from the input to the output plane of a system with $\det M = 1$ and $C \neq 0$. Then, with all positions measured in the direction of the light:
>
> $$
> f = -\frac{1}{C}, \qquad z_{F'} = z_{\text{out}} + Af, \qquad z_{H'} = z_{\text{out}} + (A - 1)f, \qquad z_F = z_{\text{in}} - Df, \qquad z_H = z_{\text{in}} + (1 - D)f .
> $$
>
> The matrix from $H$ to $H'$ is that of a thin lens, $L(f)$. Hence object and image distances measured from $H$ and from $H'$ obey the thin-lens equation $1/s + 1/s' = 1/f$, with $m = -s'/s$: every such system acts like a thin lens of focal length $f$ whose single plane has been split into the pair $H$, $H'$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.2 (the thick lens "with distances measured from the principal planes"); the general formulas are derived here from Fowles §10.4*

^thm-b12-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Focal points.* A ray $(\rho, 0)$ entering parallel leaves as $(A\rho, C\rho)$ and crosses the axis a distance $x$ later when $A\rho + xC\rho = 0$, i.e. $x = -A/C = Af$, the same for all $\rho$. It exits at angle $C\rho = -\rho/f$; following it back by $f$ from $F'$ brings it to height $\rho$, the height of the incoming ray, so $H'$ lies $f$ before $F'$. For $F$: a ray leaving the axis a distance $x$ before the input plane, $T(x)(0, \theta) = (x\theta, \theta)$, exits with angle $C x\theta + D\theta$, which vanishes for $x = -D/C = Df$; the same argument backward gives $z_H = z_F + f$.
>
> *Thin lens between $H$ and $H'$.* The matrix from $H$ to $H'$ is $T(p')\,M\,T(-p)$ with $p = (1 - D)f$ and $p' = (A - 1)f$. First $MT(-p) = \begin{pmatrix}A & B - Ap\\ C & D - Cp\end{pmatrix}$, and $D - Cp = D + (1 - D) = 1$. Then
>
> $$
> T(p')\,M\,T(-p) = \begin{pmatrix}A + p'C & B - Ap + p'\\ C & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad A + p'C = A - (A - 1) = 1,
> $$
>
> $$
> B - Ap + p' = B + f\bigl(-A + AD + A - 1\bigr) = B + f(AD - 1) = B + fBC = 0,
> $$
>
> using $AD - BC = 1$ ([[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-3|Theorem §B12.1.3]]) and $fC = -1$. So the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ -1/f & 1\end{pmatrix}$, and the image equation follows exactly as for a thin lens ([[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-5|Theorem §B12.1.5]]).

*Uses:* [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^def-b12-2-1|Def. §B12.2.1]], [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-3|Theorem §B12.1.3]], [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-5|Theorem §B12.1.5]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.2.2: Thick Lens
> A lens of index $n$ in air, with radii $r_1$, $r_2$ and thickness $t$ between its vertices, has
>
> $$
> \frac1f = (n - 1)\left[\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2} + \frac{(n - 1)\,t}{n\,r_1r_2}\right],
> $$
>
> and its principal planes lie at
>
> $$
> d_1 = \frac{f\,t\,(1 - n)}{n\,r_2} \ \text{ from the first vertex (to } H\text{)}, \qquad d_2 = \frac{f\,t\,(1 - n)}{n\,r_1} \ \text{ from the second vertex (to } H'\text{)},
> $$
>
> both counted positive in the direction of the light. For a biconvex lens $d_1 > 0$ and $d_2 < 0$: both planes lie inside the glass. As $t \to 0$ this becomes the lensmaker's equation with $H = H'$ at the lens.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.2, eqs. (10.11)–(10.12), and Problem 10.5 · PHY 300, Worksheet 12 (question 4) and Final practice exam (formula sheet)*

^thm-b12-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The light meets the first surface (air to glass), travels $t$, and meets the second (glass to air). With [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-1|Theorem §B12.1.1]],
>
> $$
> M = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ \frac{n - 1}{r_2} & n\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & t\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ \frac{1 - n}{n r_1} & \frac1n\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> The product of the last two is $\begin{pmatrix}1 - \frac{(n - 1)t}{nr_1} & \frac tn\\ \frac{1 - n}{nr_1} & \frac1n\end{pmatrix}$, and multiplying by the first,
>
> $$
> A = 1 - \frac{(n - 1)t}{nr_1}, \qquad B = \frac tn, \qquad D = 1 + \frac{(n - 1)t}{nr_2},
> $$
>
> $$
> C = \frac{n - 1}{r_2}\Bigl(1 - \frac{(n - 1)t}{nr_1}\Bigr) - \frac{n - 1}{r_1} = -(n - 1)\left[\frac1{r_1} - \frac1{r_2} + \frac{(n - 1)t}{nr_1r_2}\right] .
> $$
>
> $f = -1/C$ is the first formula. By [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-1|Theorem §B12.2.1]], $H$ lies $(1 - D)f = -\frac{(n - 1)t}{nr_2}f = d_1$ after the first vertex, and $H'$ lies $(A - 1)f = -\frac{(n - 1)t}{nr_1}f = d_2$ after the second.

^der-b12-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-1|Theorem §B12.1.1]], [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-3|Theorem §B12.1.3]], [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-1|Theorem §B12.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: A glass sphere as a lens
> A ball of radius $r$ and index $n$ is a thick lens with $r_1 = r$, $r_2 = -r$, $t = 2r$. By [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-2|Theorem §B12.2.2]],
>
> $$
> \frac1f = (n - 1)\Bigl[\frac2r - \frac{2(n - 1)}{nr}\Bigr] = \frac{2(n - 1)}{nr}, \qquad f = \frac{nr}{2(n - 1)},
> $$
>
> $$
> d_1 = \frac{f(2r)(1 - n)}{n(-r)} = \frac{2(n - 1)}{n}f = r, \qquad d_2 = \frac{f(2r)(1 - n)}{nr} = -r :
> $$
>
> both principal planes pass through the centre, as symmetry suggests. The focus lies $f - r = r(2 - n)/(2(n - 1))$ beyond the back surface: $0.5r$ for $n = 1.5$, on the surface itself for $n = 2$. Small glass balls are used this way to couple light into optical fibres.
>
> *Source: Fowles, Problem 10.2*

^rem-b12-2-1

> [!example] Example §B12.2.1: Two Separated Lenses; the Telephoto Pair
> Thin lenses of focal lengths $f_1$ and $f_2$ a distance $d$ apart (the light meets $f_1$ first) have
>
> $$
> M = L(f_2)\,T(d)\,L(f_1) = \begin{pmatrix}1 - \frac{d}{f_1} & d\\[2pt] -\Bigl(\frac{1}{f_1} + \frac{1}{f_2} - \frac{d}{f_1f_2}\Bigr) & 1 - \frac{d}{f_2}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \frac1f = \frac1{f_1} + \frac1{f_2} - \frac{d}{f_1f_2} .
> $$
>
> For $d = 0$ the powers add ([[§A5.4 The Eye and the Camera#^thm-a5-4-2|Theorem §A5.4.2]]). By [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-1|Theorem §B12.2.1]] the focus lies $Af = f(1 - d/f_1)$ behind the second lens, $H'$ at $(A - 1)f = -fd/f_1$ from it, and $H$ at $(1 - D)f = fd/f_2$ from the first lens.
>
> Take $f_1 = +10\ \mathrm{cm}$, $f_2 = -10\ \mathrm{cm}$, $d = 5\ \mathrm{cm}$: $1/f = 0.1 - 0.1 + 0.05\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, so $f = 20\ \mathrm{cm}$; $F'$ is $20(1 - 0.5) = 10\ \mathrm{cm}$ behind the second lens, and $H'$ is $10\ \mathrm{cm}$ in front of it, $5\ \mathrm{cm}$ in front of the first lens (figure). The pair forms images like a $20\ \mathrm{cm}$ lens but measures only $15\ \mathrm{cm}$ from the front lens to the focal plane: a **telephoto** combination, whose second principal plane lies outside the system.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Final practice exam (problem 6) · Fowles §10.2, eq. (10.10); numbers chosen here*

> [!example] Example §B12.2.2: The Telescope as an Afocal System
> Two converging lenses with $d = f_1 + f_2$ give $C = -(f_1 + f_2 - d)/(f_1f_2) = 0$: no focal length at all. The matrix is
>
> $$
> M = \begin{pmatrix}-\dfrac{f_2}{f_1} & f_1 + f_2\\ 0 & -\dfrac{f_1}{f_2}\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> A parallel bundle at angle $\theta_1$, rays $(\rho, \theta_1)$ for all $\rho$, leaves as a parallel bundle at $\theta_2 = D\theta_1 = -(f_1/f_2)\,\theta_1$: the angular magnification of [[§A5.5 Magnifiers, Microscopes and Telescopes#^thm-a5-5-3|Theorem §A5.5.3]] with $f_1 = f_{\text{obj}}$, $f_2 = f_{\text{eye}}$, now read off one matrix element. Its width is multiplied by $A = -f_2/f_1$, and $AD = 1$: what the telescope gains in angle it loses in beam width. The PHY 300 telescope ($f_1 = 20\ \mathrm{cm}$, $f_2 = 10\ \mathrm{cm}$, $d = 30\ \mathrm{cm}$) has $D = -2$, $A = -\frac12$. Used backwards it is a beam expander, as in front of a laser.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 12 (measurements 5–6) · Fowles §10.4; the afocal form is derived here*

## Periodic systems

> [!definition] Definition §B12.2.2: Periodic System; Lens Waveguide; Optical Resonator
> A **periodic system** repeats one unit with matrix $M$ (same medium at both ends, so $\det M = 1$). A **lens waveguide** is a row of lenses, a unit being a lens and the gap to the next. An **optical resonator** is a pair of facing mirrors; unfolded ([[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^def-b12-1-3|Def. §B12.1.3]]), a ray bouncing between them is a ray through a periodic lens waveguide whose unit is one round trip. A ray is **confined** if its height stays bounded for any number of units; the system is **stable** if every ray is confined.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.5, Fig. 10.7*

^def-b12-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.2.3: Stability of a Periodic System
> Let the unit matrix $M$ have $\det M = 1$ and half-trace $a = \frac12(A + D)$. The ray vectors $v_N = M^Nv_0$ after $N$ units satisfy
>
> $$
> v_{N+1} = 2a\,v_N - v_{N-1},
> $$
>
> and the eigenvalues of $M$ are $\lambda = a \pm\sqrt{a^2 - 1}$, with $\lambda_+\lambda_- = 1$.
> - If $\lvert a\rvert < 1$, then $\lambda = e^{\pm i\phi}$ with $\cos\phi = a$, and every ray is confined: its heights at successive units lie on a sampled sinusoid, $\rho_N = \hat\rho\sin(N\phi + \delta)$.
> - If $\lvert a\rvert > 1$, the eigenvalues are real with $\lvert\lambda_+\rvert > 1$, and every ray except those along one eigenvector grows like $\lvert\lambda_+\rvert^N$: the system is unstable.
>
> The stability condition is therefore $-1 < \frac12(A + D) < 1$ (the boundary $\lvert a\rvert = 1$ is marginal).
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.5, eqs. (10.25)–(10.30)*

^thm-b12-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The characteristic polynomial of a $2\times2$ matrix is $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}M)\lambda + \det M = \lambda^2 - 2a\lambda + 1$ ([[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-65|LADR Thm. 9.65]]); its roots are the eigenvalues ([[§14 Invariant Subspaces#^ladr-5-5|LADR Def. 5.5]]), and their product is $\det M = 1$. By Cayley–Hamilton ([[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-64|LADR Thm. 9.64]]), $M^2 = 2aM - I$; applied to $M^{N-1}v_0$ this is the recurrence. Each component of $v_N$, in particular $\rho_N$, obeys the scalar recurrence $x_{N+1} = 2a\,x_N - x_{N-1}$, whose solutions form a two-dimensional space fixed by $x_0$, $x_1$.
> - If $a = \cos\phi$ with $0 < \phi < \pi$, both $\cos N\phi$ and $\sin N\phi$ are solutions, because $\cos(N + 1)\phi + \cos(N - 1)\phi = 2\cos\phi\cos N\phi$ (and likewise for the sine). They are independent, so every solution is a combination of them, $\hat\rho\sin(N\phi + \delta)$, bounded by $\hat\rho$.
> - If $\lvert a\rvert > 1$, $\lambda_\pm^N$ are two independent solutions with $\lvert\lambda_+\rvert > 1 > \lvert\lambda_-\rvert$; a solution stays bounded only if its $\lambda_+^N$ component is zero, which singles out one ray direction (the eigenvector of $\lambda_-$). All other rays run away from the axis.

*Uses:* [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^def-b12-2-2|Def. §B12.2.2]], [[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-65|LADR Thm. 9.65]], [[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-64|LADR Thm. 9.64]], [[§14 Invariant Subspaces#^ladr-5-5|LADR Def. 5.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: Eigenrays
> Fowles phrases the problem as a search for rays that come back as a multiple of themselves, $Mv = \lambda v$, the same eigenvalue problem as for the eigenstates of Jones matrices ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-4|Theorem §B8.2.4]]). In the stable case the eigenvalues are complex, so no real ray is reproduced exactly; instead every ray is a combination of two complex eigenrays with $\lvert\lambda\rvert = 1$, and its height oscillates. This is the discrete version of a ray in a graded-index fibre, which oscillates sinusoidally about the axis, and of a pendulum: $\phi$ plays the role of $\omega\,\Delta t$. In the unstable case the eigenvalues are real, and the motion is like that of an inverted pendulum.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.5*

^rem-b12-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.2.4: Symmetric Lens Waveguide and Symmetric Resonator
> Identical thin lenses of focal length $f$ spaced $d$ apart guide rays stably if and only if
>
> $$
> 0 < d < 4f .
> $$
>
> A resonator of two identical spherical mirrors of radius $r$ (focal length $r/2$) a distance $d$ apart is stable if and only if $0 < d < 2r$. The **confocal** arrangement $d = 2f = r$ lies in the middle of the stable range: there $\phi = \pi/2$, and every ray retraces its path after four units (two round trips).
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.5, eqs. (10.24) and (10.27)–(10.32)*

^thm-b12-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A unit is a gap followed by a lens: $M = L(f)\,T(d) = \begin{pmatrix}1 & d\\ -\frac1f & 1 - \frac df\end{pmatrix}$, with $\det M = 1$ and $a = \frac12(2 - d/f) = 1 - \frac{d}{2f}$. By [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-3|Theorem §B12.2.3]] stability means $-1 < 1 - d/(2f) < 1$, i.e. $0 < d/(2f) < 2$: $f > 0$ and $0 < d < 4f$. For the resonator the unfolded unit is a gap and a mirror, the same matrix with $f = r/2$ ([[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-2|Theorem §B12.1.2]]); so $0 < d < 2r$. At $d = 2f$, $a = 0$, $\phi = \pi/2$, and $M^4 = I$ because $M^2 = 2aM - I = -I$.

^der-b12-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-3|Theorem §B12.2.3]], [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-2|Theorem §B12.1.2]], [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-3|Theorem §B12.1.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B12.2.5: Stability of a Two-Mirror Resonator
> Two spherical mirrors of radii $r_1$, $r_2$ (positive when concave toward the other mirror) a distance $d$ apart form a stable resonator if
>
> $$
> 0 < g_1g_2 < 1, \qquad g_i = 1 - \frac{d}{r_i} = 1 - \frac{d}{2f_i},
> $$
>
> and an unstable one if $g_1g_2 < 0$ or $g_1g_2 > 1$. The boundary $g_1g_2 = 0$ or $1$ is marginal; there rays in general drift away slowly, with the exception of the symmetric confocal resonator $g_1 = g_2 = 0$, in which every ray retraces itself. The same conditions hold for a lens waveguide of alternating lenses $f_1$, $f_2$, equally spaced by $d$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.5, eqs. (10.33)–(10.34), and Problem 10.6*

^thm-b12-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Unfold one round trip, starting just after mirror 1: gap, mirror 2, gap, mirror 1. With $L_i = L(f_i)$, $f_i = r_i/2$,
>
> $$
> M = L_1T(d)\,L_2T(d), \qquad L_iT(d) = \begin{pmatrix}1 & d\\ -\frac1{f_i} & 1 - \frac d{f_i}\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> Multiplying out the two diagonal elements of the product,
>
> $$
> A + D = \Bigl(1 - \frac{d}{f_2}\Bigr) - \frac{d}{f_1} + \Bigl(1 - \frac{d}{f_1}\Bigr)\Bigl(1 - \frac{d}{f_2}\Bigr) = 2 - \frac{2d}{f_1} - \frac{2d}{f_2} + \frac{d^2}{f_1f_2} .
> $$
>
> Since $4g_1g_2 = 4\bigl(1 - \frac{d}{2f_1}\bigr)\bigl(1 - \frac{d}{2f_2}\bigr) = 4 - \frac{2d}{f_1} - \frac{2d}{f_2} + \frac{d^2}{f_1f_2}$, the half-trace is $a = 2g_1g_2 - 1$. [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-3|Theorem §B12.2.3]]: $-1 < 2g_1g_2 - 1 < 1$, i.e. $0 < g_1g_2 < 1$. For identical mirrors, $g_1 = g_2 = g = 1 - d/r$ and the condition $0 < g^2 < 1$ is $0 < d < 2r$ with $d \neq r$. At the confocal point $d = r$ ($g = 0$) the round trip is two half-units, each with $a = 0$, so by [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-4|Theorem §B12.2.4]] the round-trip matrix is $-I$: every ray retraces itself after two round trips. That point lies on the boundary $a = -1$ of the criterion, but all rays stay bounded. Elsewhere on the boundary, $a = \pm1$ is a double eigenvalue and, unless $M = \pm I$, $M = \pm(I + K)$ with $K \neq 0$ and $K^2 = (M \mp I)^2 = 0$ (Cayley–Hamilton with $a = \pm1$), so $M^N = (\pm1)^N(I + NK)$: the heights of most rays grow in proportion to $N$, as for the tilted ray between plane mirrors.

*Uses:* [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-3|Theorem §B12.2.3]], [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-4|Theorem §B12.2.4]], [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-2|Theorem §B12.1.2]]

*The stability diagram of a two-mirror resonator, from Theorem §B12.2.5. Each resonator is a point $(g_1, g_2)$, $g_i = 1 - d/r_i$; it is stable in the shaded region between the axes and the hyperbola $g_1g_2 = 1$. The classic symmetric and half-symmetric designs (red) all sit on the boundary; the resonator of Example §B12.2.3 (orange) is safely inside.*

> [!remark] Remark: Where real resonators sit
> - **Plane-parallel** ($r_1 = r_2 = \infty$, $g_1 = g_2 = 1$): the Fabry–Perot of [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^rem-b10-4-2|Remark: The Fabry–Perot as a resonator]]. Marginal: a ray tilted by $\theta$ walks off sideways by $2d\theta$ per round trip, and a slight misalignment makes it unstable.
> - **Confocal** ($d = r_1 = r_2$, the origin): least sensitive to the mirror spacing for a given mirror size, and often used (Fowles).
> - **Concentric** ($d = 2r$, $g_1 = g_2 = -1$) and **hemispherical** (one flat mirror and one of radius $r = d$, $g_1 = 1$, $g_2 = 0$) are also on the boundary. Practical lasers use designs slightly inside the stable region near these points.
>
> The resonator is the feedback element of a laser ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^mod-a5-4-8|QM Model §A5.4.8]]): light must pass the gain medium many times, and only a stable resonator keeps it near the axis while it does. Ray optics ignores diffraction; the actual modes of a stable resonator are Gaussian beams whose waist and curvature are fixed by the same matrices (Chapter C2, planned).
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.5 and Problem 10.7*

^rem-b12-2-3

> [!example] Example §B12.2.3: A Half-Symmetric Laser Resonator
> A helium–neon laser has a flat output mirror and a concave mirror of radius $r_2 = 1.0\ \mathrm m$, a distance $d = 0.30\ \mathrm m$ apart. Then $g_1 = 1$, $g_2 = 1 - 0.30/1.0 = 0.70$, and $g_1g_2 = 0.70$ lies in $(0, 1)$: stable ([[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-5|Theorem §B12.2.5]]). The half-trace of the round trip is $a = 2(0.70) - 1 = 0.40$, so $\phi = \arccos0.40 = 66.4^\circ$: the height at which a ray strikes the flat mirror follows $\hat\rho\sin(N\cdot66.4^\circ + \delta)$ over successive round trips ([[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-3|Theorem §B12.2.3]]), never leaving the band $\lvert\rho\rvert \le \hat\rho$, and the pattern almost repeats every $360/66.4 \approx 5.4$ round trips. Lengthening the tube keeps it stable up to $d = 1.0\ \mathrm m$, where $g_2 = 0$ (the hemispherical point); beyond, $g_1g_2 < 0$ and rays escape.
>
> *Source: Fowles §10.5 (stability criterion); numbers chosen here*
