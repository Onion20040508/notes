A thin film splits light into two beams whose path difference is fixed by the film ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference|§A6.3]]). An interferometer does the same on purpose, with the two paths under the experimenter's control. Because one fringe corresponds to a change of half a wavelength in a mirror's position, counting fringes measures lengths, and small changes of refractive index, to a fraction of a wavelength of light.

## The instrument

> [!definition] Definition §A6.4.1: Interferometer; Michelson Interferometer
> An **interferometer** is an instrument that uses the interference of waves to make measurements. In the **Michelson interferometer** a half-silvered mirror $M$ (the beam splitter) divides light from a monochromatic extended source $S$ of vacuum wavelength $\lambda_0$ into two beams. One travels to a movable plane mirror $M_1$, a distance $d_1$ from $M$; the other passes through a compensator plate $C$ to a fixed mirror $M_2$, a distance $d_2$ from $M$. Both return to $M$, which sends them together to the observer.
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.5*

^def-a6-4-1

![[ph-wo-a6-4-1.svg]]
*Planar view of the Michelson interferometer. The blue beam is reflected by $M$ to the movable mirror $M_1$ and back through $M$ to the observer; the red beam passes through $M$ and the compensator $C$ to $M_2$, returns, and is reflected by $M$ to the observer. The outgoing and returning beams are drawn slightly apart for clarity.*

> [!remark] Remark: Why the two beams interfere, and what the compensator is for
> Each point of the extended source sends light down both arms, so the two beams that reach the observer are copies of one wave: they are coherent ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]]). Points of the source whose two beams have the same path difference look equally bright, and together they form a fringe. The beam to $M_1$ passes through the glass of $M$ three times, the beam to $M_2$ only once; the compensator, a piece of the same glass without the silvering, makes the glass thickness in the two arms equal. Then the phase difference between the beams comes only from the difference of the distances travelled in air.

^rem-a6-4-1

## Counting fringes

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.4.1: Fringe Count for a Moving Mirror
> The two beams of a Michelson interferometer have path difference $2d_1 - 2d_2$. When the mirror $M_1$ moves a distance $\Delta d$, the number $m$ of fringes that pass a fixed point of the field of view satisfies
>
> $$
> \Delta d = \frac{m\lambda_0}{2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.5, eq. (3.7)*

^thm-a6-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each beam crosses its arm twice, out and back, so the paths differ by $2d_1 - 2d_2$. Whether a given point of the field is bright or dark depends on this difference modulo $\lambda_0$ ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]). Moving $M_1$ by $\Delta d$ changes $d_1$ by $\Delta d$ and the path difference by $2\Delta d$. Each change of $\lambda_0$ in the path difference brings every point back to its previous brightness, so each fringe moves into the place of its neighbour: one fringe passes. Hence $m$ fringes pass when $2\Delta d = m\lambda_0$.

^der-a6-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^def-a6-4-1|Def. §A6.4.1]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.4.2: Fringe Count for a Medium in One Arm
> If a chamber of length $t$ in one arm, initially evacuated, is filled with a gas of index $n$, the number of fringes that pass is
>
> $$
> m = \frac{2t}{\lambda_0}\,(n - 1) .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 3.6*

^thm-a6-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The beam crosses the chamber twice, a distance $2t$. Empty, the chamber holds $N_0 = 2t/\lambda_0$ wavelengths; filled, the wavelength is $\lambda_0/n$ ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]]) and it holds $N = 2tn/\lambda_0$. Equivalently, the optical path length of that stretch grows from $2t$ to $2nt$ ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^def-a6-3-2|Def. §A6.3.2]]). Each added wavelength shifts the pattern by one fringe, as in [[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^thm-a6-4-1|Theorem §A6.4.1]], so $m = N - N_0 = (2t/\lambda_0)(n - 1)$.

^der-a6-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]], [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^def-a6-3-2|Def. §A6.3.2]], [[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^thm-a6-4-1|Theorem §A6.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Constant phase offsets do not matter
> The two beams also differ in how they meet the beam splitter: one is reflected by it on the way in and transmitted on the way out, the other the reverse, and reflection can add a phase. Such offsets are constant. They decide whether the field is bright or dark for equal arms, but not how many fringes pass when a mirror moves or a gas flows in: the counts above depend only on changes of path.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 10*

^rem-a6-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: In the lab
> In the PHY 300 laboratory version, a laser lights the interferometer, a photodiode watches one point of the pattern, and $M_1$ sits on a piezoelectric transducer whose length changes with the applied voltage. Sweeping the voltage, the photodiode signal goes bright–dark–bright each time the mirror moves $\lambda_0/2$, which calibrates the transducer's displacement per volt. The fringes jitter if the table vibrates or the air in one arm is disturbed, since changes of a fraction of a wavelength are visible. Related instruments — the Mach–Zehnder and Fabry–Perot interferometers, and what the fringes reveal about the coherence of the source — are Chapter B10 (planned).
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 10*

^rem-a6-4-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.4.1: One Fringe
> With red laser light of $\lambda_0 = 630\ \mathrm{nm}$, a mirror is moved until one fringe ($m = 1$) has crossed the cross-hair. By [[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^thm-a6-4-1|Theorem §A6.4.1]] (it does not matter which mirror moves),
>
> $$
> \Delta d = \frac{m\lambda_0}{2} = \frac{630\ \mathrm{nm}}{2} = 315\ \mathrm{nm} = 0.315\ \mu\mathrm m .
> $$
>
> Counting fringes thus measures displacements to a fraction of a micrometre. The metre was once defined as the mirror displacement corresponding to $1\,650\,763.73$ wavelengths of a spectral line of krypton-86.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 3.5*

^ex-a6-4-1

> [!example] Example §A6.4.2: The Refractive Index of a Gas
> A chamber $t = 2.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ long in one arm is slowly filled with a gas while $m = 122$ dark fringes pass a reference line; $\lambda_0 = 632.8\ \mathrm{nm}$. By [[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^thm-a6-4-2|Theorem §A6.4.2]],
>
> $$
> n - 1 = \frac{m\lambda_0}{2t} = \frac{122\,(632.8 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm m)}{2\,(2.0 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm m)} = 1.9 \times 10^{-3}, \qquad n = 1.0019 .
> $$
>
> A difference this small from $1$ is invisible to a measurement based on Snell's law; the interferometer turns it into a count of more than a hundred fringes.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 3.6*

^ex-a6-4-2
