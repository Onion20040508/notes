The colours of a soap bubble or of an oil slick on a wet road are interference between two waves split off from one: the light reflected at the top surface of a thin layer and the light reflected at its bottom surface. As with the double slit ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference|§A6.1]]), the outcome depends on the path difference, now twice the film's thickness; but it also depends on a phase jump that reflection itself can introduce. This section states that jump as a law and combines the two effects.

## Thin films and reflection

> [!definition] Definition §A6.3.1: Thin Film; Thin-Film Interference
> A **thin film** is a transparent layer whose thickness $t$ is at most a few wavelengths of light. **Thin-film interference** is the interference between the light reflected from its top surface (ray 1) and the light that enters the film, reflects from its bottom surface and comes back out (ray 2).
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.4*

^def-a6-3-1

![[ph-wo-a6-3-1.svg]]
*A film of index $n_2$ and thickness $t$ between media $n_1$ and $n_3$. Ray 1 (blue) reflects at the top surface; ray 2 (red) reflects at the bottom surface and travels the extra distance inside the film. The incidence is drawn oblique only to separate the rays; the formulas below are for normal incidence, where the extra distance is $2t$.*

> [!law] Law §A6.3.1: Phase Change on Reflection
> Light reflected at an interface beyond which the index of refraction is **higher** undergoes a phase change of $\pi$ rad ($180^\circ$), equivalent to an extra path of $\lambda/2$. Light reflected at an interface beyond which the index is **lower** undergoes no phase change. Transmitted light never changes phase.
>
> *Valid when: transparent (non-absorbing) media at normal or near-normal incidence. The law follows from the Fresnel equations of electromagnetic theory ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]]).*
> *Source: UP3 §3.4*

^law-a6-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: The same rule as for waves on a string
> A wave on a string is inverted when it reflects from a junction with a heavier, slower string and comes back upright from a lighter one ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-4|Theorem §A2.5.4]]); the fixed end, which returns the wave with a phase change of $\pi$, is the limit of an infinitely heavy second string ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-3|Theorem §A2.5.3]]). For light the slower medium is the one of higher index, and $n$ plays the role of $\sqrt\mu$ in the string's $A_r/A_i = (\sqrt{\mu_1} - \sqrt{\mu_2})/(\sqrt{\mu_1} + \sqrt{\mu_2})$ (both are inversely proportional to the wave speed). At normal incidence the reflected field is $E_R/E_I = (n_1 - n_2)/(n_1 + n_2)$, negative (inverted) exactly when $n_2 > n_1$, and small for ordinary glass: about $4\%$ of the intensity is reflected at an air–glass surface. Both rays in a thin film are therefore weak and of comparable strength, which is why their interference can be nearly complete.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 15*

^rem-a6-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §A6.3.2: Optical Path Length
> A distance $L$ travelled in a medium of index $n$ has **optical path length** $nL$. Over it the phase of a wave of vacuum wavelength $\lambda_0$ advances by $2\pi L/\lambda_n = 2\pi\,nL/\lambda_0$: the same as over the distance $nL$ in vacuum. Two waves from one source interfere according to the difference of their optical path lengths, plus any phase changes on reflection.
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.4–3.5*

^def-a6-3-2

## The interference conditions

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.3.2: Thin-Film Conditions at Normal Incidence
> For light of vacuum wavelength $\lambda$ incident normally on a film of index $n$ and thickness $t$, let $\lambda_n = \lambda/n$ be the wavelength in the film. Count the phase changes of $\pi$ that rays 1 and 2 undergo on reflection ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^law-a6-3-1|Law §A6.3.1]]).
> - If both rays, or neither, change phase:
> $$
> 2t = m\lambda_n \ \ \text{(constructive)}, \qquad 2t = \bigl(m + \tfrac12\bigr)\lambda_n \ \ \text{(destructive)} .
> $$
> - If exactly one of them changes phase, the conditions swap:
> $$
> 2t = \bigl(m + \tfrac12\bigr)\lambda_n \ \ \text{(constructive)}, \qquad 2t = m\lambda_n \ \ \text{(destructive)} .
> $$
>
> Here $m = 0, 1, 2, \dots$
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.4*

^thm-a6-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At normal incidence ray 2 crosses the film twice, so it travels $2t$ farther than ray 1, all of it in the film, where the wavelength is $\lambda_n = \lambda/n$ ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]]). This contributes the phase difference $2\pi(2t)/\lambda_n$ (optical path difference $2nt$, [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^def-a6-3-2|Def. §A6.3.2]]). Reflection adds $\pi$ to each ray that reflects off a higher index. If both rays or neither get it, the reflections leave the relative phase unchanged and only the path counts: by [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]] the waves reinforce for $2t = m\lambda_n$ and cancel for $2t = (m + \frac12)\lambda_n$. If only one ray gets it, the relative phase is shifted by $\pi$, i.e. by half a wavelength, which exchanges the two conditions.

^der-a6-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^law-a6-3-1|Law §A6.3.1]], [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^def-a6-3-2|Def. §A6.3.2]], [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Three things to get right
> - Use the wavelength **in the film**, $\lambda_n = \lambda/n$, not the vacuum wavelength.
> - Decide the phase change separately at each surface, by comparing the index on the near side with the index beyond. Only the **difference** between the two rays matters: two flips cancel.
> - The two-ray picture neglects light that bounces back and forth inside the film several times. That is fine for weak reflections; for highly reflecting surfaces the multiple reflections dominate (the Fabry–Perot interferometer, [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-2|Theorem §B10.4.2]], [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-3|Theorem §B10.4.3]]).

^cau-a6-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why the thinnest films are dark, and why films must be thin
> - A soap film in air has a flip at the top surface ($1.00 \to 1.33$) and none at the bottom ($1.33 \to 1.00$). As $t \to 0$ the path difference vanishes and the one flip makes the two reflections cancel at **every** wavelength: a bubble turns black just before it bursts.
> - In white light each wavelength is reflected most strongly at its own thicknesses, so a film of varying thickness shows bands of colour. For a thick layer the conditions for neighbouring wavelengths, and for slightly different angles of incidence, crowd together, and the colours wash out to white. That is why interference colours come from films only a few wavelengths thick.

^rem-a6-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Air wedges and Newton's rings
> Two flat glass slides touching along one edge enclose a wedge of air. The phase flip happens at the lower (air–glass) surface only, so the line of contact is dark, and the condition for a given wavelength repeats each time the air gap grows by $\lambda/2$: in monochromatic light one sees evenly spaced bright and dark bands, in white light repeating rainbows. A curved lens resting on a flat plate gives circular bands, **Newton's rings**. Since one band corresponds to half a wavelength of gap, opticians use them to test lens surfaces against a master to a fraction of a wavelength; a perfect match shows no rings.

^rem-a6-3-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.3.1: Nonreflective Coating
> A camera lens of glass ($n_3 = 1.52$) is coated with magnesium fluoride ($n_2 = 1.38$) to suppress the reflection of $\lambda = 550\ \mathrm{nm}$ light, near the peak of visible sunlight. Both reflections, air $\to$ coating and coating $\to$ glass, go to a higher index, so both rays flip and the first case of [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]] applies. The thinnest destructive film has $2t = \frac12\lambda_n$:
>
> $$
> t = \frac{\lambda}{4n_2} = \frac{550\ \mathrm{nm}}{4(1.38)} = 99.6\ \mathrm{nm} .
> $$
>
> The thinnest coating is best, because its condition changes least with the angle of incidence. Other wavelengths are only partly cancelled, so a "nonreflective" coating is only approximately so. (University Physics substitutes $500\ \mathrm{nm}$ here and prints $90.6\ \mathrm{nm}$.)
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 3.3*

^ex-a6-3-1

> [!example] Example §A6.3.2: Bands on a Soap Film
> A soap film ($n = 1.333$, as water) in air is lit by red light, $\lambda = 650\ \mathrm{nm}$, so $\lambda_n = 650\ \mathrm{nm}/1.333 = 488\ \mathrm{nm}$. Only ray 1 flips (second case of [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]]):
> - constructive for $2t = \frac12\lambda_n, \frac32\lambda_n, \frac52\lambda_n$: $t = 122\ \mathrm{nm},\ 366\ \mathrm{nm},\ 610\ \mathrm{nm}$;
> - destructive for $2t = 0, \lambda_n, 2\lambda_n$: $t = 0,\ 244\ \mathrm{nm},\ 488\ \mathrm{nm}$.
>
> Bright and dark bands alternate every $\lambda_n/4 = 122\ \mathrm{nm}$ of thickness, starting dark at zero thickness; on a smoothly tapering film they are evenly spaced.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 3.4*

^ex-a6-3-2
