Most applications of slit interference use not two slits but many: the diffraction grating ([[§A7.3 Diffraction Gratings|§A7.3]]) has thousands. This section extends the double slit of [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference|§A6.1]] to $N$ equally spaced slits. The bright fringes stay where they were, but they become brighter and much narrower, and weak secondary maxima appear between them.

## Principal maxima

> [!definition] Definition §A6.2.1: Principal and Secondary Maxima
> In the interference pattern of $N > 2$ equally spaced narrow slits, the **principal maxima** are the brightest fringes, where the waves from all slits arrive in phase. The weaker bright fringes between them are **secondary maxima**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.3*

^def-a6-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.2.1: Principal Maxima of N Slits
> For $N$ narrow slits a distance $d$ apart, illuminated as in [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^mod-a6-1-2|Model §A6.1.2]], the principal maxima lie at the double-slit angles
>
> $$
> d\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.3*

^thm-a6-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Toward a distant point at angle $\theta$, the path from slit $j$ is longer than that from slit $1$ by $(j - 1)\,d\sin\theta$, by the construction of [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]] applied to each pair. If $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, every one of these path differences is a whole number of wavelengths, so all $N$ waves arrive crest to crest ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]).

^der-a6-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]

## The intensity pattern

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.2.2: N-Slit Intensity
> Let $\phi = 2\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda$ be the phase difference between waves from adjacent slits. The intensity of the $N$-slit pattern is
>
> $$
> I(\theta) = I_1\,\frac{\sin^2(N\phi/2)}{\sin^2(\phi/2)},
> $$
>
> where $I_1$ is the intensity from one slit alone. Consequently:
> - at the principal maxima ($\phi = 2\pi m$) the intensity is $N^2 I_1$;
> - the intensity is zero where $d\sin\theta = \dfrac{k}{N}\,\lambda$ with $k$ an integer that is not a multiple of $N$: between two neighbouring principal maxima there are $N - 1$ dark fringes and $N - 2$ secondary maxima;
> - the dark fringes next to the principal maximum of order $m$ lie at $d\sin\theta = \bigl(m \pm \tfrac1N\bigr)\lambda$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 14 slides · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19 · UP3 §3.3*

^thm-a6-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Represent the wave from each slit at the distant point by a **phasor** of length $A_0$: the arrow from the centre of the reference circle to the reference point ([[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^def-a1-3-2|Def. §A1.3.2]]), whose angle is the phase of the wave and whose projection is the field. The waves all have the same frequency, so their phasors turn together and the resultant field is the projection of their vector sum (phasor addition, [[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^thm-a1-3-2|Theorem §A1.3.2]]; in complex form, the sum of the complex numbers of [[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^rem-a1-3-2|Remark: The reference point as a complex number]]). Each phasor is turned by $\phi$ from the one before, so placed head to tail they form part of a regular polygon (figure below). Its vertices lie on a circle of radius $R$, and each phasor is a chord subtending the angle $\phi$ at the centre: $A_0 = 2R\sin(\phi/2)$. The resultant joins the first vertex to the last, a chord subtending $N\phi$: $A = 2R\sin(N\phi/2)$. Eliminating $R$,
>
> $$
> A = A_0\,\frac{\sin(N\phi/2)}{\sin(\phi/2)},
> $$
>
> and since intensity goes as amplitude squared, $I = I_1\sin^2(N\phi/2)/\sin^2(\phi/2)$. (With complex exponentials the same result is the sum of the geometric series $\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{ij\phi}$.)
> - At $\phi \to 2\pi m$ numerator and denominator both vanish; by l'Hôpital's rule ([[§30 L'Hospital's Rule#^thm-30-1|451 Thm. §30.1]]) the ratio $\sin(N\phi/2)/\sin(\phi/2)$ tends to $\pm N$, so $I = N^2I_1$: the phasors line up in a straight line of length $NA_0$.
> - $I = 0$ when $\sin(N\phi/2) = 0$ but $\sin(\phi/2) \neq 0$: $N\phi/2 = k\pi$ with $k$ not a multiple of $N$, i.e. $d\sin\theta = k\lambda/N$. The polygon closes on itself. Between $k = mN$ and $k = (m + 1)N$ lie the $N - 1$ values $k = mN + 1, \dots, mN + N - 1$; between neighbouring zeros the intensity rises again, giving $N - 2$ secondary maxima. The zeros nearest the principal maximum $m$ are $k = mN \pm 1$.

> [!remark] Remark: Checks
> For $N = 2$, $\sin^2\phi/\sin^2(\phi/2) = 4\cos^2(\phi/2)$, which is the double-slit result $I = 4I_1\cos^2(\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda)$ ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-5|Theorem §A6.1.5]]): one zero and no secondary maximum between principal maxima, as $N - 1 = 1$ and $N - 2 = 0$ require.

^rem-a6-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Taller and narrower
> The principal maxima grow as $N^2$ while their width, the distance between the flanking zeros, shrinks: $\Delta(d\sin\theta) = 2\lambda/N$. Height times width grows as $N$, in step with the power, which is $N$ times that of one slit: interference redistributes the light of the $N$ slits into ever sharper peaks without creating or destroying energy. In the limit of very large $N$ only the bright, narrow principal maxima are visible: a grating ([[§A7.3 Diffraction Gratings#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]).

^rem-a6-2-2

![[ph-wo-a6-2-2.svg]]
*The $N$-slit intensity in units of $I_1$, computed from Theorem §A6.2.2 for $N = 2$, $3$ and $4$. The principal maxima at integer $d\sin\theta/\lambda$ keep their positions and rise to $N^2 = 4, 9, 16$ while narrowing. Between them lie $N - 1$ zeros (red dots for $N = 4$) and $N - 2$ secondary maxima; for $N = 3$ the single secondary maximum has height $I_1$, one ninth of the principal maxima.*

> [!caution] Caution: How bright are the secondary maxima?
> University Physics says that the secondary maxima have $1/N^2$ of the intensity of the principal maxima. That holds for the secondary maximum exactly halfway between principal maxima when $N$ is odd: there $\phi = \pi$ and $I = I_1 = (N^2I_1)/N^2$. The others are brighter. For $N = 4$ both secondary maxima have $0.074$ of the principal intensity, not $1/16 = 0.0625$, and for large $N$ the ones next to a principal maximum approach about $5\%$ of it, the same fraction as the side maxima of a single slit ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-4|Theorem §A7.1.4]]). What is true for every $N$ is that the secondary maxima are far weaker than the principal ones.

^cau-a6-2-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.2.1: Three Slits Halfway between Principal Maxima
> At $d\sin\theta = \lambda/2$, waves from adjacent slits differ by half a wavelength. With two slits this would be a dark fringe. With three, slits 1 and 2 cancel, but the wave from slit 3 has no partner: the phasors point $+, -, +$ and sum to one phasor $A_0$. The intensity is $I_1$, against $(3A_0)^2 \to 9I_1$ at a principal maximum, a ratio of $1/9$. This is the secondary maximum of the $N = 3$ curve in the figure; it is flanked by zeros at $d\sin\theta = \lambda/3$ and $2\lambda/3$, where the three phasors, turned by $120^\circ$ each, form a closed triangle.
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.3*

^ex-a6-2-1

> [!example] Example §A6.2.2: The Four-Slit Pattern
> Sketch the intensity of $N = 4$ narrow slits, with the minima and the peak heights. By [[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]] with $N = 4$:
> - **maxima:** principal maxima where $\sin(\phi/2) = 0$, i.e. $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, of height $16I_1$;
> - **minima:** where $\sin(2\phi) = 0$ but $\sin(\phi/2) \neq 0$, i.e. $d\sin\theta = \frac14\lambda, \frac24\lambda, \frac34\lambda$ (plus whole multiples of $\lambda$): three zeros between neighbouring principal maxima;
> - **secondary maxima:** one between each pair of neighbouring zeros, so two between principal maxima, near $d\sin\theta = 0.37\lambda$ and $0.63\lambda$. Using $\sin 2\phi/\sin(\phi/2) = 4\cos(\phi/2)\cos\phi$, their height comes out (numerically) as $1.19I_1$, about $7\%$ of the principal maxima.
>
> The pattern repeats with period $\lambda$ in $d\sin\theta$; it is the bottom panel of the figure.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Final practice*

^ex-a6-2-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The limit $\sin(N\phi/2)/\sin(\phi/2) \to \pm N$ at a principal maximum is a $0/0$ limit handled by l'Hôpital's rule ([[§30 L'Hospital's Rule#^thm-30-1|451 Thm. §30.1]]).
> - The same phasor polygon, in the limit of infinitely many infinitesimal phasors, becomes a circular arc and gives the single-slit pattern ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-3|Theorem §A7.1.3]]).
