Geometric optics treats light as rays; it cannot explain the colours of a soap bubble or the bright and dark bands behind two narrow slits. The surest sign of a wave is interference: two waves meeting at a point add ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves|§A2.5]]), and whether they reinforce or cancel depends on how far each has travelled. Young's double-slit experiment (1801) showed this for light and settled that light is a wave. This section works out when two coherent sources interfere constructively or destructively, where the fringes of a double slit fall, and how bright they are.

## Coherent sources

> [!definition] Definition §A6.1.1: Coherent and Incoherent Sources; Monochromatic Light
> Two sources of waves of the same frequency are **coherent** if they keep a definite, constant phase relation (in phase, or with a fixed phase difference). They are **incoherent** if their phase relation changes randomly in time. Light of a single wavelength is **monochromatic**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.1*

^def-a6-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why two lamps give no fringes
> Two independent lamps, or two areas of one lamp or of the Sun, do not emit in step: their relative phase jumps at random far faster than any eye or detector can follow, so any interference pattern washes out. Young made his two sources coherent by lighting both from a single pinhole $S_0$: the light at the two slits is then a copy of one wave, and if $S_0$ is equidistant from them, the two copies are in phase and of equal amplitude.

^rem-a6-1-1

## Interference by path difference

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.1: Interference of Two Coherent Sources
> Two coherent sources emit in phase sinusoidal waves of wavelength $\lambda$ that arrive at a point $P$ with equal amplitude, after travelling distances $r_1$ and $r_2$. With the **path difference** $\Delta l = \lvert r_2 - r_1\rvert$ and the phase difference $\phi = 2\pi\,\Delta l/\lambda$ it produces, the intensity at $P$ is
>
> $$
> I = 4I_1\cos^2\frac{\phi}{2} = 4I_1\cos^2\frac{\pi\,\Delta l}{\lambda},
> $$
>
> where $I_1$ is the intensity one source alone would give at $P$. The interference is
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &\text{constructive } (I = 4I_1) &&\text{if } \Delta l = m\lambda, \\
> &\text{destructive } (I = 0) &&\text{if } \Delta l = \bigl(m + \tfrac12\bigr)\lambda,
> \end{aligned}
> \qquad m = 0, 1, 2, \dots
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.1, eqs. (3.1)–(3.2) · PHY 287, week 14 slides*

^thm-a6-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the waves arriving at $P$ (light: the electric field) as $E_1 = E_0\sin(kr_1 - \omega t)$ and $E_2 = E_0\sin(kr_2 - \omega t)$, with $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]); they leave the sources in phase and differ only through the distance travelled. By the superposition principle ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves|§A2.5]]) the field at $P$ is $E_1 + E_2$, and $\sin a + \sin b = 2\sin\frac{a + b}{2}\cos\frac{a - b}{2}$ gives
>
> $$
> E = \Bigl[2E_0\cos\frac{k(r_1 - r_2)}{2}\Bigr]\sin(k\bar r - \omega t), \qquad \bar r = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2} .
> $$
>
> At $P$ the field oscillates at the source frequency with the time-independent amplitude $2E_0\lvert\cos(\pi\,\Delta l/\lambda)\rvert$ (cosine is even, so the sign of $r_1 - r_2$ does not matter). Intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]]); one source alone gives $I_1 \propto E_0^2$, so $I = 4I_1\cos^2(\pi\,\Delta l/\lambda)$. The cosine is $\pm1$ when $\pi\,\Delta l/\lambda = m\pi$ and zero when $\pi\,\Delta l/\lambda = (m + \frac12)\pi$. In words: a whole number of wavelengths brings the waves back crest to crest, an odd number of half wavelengths puts crest on trough.

^der-a6-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A2.5 Interference of Waves|§A2.5]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]], [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Interference moves energy, it does not create or destroy it
> At a bright fringe the intensity is $4I_1$, twice the $2I_1$ that two separate sources would give; at a dark fringe it is zero. Averaged over many fringes $\cos^2$ is $\frac12$, so the average is $2I_1$: energy is taken from the dark places and delivered to the bright ones. For incoherent sources the phase difference wanders at random, $\cos^2$ averages to $\frac12$ at every point, and the screen is uniformly lit at $2I_1$: intensities, not amplitudes, add.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 18*

^rem-a6-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Path difference, phase difference, and the medium
> - The conditions $\Delta l = m\lambda$ and $(m + \frac12)\lambda$ assume the sources are in phase. If they start with a phase difference, it adds to $2\pi\,\Delta l/\lambda$ (a reflection can supply such a shift: [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference|§A6.3]]).
> - $\lambda$ is the wavelength in the medium the waves travel through, $\lambda_n = \lambda_0/n$ ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]]). Immersing a double slit in water shrinks the wavelength, and the fringes move closer together.

^cau-a6-1-1

## The double slit

> [!model] Model §A6.1.2: Young's Double Slit
> Monochromatic light from one source $S_0$ falls on two parallel slits $S_1$, $S_2$ a distance $d$ apart (centre to centre), equidistant from $S_0$. Assumptions:
> - the slits are so narrow that each acts as a single Huygens source ([[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^pr-a4-4-1|Principle §A4.4.1]]) sending light in all forward directions;
> - the two slit sources are coherent, in phase and of equal amplitude;
> - the screen is a distance $D \gg d$ away (typically $d \lesssim 1\ \mathrm{mm}$, $D \approx 1\ \mathrm m$), so the paths from $S_1$ and $S_2$ to a point $P$ on it are essentially parallel and make the same angle $\theta$ with the original beam.
>
> The finite width of real slits is added in [[§A7.2 Double-Slit Diffraction|§A7.2]].
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.1–3.2*

^mod-a6-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.3: Bright and Dark Fringes of a Double Slit
> In Young's double slit, the path difference to a point at angle $\theta$ is $\Delta l = d\sin\theta$. Bright and dark fringes lie at
>
> $$
> d\sin\theta = m\lambda \quad\text{(constructive)}, \qquad d\sin\theta = \bigl(m + \tfrac12\bigr)\lambda \quad\text{(destructive)}, \qquad m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.2, eqs. (3.3)–(3.5)*

^thm-a6-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Since $D \gg d$, the rays $r_1$ from $S_1$ and $r_2$ from $S_2$ to $P$ are parallel, both at angle $\theta$ to the axis (figure, right). Drop the perpendicular from $S_1$ onto $r_2$. From there on the two paths have equal length, so the extra length of $r_2$ is the short side of the right triangle with hypotenuse $S_1S_2 = d$; the angle at $S_1$ between the barrier and the perpendicular equals $\theta$ (both are complements of the angle between the barrier and the rays), so $\Delta l = d\sin\theta$. Insert this into [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]; negative $m$ give the fringes on the other side of the axis.

^der-a6-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^mod-a6-1-2|Model §A6.1.2]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]

![[ph-wo-a6-1-1.svg]]
*(a) The layout, with $D$ hugely shortened: the blue paths $r_1$ and $r_2$ from the two slits meet at $P$, at height $y$ and angle $\theta$. (b) Near the slits the paths to the distant $P$ are parallel; their lengths differ by the red segment $\Delta l = d\sin\theta$.*

> [!definition] Definition §A6.1.2: Interference Fringes; Order
> The alternating bright and dark bands on the screen are **interference fringes**. The integer $m$ in $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ is the **order** of the bright fringe ($m = 4$: fourth order); $m = 0$ is the **central maximum**, on the axis, where the two paths are equal.
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.2*

^def-a6-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.4: Fringe Positions on the Screen
> For small angles, the $m$th bright fringe lies a distance
>
> $$
> y_m = \frac{m\lambda D}{d}
> $$
>
> from the central maximum, and neighbouring bright fringes are evenly spaced by $\Delta y = \lambda D/d$ (the dark fringes lie halfway between).
>
> *Source: UP3 §3.2, eq. (3.6)*

^thm-a6-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The point $P$ at height $y$ is seen from the slits at an angle with $\tan\theta = y/D$. For $\theta \ll 1$ rad, $\sin\theta \approx \theta \approx \tan\theta$, the first-order Taylor approximation of both functions at $0$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]). Then $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]]) becomes $d\,y_m/D = m\lambda$, and $y_{m+1} - y_m = \lambda D/d$.

^der-a6-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Small $d$, big effect
> At fixed $\lambda$ and $m$, $\sin\theta = m\lambda/d$: the closer the slits, the farther apart the fringes. This is the general rule that wave effects are large when the object the wave meets (here the slit spacing) is not large compared with $\lambda$. Measuring $d$, $D$ and the fringe spacing gives $\lambda$; Young measured visible wavelengths this way. Since $\lvert\sin\theta\rvert \le 1$, the orders are limited to $\lvert m\rvert \le d/\lambda$.

^rem-a6-1-3

## Intensity

> [!theorem] Theorem §A6.1.5: Double-Slit Intensity
> In Young's double slit the intensity at angle $\theta$ is
>
> $$
> I(\theta) = I_0\cos^2\Bigl(\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\Bigr), \qquad I_0 = 4I_1,
> $$
>
> with $I_0$ the intensity of the central maximum and $I_1$ that of one slit alone. All bright fringes are equally bright.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 14 slides · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 18*

^thm-a6-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $\Delta l = d\sin\theta$ ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]]) into $I = 4I_1\cos^2(\pi\,\Delta l/\lambda)$ ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]). Narrow slits send equal amplitudes in all directions (Model §A6.1.2), so $I_1$ does not depend on $\theta$.

^der-a6-1-5

*Uses:* [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^mod-a6-1-2|Model §A6.1.2]]

![[ph-wo-a6-1-2.svg]]
*The double-slit intensity (blue), computed from Theorem §A6.1.5, against $d\sin\theta/\lambda$: equal bright fringes at the integers $m$, zeros halfway between. The dashed grey level $I_0/2 = 2I_1$ is what two incoherent sources would give everywhere, and it is the average of the fringe pattern.*

> [!caution] Caution: Real fringes fade
> A photograph of a real double-slit pattern shows fringes that dim away from the centre. That is not in Theorem §A6.1.5: it comes from the finite width of each slit, which sends less light at larger angles. The fringe *positions* stay those of Theorem §A6.1.3; the *intensities* are modulated by the single-slit pattern ([[§A7.2 Double-Slit Diffraction|§A7.2]]).

^cau-a6-1-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A6.1.1: Measuring a Wavelength
> Light from a He–Ne laser passes through two slits $d = 0.0100\ \mathrm{mm}$ apart, and the third bright line is at $\theta = 10.95^\circ$. With $m = 3$ in $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]]),
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{d\sin\theta}{m} = \frac{(1.00 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm m)\sin 10.95^\circ}{3} = 6.33 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm m = 633\ \mathrm{nm},
> $$
>
> the red He–Ne line. The highest order follows from $\sin\theta \le 1$: $m \le d/\lambda = (1.00 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm m)/(633 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm m) = 15.8$, so $m_{\max} = 15$ (at $\theta = 71.7^\circ$; the small-angle formula of Theorem §A6.1.4 is useless out there). In practice the outer fringes are too faint to see (Caution: Real fringes fade).
>
> *Source: UP3 Examples 3.1–3.2*

^ex-a6-1-1

> [!example] Example §A6.1.2: Two Radio Antennas
> Two identical antennas, $d = 10.0\ \mathrm m$ apart, broadcast in phase at $f = 60.0\ \mathrm{MHz}$, so $\lambda = c/f = 5.00\ \mathrm m$. At $700\ \mathrm m \gg d$ from the midpoint, in the direction $\theta = 0$, the intensity is $I_0 = 0.020\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$. At the same distance, by [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-5|Theorem §A6.1.5]]:
> - at $\theta = 4.0^\circ$: $\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda = \pi(10.0)(0.0698)/5.00 = 0.438\ \mathrm{rad}$, so $I = (0.020\ \mathrm{W/m^2})\cos^2(0.438) = 0.016\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$;
> - half intensity, $\cos^2 = \frac12$, needs $\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda = \pi/4$: $\sin\theta = \lambda/4d = 0.125$, $\theta = 7.2^\circ$;
> - zero intensity: $d\sin\theta = (m + \frac12)\lambda$ gives $\sin\theta = 0.25$ or $0.75$ (larger $m$ would need $\sin\theta > 1$), so $\theta = \pm14.5^\circ$ and $\pm48.6^\circ$.
>
> The same mathematics as for light, with $\lambda$ now in metres: a pair of antennas sends its power into chosen directions and almost none into others.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Final practice*

^ex-a6-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The sum-to-product step is the algebra of beats ([[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]), with the two waves differing in path instead of frequency: here the "envelope" $\cos(\pi\,\Delta l/\lambda)$ varies across the screen instead of in time.
> - The small-angle step $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta$ is first-order Taylor approximation ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).
> - Sent one photon or one electron at a time, a double slit still builds up the fringe pattern dot by dot; each particle interferes with itself (Quantum Mechanics, level A, planned).
