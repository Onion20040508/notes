*Sources: Fowles, Introduction to Modern Optics, §2.7 and §2.9–2.11 · Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lecture 15 · PHY 300 (Mihaly, Fall 2024).*

What happens beyond the critical angle? In the ray model there is simply no refracted ray, and all the light is reflected ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^def-a4-3-1|Def. §A4.3.1]], [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]). The wave picture says more. Phase matching ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]) still holds, but the transmitted wave vector acquires an imaginary normal component: a field exists beyond the surface, travelling along it and decaying away from it within a fraction of a wavelength, and carrying no energy across. The Fresnel coefficients of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]] become complex numbers of modulus $1$: the reflection is total, but s and p light are reflected with different phase shifts, so total reflection is a retarder. Here $n = n_2/n_1 < 1$ (internal reflection) and $\theta = \theta_1 > \theta_c = \sin^{-1}n$. Signs of phases depend on the optics convention ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-1|§B8.1, Caution: The optics sign convention]]).

## The evanescent wave

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.3.1: The Evanescent Wave
> When light in medium 1 meets medium 2 at $\theta_1 > \theta_c$, i.e. $n_1\sin\theta_1 > n_2$, the transmitted field is
>
> $$
> \vec E_t = \vec E_{t0}\,e^{-\beta z}\,e^{i(k_xx - \omega t)}, \qquad k_x = \frac{n_1\omega}{c}\sin\theta_1, \qquad \beta = \frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{n_1^2\sin^2\theta_1 - n_2^2} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}\sqrt{n_1^2\sin^2\theta_1 - n_2^2} .
> $$
>
> 1. Its surfaces of constant phase move along the interface with speed $\omega/k_x = (c/n_1)/\sin\theta_1$, which is larger than $c/n_1$ by the factor $1/\sin\theta_1$ and smaller than $c/n_2$.
> 2. Its amplitude falls by $1/e$ over the **penetration depth** $1/\beta$, typically a fraction of a wavelength; it diverges as $\theta_1 \to \theta_c$.
> 3. It carries no energy across the interface on average, $\langle S_z\rangle = 0$, but carries energy along it, $\langle S_x\rangle > 0$.
>
> In the Fresnel formulas this amounts to $n_2\cos\theta_2 = i\,n_1\sqrt{\sin^2\theta_1 - n^2}$: $\cos\theta_2$ is imaginary and $\theta_2$ is no longer a real angle.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.9, eqs. (2.65)–(2.67)*

^thm-b9-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Phase matching does not depend on whether the transmitted wave propagates: the transmitted field must contain $e^{i(k_xx - \omega t)}$ with the incident $k_x$ ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]). In medium 2 each field component obeys the wave equation $\nabla^2E = (n_2^2/c^2)\,\partial_t^2E$ (from [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|Model §B8.1.1]]: $\nabla\times\nabla\times\vec E = -\nabla^2\vec E$ when $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$), so a field $e^{i(k_xx + k_zz - \omega t)}$ needs $k_x^2 + k_z^2 = n_2^2\omega^2/c^2$. For $k_x > n_2\omega/c$ this forces $k_z = \pm i\beta$ with $\beta$ as stated. The root $k_z = -i\beta$ would grow without limit away from the interface, with nothing in medium 2 to feed it; so $k_z = +i\beta$ and $e^{ik_zz} = e^{-\beta z}$. Writing $k_z = (n_2\omega/c)\cos\theta_2$ gives $n_2\cos\theta_2 = i(c/\omega)\beta = in_1\sqrt{\sin^2\theta_1 - n^2}$.
>
> *1.* A point of constant phase has $k_xx - \omega t$ fixed, so it moves at $\omega/k_x = c/(n_1\sin\theta_1)$; since $n_2 < n_1\sin\theta_1 < n_1$, this lies between $c/n_1$ and $c/n_2$.
>
> *3.* Take s polarization, $\vec E = \hat yE_y$ with $E_y \propto e^{-\beta z}e^{i(k_xx - \omega t)}$. Faraday's law $\nabla\times\vec E = i\omega\mu_0\vec H$ gives $\vec H = (i\omega\mu_0)^{-1}(-\partial_zE_y, 0, \partial_xE_y) = (\omega\mu_0)^{-1}(-i\beta E_y,\ 0,\ k_xE_y)$. The time-averaged Poynting vector is $\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\vec E\times\vec H^*)$ ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-4|Theorem §B8.1.4]]):
>
> $$
> \langle S_z\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(-E_yH_x^*\bigr) = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\Bigl(\frac{-i\beta\lvert E_y\rvert^2}{\omega\mu_0}\Bigr) = 0, \qquad \langle S_x\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(E_yH_z^*\bigr) = \frac{k_x\lvert E_y\rvert^2}{2\omega\mu_0} > 0 .
> $$
>
> $E_y$ and $H_x$ oscillate a quarter period apart, so the energy flowing into medium 2 during one half-cycle flows back during the next. The p case is the same with $\vec E$ and $\vec H$ exchanged in role.

> [!caution] Caution: "No refracted ray" is not "no field"
> Level A's statement that beyond $\theta_c$ nothing enters the second medium ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^cau-a4-3-1|§A4.3, Caution: Which way the light goes]]) is true of energy, not of fields. The field beyond the surface is needed to satisfy the boundary conditions; it simply does not propagate. If a third medium is brought within a few penetration depths, the evanescent field becomes a propagating wave in it, and energy does cross ([[§B9.3 Total Internal Reflection and the Evanescent Wave#^rem-b9-3-1|Remark: Frustrated total reflection]]).

## Total reflection and its phase shifts

> [!theorem] Theorem §B9.3.2: Total Reflection and the Phase Shifts
> For $\sin\theta > n$ the reflection coefficients of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]] become
>
> $$
> r_s = \frac{\cos\theta - i\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2}}{\cos\theta + i\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2}} = e^{-i\delta_s}, \qquad r_p = \frac{-n^2\cos\theta + i\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2}}{n^2\cos\theta + i\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2}} = -e^{-i\delta_p} ,
> $$
>
> so $R_s = R_p = 1$: the reflection is **total**, for both polarizations and every $\theta > \theta_c$. The phase shifts are given by
>
> $$
> \tan\frac{\delta_s}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2}}{\cos\theta}, \qquad \tan\frac{\delta_p}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2}}{n^2\cos\theta}, \qquad \tan\frac{\Delta}{2} = \frac{\cos\theta\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2}}{\sin^2\theta}, \quad \Delta = \delta_p - \delta_s .
> $$
>
> $\delta_s$ and $\delta_p$ rise from $0$ at $\theta_c$ to $\pi$ at grazing incidence, with $\delta_p > \delta_s$ in between. The relative phase $\Delta$ vanishes at both ends and is largest at $\sin^2\theta_m = 2n^2/(1 + n^2)$, where $\tan(\Delta_{\max}/2) = (1 - n^2)/2n$. In the right-handed frames of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-5|Theorem §B9.2.5]] the reflection matrix is
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix} -r_p & 0 \\ 0 & r_s \end{pmatrix} = e^{-i\delta_s}\begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\Delta} & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} :
> $$
>
> up to a phase, a linear retarder with retardance $\Delta$ whose fast axis is p ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^def-b8-2-3|Def. §B8.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.7 (total internal reflection), eqs. (2.62)–(2.63), and §2.10–2.11, eqs. (2.68)–(2.72) · PHY 300, Worksheet 9, questions 5–6*

^thm-b9-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the $n$-form of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]] the root $\sqrt{n^2 - \sin^2\theta}$ stands for $n_2\cos\theta_2/n_1$, which beyond $\theta_c$ is $+i\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2}$ ([[§B9.3 Total Internal Reflection and the Evanescent Wave#^thm-b9-3-1|Theorem §B9.3.1]]); the boundary conditions, and hence the formulas, are unchanged. Write $c = \cos\theta$, $s = \sin\theta$, $\alpha = \sqrt{s^2 - n^2} > 0$.
>
> *Moduli and phases.* $c + i\alpha = \rho e^{ia}$ with $\tan a = \alpha/c$, so $r_s = (c - i\alpha)/(c + i\alpha) = e^{-2ia}$: $\lvert r_s\rvert = 1$ and $\delta_s = 2a$. Likewise $n^2c + i\alpha = \rho'e^{ib}$ with $\tan b = \alpha/(n^2c)$, and $r_p = -(n^2c - i\alpha)/(n^2c + i\alpha) = -e^{-2ib}$, $\delta_p = 2b$. Since $n^2 < 1$, $b > a$. As $\theta \to \theta_c$, $\alpha \to 0$ and both shifts vanish; as $\theta \to 90^\circ$, $c \to 0$, both tangents diverge and both shifts tend to $\pi$.
>
> *Relative phase.* $\tan(\Delta/2) = \tan(b - a) = (\tan b - \tan a)/(1 + \tan a\tan b)$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{\dfrac{\alpha}{n^2c} - \dfrac{\alpha}{c}}{1 + \dfrac{\alpha^2}{n^2c^2}} = \frac{\alpha c(1 - n^2)}{n^2c^2 + \alpha^2} = \frac{\alpha c(1 - n^2)}{s^2(1 - n^2)} = \frac{c\alpha}{s^2} ,
> $$
>
> using $n^2c^2 + \alpha^2 = n^2 - n^2s^2 + s^2 - n^2 = s^2(1 - n^2)$. With $u = s^2$, $\tan^2(\Delta/2) = (1 - u)(u - n^2)/u^2 = -1 + (1 + n^2)/u - n^2/u^2$, a quadratic in $1/u$ that is largest at $1/u = (1 + n^2)/2n^2$, with value $(1 + n^2)^2/4n^2 - 1 = (1 - n^2)^2/4n^2$.
>
> *Matrix.* $-r_p = e^{-i\delta_p} = e^{-i\delta_s}e^{-i\Delta}$ and $r_s = e^{-i\delta_s}$. The factor $e^{-i\Delta}$ on p, relative to s, means that s is delayed by $\Delta$ with respect to p.

> [!caution] Caution: Signs of the phase shifts
> - With $e^{+i\omega t}$ instead of $e^{-i\omega t}$, all these coefficients are complex conjugated: $r_s = e^{+i\delta_s}$, and the evanescent root is $-i\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2}$. The magnitudes $\delta_s$, $\delta_p$, $\Delta$ and every observable consequence are unchanged.
> - The minus sign in $r_p = -e^{-i\delta_p}$ belongs to the sign convention of Def. §B9.2.1 (where $r_p = +1$ at grazing incidence); Fowles includes it so that $\delta_p$ is the physically meaningful shift and $\Delta = \delta_p - \delta_s$ the relative retardation of p and s, which is what [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-5|Theorem §B9.2.5]]'s right-handed frame shows.

^cau-b9-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Frustrated total reflection and the Goos–Hänchen shift
> - Two $45^\circ$ prisms whose long faces are close but not touching transmit part of a beam that each alone would totally reflect: the evanescent field of the first reaches across the gap and launches a propagating wave in the second. The transmitted fraction falls rapidly as the gap grows beyond the penetration depth, so the device works as a variable beam splitter, used as an adjustable output coupler for lasers. Raman showed the same penetration by bringing a sharp metal edge close to a totally reflecting face: it scattered light.
> - This is the optical counterpart of a quantum particle meeting a potential step higher than its energy, whose wave function decays into the step ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-4|QM Theorem §A4.6.4]]), and of tunnelling through a thin barrier ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^rem-a4-6-3|QM §A4.6, Remark: What controls tunnelling]]).
> - Without a second medium the evanescent energy returns to the first: a narrow totally reflected beam is displaced sideways along the surface by a small fraction of a wavelength (the Goos–Hänchen shift, studied by Goos and Hänchen in 1947).
> - Light guided by repeated total reflection in a fibre ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^ex-b9-1-1|Example §B9.1.1]]) has an evanescent tail in the cladding; in fibres a few micrometres across only certain field patterns, the modes, propagate, and the fibre becomes an optical waveguide.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.7 (fibre optics) and §2.9, Figures 2.15–2.16 and footnote 5*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B9.3.1: How Far the Light Reaches
> Light of vacuum wavelength $500\ \mathrm{nm}$ is totally reflected inside a $45^\circ$–$90^\circ$–$45^\circ$ glass prism ($n_1 = 1.5$), meeting the hypotenuse face at $45^\circ > \theta_c = 41.8^\circ$. By [[§B9.3 Total Internal Reflection and the Evanescent Wave#^thm-b9-3-1|Theorem §B9.3.1]],
>
> $$
> \beta = \frac{2\pi}{500\ \mathrm{nm}}\sqrt{(1.5\sin 45^\circ)^2 - 1} = \frac{2\pi}{500\ \mathrm{nm}}\sqrt{1.125 - 1} = \frac{2\pi \times 0.3536}{500\ \mathrm{nm}} = 4.44 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} ,
> $$
>
> so the amplitude falls to $1/e$ at $1/\beta = 225\ \mathrm{nm}$ from the surface, $0.45$ vacuum wavelengths. At $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ the irradiance is reduced by $e^{-2\beta(1\ \mathrm{mm})} = e^{-8886} \approx 10^{-3859}$: nothing at all. The phase fronts of the evanescent wave slide along the face at $c/(1.5\sin 45^\circ) = 0.943c$.
>
> *Source: Fowles Problem 2.19*

^ex-b9-3-1

> [!example] Example §B9.3.2: Fresnel's Rhomb
> Two total reflections, each with $\Delta = 45^\circ$, give a relative retardation of $90^\circ$ between p and s: a quarter-wave retarder ([[§B9.3 Total Internal Reflection and the Evanescent Wave#^thm-b9-3-2|Theorem §B9.3.2]]). In Fresnel's rhomb, linear light polarized at $45^\circ$ to the plane of incidence enters one face normally, is totally reflected twice inside, and leaves through the opposite face circularly polarized. For glass with $n_1 = 1.50$ the largest attainable $\Delta$ is $2\tan^{-1}\bigl((1 - n^2)/2n\bigr) = 2\tan^{-1}(0.4167) = 45.2^\circ$, at $51.7^\circ$, just enough; solving $\tan(\Delta/2) = \tan 22.5^\circ$ gives the two working angles $50.2^\circ$ and $53.3^\circ$. Fowles quotes $54^\circ$, where $\Delta = 44.7^\circ$; for $n_1 = 1.51$ the angles are $48.6^\circ$ and $54.6^\circ$. Unlike a crystal quarter-wave plate ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^ex-b8-3-1|Example §B8.3.1]]), the rhomb's retardation depends on wavelength only through the slow dispersion of $n_1$: it is nearly achromatic. Because $\Delta$ is flat near its maximum, a rhomb cut near $\theta_m$ is also insensitive to the exact angle.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.10, Figure 2.18*

^ex-b9-3-2

> [!example] Example §B9.3.3: Circular Light Totally Reflected at 45°
> Right-circular light, $J_i = (1, -i)^T/\sqrt2$ in the frame $(\hat p_i, \hat s)$, meets a glass–air face ($n = 1/1.5$) from inside at $45^\circ$. Here $\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - n^2} = \sqrt{0.5 - 0.4444} = 0.2357$ and $\cos\theta = 0.7071$, so
>
> $$
> \tan\frac{\delta_s}{2} = 0.3333, \quad \delta_s = 36.87^\circ; \qquad \tan\frac{\delta_p}{2} = 0.7500, \quad \delta_p = 73.74^\circ; \qquad \Delta = 36.87^\circ .
> $$
>
> By [[§B9.3 Total Internal Reflection and the Evanescent Wave#^thm-b9-3-2|Theorem §B9.3.2]] the reflected Jones vector is $e^{-i\delta_s}(e^{-i\Delta}, -i)^T/\sqrt2$: equal amplitudes, relative phase $\delta = -90^\circ + 36.87^\circ = -53.13^\circ$. So all the light is reflected, still **right**-handed but elliptical, with axes at $\pm45^\circ$ to the plane of incidence and axis ratio $\tan(53.13^\circ/2) = 0.50$ ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]). The same light reflected externally at $45^\circ$ loses $95\%$ of its power and comes back left-handed ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^ex-b9-2-3|Example §B9.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.11 and Problem 2.22*

^ex-b9-3-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The evanescent wave is a plane wave with a complex wave vector, $\vec k\cdot\vec k = n_2^2\omega^2/c^2$ with $\vec k$ not real; the same objects describe absorbing media ([[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media|§B13.2]]), where the decay is caused by loss rather than by kinematics.
> - Total reflection as a retarder places the phase shifts of this section in the Jones calculus of [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus|§B8.2]]; the phase changes on reflection also enter the interference conditions of [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer|§B10.4]] and [[§B10.5 Multilayer Films|§B10.5]].
> - The wave function of a particle at a potential step with $E < U_0$ ([[§A4.6 Barriers, Steps and Tunnelling#^thm-a4-6-4|QM Theorem §A4.6.4]]) is the s-polarized field of this section with $\psi$ in place of $E_y$: an incident and a totally reflected wave with a phase shift, and an exponential tail.
