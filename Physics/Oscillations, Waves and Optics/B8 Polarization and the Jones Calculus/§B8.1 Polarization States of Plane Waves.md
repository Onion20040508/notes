*Sources: Fowles, Introduction to Modern Optics, §2.1–2.4 and §6.2–6.3 · Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lectures 13 and 14 · PHY 300 (Mihaly, Fall 2024).*

Which states of polarization can a monochromatic plane light wave have, and how are they described? Level A answered for linear polarization: the field oscillates along one direction perpendicular to the ray ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]), a consequence of the transversality of electromagnetic waves ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-2|EM Theorem §A12.2.2]]); it also noted that the two transverse components may oscillate out of phase ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^rem-a4-5-2|§A4.5, Remark: Linear polarization is one case]]). This section works in a transparent medium of index $n$ instead of vacuum, describes a wave by a complex vector amplitude, and proves that the most general state is elliptical, with linear and circular polarization as special cases. It fixes the conventions used in all of Chapters B8–B13: the optics sign convention for complex fields, and the naming of right- and left-handed light.

## Complex fields in optics

> [!caution] Caution: The optics sign convention, opposite to Chapters B1–B7
> From Chapter B8 on, a monochromatic field is written, as in Fowles, Schwartz's light lectures, quantum mechanics and Electromagnetism level B ([[§B10.1 Electromagnetic Waves in Vacuum#^cau-b10-1-1|EM Caution: Complex fields, and Waves and Optics' two conventions]]), as
>
> $$
> \vec E(\vec r, t) = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\vec E_0\,e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)}\bigr], \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \to -i\omega, \qquad \nabla \to i\vec k ,
> $$
>
> and the **complex refractive index** is $\tilde n = n + i\kappa$ with $\kappa \ge 0$ for an absorbing medium: a wave $e^{i(\tilde n\omega z/c - \omega t)}$ then decays as $e^{-\kappa\omega z/c}$ (Fowles §6.4, eqs. (6.28)–(6.31)). Chapters B1–B7 use the opposite convention $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat a\,e^{+i\omega t}\bigr]$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]]), and plane waves $e^{i(\omega t - \vec k\cdot\vec r)}$ ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]); the general warning is [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^cau-b1-1-1|§B1.1, Caution: Where the real-part rule fails, and sign conventions]].
> - The two descriptions of one real field have **complex-conjugate amplitudes**: $\operatorname{Re}[\hat a\,e^{i\omega t}] = \operatorname{Re}[\hat a^{\ast}e^{-i\omega t}]$. Every complex coefficient and every phase angle changes sign on translation: an inductor's impedance $i\omega L$ becomes $-i\omega L$, a retarder that multiplies the slow component by $e^{i\Gamma}$ here does so by $e^{-i\Gamma}$ there, the Jones vector of right-circular light is $(1, -i)$ here and $(1, i)$ there (Fowles's note to his Table 2.1), the complex index of an absorber is $n - i\kappa$ there, an evanescent wave $e^{-\beta z}$ has $k_z = +i\beta$ here and $-i\beta$ there, and the phase shifts on total internal reflection ([[§B9.3 Total Internal Reflection and the Evanescent Wave#^thm-b9-3-2|Theorem §B9.3.2]]) and the complex Fresnel coefficients of absorbing media reverse sign. Real Fresnel coefficients ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]) are the same in both.
> - A formula imported from B1–B7 must be conjugated (equivalently, $\omega \to -\omega$ in its complex response function) before use; the Lorentz model of dispersion ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^mod-b13-1-2|Model §B13.1.2]]) is the main case.
> - Real, measurable quantities do not depend on the convention: intensities, reflectances, rotation angles, and the handedness of polarization once it is defined through the real field ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-2|Def. §B8.1.2]]). Time averages are $\langle\operatorname{Re}\hat a\,\operatorname{Re}\hat b\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\hat a\hat b^*)$ in both conventions ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|Theorem §B1.1.6]]).
> - Schwartz switches within Lecture 14: §1–3 use $e^{i(kz - \omega t)}$, §7 (wave plates) uses $e^{i\omega(t - nz/c)}$. His results are right in each part, but phases cannot be carried from one part to the other.

> [!model] Model §B8.1.1: Linear Isotropic Dielectric
> A **linear isotropic dielectric** is a medium without free charges or currents whose polarization is proportional to the field, $\vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi\vec E$, with a real scalar susceptibility $\chi$ at the frequency considered, and which is non-magnetic. Its macroscopic Maxwell equations, written with $\vec H = \vec B/\mu_0$ and the permittivity $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(1 + \chi)$, are
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec E = -\mu_0\frac{\partial\vec H}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla\times\vec H = \varepsilon\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec E = 0, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec H = 0 ,
> $$
>
> the vacuum equations ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|EM Principle §A12.1.3]], here in differential form) with $\varepsilon_0$ replaced by $\varepsilon$ ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^rem-a4-4-2|EM §A4.4, Remark: Permittivity]]). The **index of refraction** is $n = \sqrt{\varepsilon/\varepsilon_0} = \sqrt{1 + \chi}$.
>
> - *Assumes:* response linear ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^law-a4-4-1|EM Law §A4.4.1]], extended to optical frequencies; level B: [[§B5.3 Linear Dielectrics#^law-b5-3-1|EM Law §B5.3.1]]), isotropic (the same $\chi$ for every direction of $\vec E$), lossless ($\chi$ real), local, and $\mu = \mu_0$ (Schwartz, Lecture 15 §2, quotes $\mu = 1.256 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{H/m}$ for water, the vacuum value to the digits given); one frequency at a time, since $\chi$ depends on $\omega$ (dispersion, [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]]).
> - *Fails:* in crystals, where $\chi$ is a tensor ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-1|Model §B8.3.1]]); in absorbing media and metals, where $\chi$ is complex or a conduction current flows ([[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media|§B13.2]]); at high intensities (nonlinear optics). The macroscopic equations themselves are derived in Electromagnetism level B: the auxiliary fields are [[§B5.2 The Electric Displacement#^def-b5-2-2|EM Def. §B5.2.2]] and [[§B7.2 The Auxiliary Field H and Magnetic Media#^def-b7-2-2|EM Def. §B7.2.2]], the general equations [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-5|EM Theorem §B8.4.5]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.1, eqs. (2.7)–(2.10), and §6.2–6.3*

^mod-b8-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.2: Plane Harmonic Waves in a Dielectric
> In the medium of [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|Model §B8.1.1]], the fields $\vec E = \vec E_0e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)}$, $\vec H = \vec H_0e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)}$, with constant complex amplitudes and a real wave vector, solve Maxwell's equations if and only if
>
> $$
> \vec k\cdot\vec E_0 = 0, \qquad \vec k\cdot\vec H_0 = 0, \qquad \vec k\times\vec E_0 = \mu_0\omega\vec H_0, \qquad \vec k\times\vec H_0 = -\varepsilon\omega\vec E_0 .
> $$
>
> Consequently $\lvert\vec k\rvert = n\omega/c$ (phase velocity $v = c/n$), both fields are transverse, and
>
> $$
> \vec H_0 = \frac{n}{Z_0}\,\hat k\times\vec E_0, \qquad Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0}} = \mu_0c = 376.7\ \Omega ,
> $$
>
> so $\hat k$, $\operatorname{Re}\vec E$ and $\operatorname{Re}\vec H$ form a right-handed orthogonal triad and the field ratio is $\lvert\vec H\rvert/\lvert\vec E\rvert = n/Z_0$. $Z_0$ is the **impedance of free space**; the medium has $E/H = Z_0/n$, which is why light passing from air into glass ($n = 1.5$) has its ratio of magnetic to electric field raised by the factor $1.5$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.1, eqs. (2.11)–(2.16) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 13 §3*

^thm-b8-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> On $e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)}$, $\partial_t$ acts as multiplication by $-i\omega$ and $\partial_x$ as multiplication by $ik_x$ (and likewise for $y$, $z$), so $\nabla\cdot\vec E = i\vec k\cdot\vec E$ and $\nabla\times\vec E = i\vec k\times\vec E$ (Fowles eqs. (2.2)–(2.6)). The four Maxwell equations become the four algebraic conditions, after cancelling the common nonzero factor $ie^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)}$. Conversely, those conditions make every equation hold at every point. Taking $\vec k\times$ of the third and using the fourth,
>
> $$
> \vec k\times(\vec k\times\vec E_0) = \mu_0\omega\,\vec k\times\vec H_0 = -\mu_0\varepsilon\omega^2\vec E_0 ,
> $$
>
> while the left side is $\vec k(\vec k\cdot\vec E_0) - k^2\vec E_0 = -k^2\vec E_0$. A nonzero wave therefore needs $k^2 = \mu_0\varepsilon\omega^2 = n^2\omega^2/c^2$, with $c^2 = 1/(\mu_0\varepsilon_0)$. Then $\vec H_0 = (k/\mu_0\omega)\,\hat k\times\vec E_0 = (n/\mu_0c)\,\hat k\times\vec E_0$, and $\mu_0c = \mu_0/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = Z_0$. For real parts, $\operatorname{Re}\vec H = (n/Z_0)\,\hat k\times\operatorname{Re}\vec E$ at every point and instant, since $\hat k$ and $n/Z_0$ are real; $\hat k\times\vec E \perp \vec E$ and $\lvert\hat k\times\vec E\rvert = \lvert\vec E\rvert$ for $\vec E \perp \hat k$. Numerically $Z_0 = \sqrt{(4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{H/m})/(8.854 \times 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m})} = 376.7\ \Omega$.

> [!remark] Remark: What is new compared with level A
> In vacuum this is [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-5|EM Theorem §A12.2.5]] ($n = 1$, $B = E/c$, i.e. $H = E/Z_0$). Two things are added. In a medium, $v = c/n$ and $H/E = n/Z_0$: the electric and magnetic parts of the wave are reweighted, which is what makes reflection at an interface depend on $n$ ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]). And the amplitude $\vec E_0$ is a *complex* vector, two complex numbers for a wave along $z$: their moduli and their phase difference are the polarization.

## States of polarization

> [!definition] Definition §B8.1.1: Complex Vector Amplitude; State of Polarization
> For a plane wave travelling along $+z$, write the complex vector amplitude as
>
> $$
> \vec E_0 = \hat x\,E_{0x} + \hat y\,E_{0y}, \qquad E_{0x} = a\,e^{i\varphi_x}, \quad E_{0y} = b\,e^{i\varphi_y}, \quad a, b \ge 0 ,
> $$
>
> and let $\delta = \varphi_y - \varphi_x$ be the **relative phase**. The **state of polarization** is what is left of $\vec E_0$ after the overall size and the overall phase are discarded: the ratio $b/a$ together with $\delta$ (modulo $2\pi$). The wave is
> - **linearly polarized** if $\delta = 0$ or $\pi$, or if $a = 0$ or $b = 0$: equivalently, $\vec E_0$ is a real vector times one complex number;
> - **circularly polarized** if $a = b$ and $\delta = \pm\pi/2$;
> - **elliptically polarized** otherwise.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.3–2.4, eqs. (2.24), (2.31)–(2.32) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 §1 and §3, eq. (20)*

^def-b8-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B8.1.2: Right- and Left-Handed Polarization
> Look at a fixed plane $z = \text{const}$ while the wave comes toward you (you face the source). If the tip of the real field $\operatorname{Re}\vec E$ turns **clockwise** as time goes on, the light is **right-handed** (right-circularly or right-elliptically polarized); if it turns **counterclockwise**, it is **left-handed**. With $\hat x$ to the right and $\hat y$ up for this observer, $\hat z$ points toward the observer, and
>
> $$
> \text{right circular:}\ \ \vec E = E_0\bigl[\hat x\cos(kz - \omega t) + \hat y\sin(kz - \omega t)\bigr] = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[E_0(\hat x - i\hat y)\,e^{i(kz - \omega t)}\bigr],
> $$
>
> $$
> \text{left circular:}\ \ \vec E = E_0\bigl[\hat x\cos(kz - \omega t) - \hat y\sin(kz - \omega t)\bigr] = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[E_0(\hat x + i\hat y)\,e^{i(kz - \omega t)}\bigr].
> $$
>
> At a fixed instant the tips of the right-circular field vectors along $z$ lie on a right-handed helix, of the left-circular ones on a left-handed helix.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.4, eqs. (2.28)–(2.30) and Figure 2.5 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 §3, eqs. (15)–(18)*

^def-b8-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Handedness names are not universal
> - The definition above (Fowles; Schwartz uses the same names) is the traditional optics one. Particle physics and many modern texts name circular polarization by **helicity**, the sense of rotation relative to the direction of travel: positive helicity, often called "right-handed", is the counterclockwise rotation seen by the oncoming observer, i.e. **left**-circular in the sense of Def. §B8.1.2. Check which convention a source uses before comparing.
> - Defined through the real field, handedness does not depend on the complex convention ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-1|Caution: The optics sign convention]]); the complex vectors do. In the $e^{+i\omega t}$ convention right-circular light is $\hat x + i\hat y$, as Fowles warns (his §2.4).
> - A circularly polarized beam reflected back toward its source changes handedness, although nothing rotates differently: the direction of travel, hence the observer's side, is reversed ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-5|Theorem §B9.2.5]]).

^cau-b8-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.3: The Polarization Ellipse
> At a fixed $z$ the tip of the real field $(E_x, E_y) = \bigl(a\cos\tau,\ b\cos(\tau + \delta)\bigr)$, $\tau = kz - \omega t + \varphi_x$, traces the curve
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{E_x}{a}\Bigr)^2 - 2\,\frac{E_x}{a}\,\frac{E_y}{b}\cos\delta + \Bigl(\frac{E_y}{b}\Bigr)^2 = \sin^2\delta ,
> $$
>
> an ellipse inscribed in the rectangle $\lvert E_x\rvert \le a$, $\lvert E_y\rvert \le b$, once per period.
> 1. It degenerates to a segment (linear polarization) for $\delta = 0, \pi$, and is a circle for $a = b$, $\delta = \pm\pi/2$.
> 2. Its axes make the angle $\psi$ with the $x$-axis given by
>    $$
>    \tan 2\psi = \frac{2ab\cos\delta}{a^2 - b^2} ;
>    $$
>    they lie along $x$ and $y$ when $\delta = \pm\pi/2$, and at $\pm 45^\circ$ when $a = b$.
> 3. The sense of rotation is fixed by the sign of $\sin\delta$: the light is **left-handed** for $0 < \delta < \pi$ and **right-handed** for $-\pi < \delta < 0$ (Def. §B8.1.2).
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.4 and Problem 2.9 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 §3*

^thm-b8-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The real field is $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[ae^{i\varphi_x}e^{i(kz - \omega t)}\bigr] = a\cos\tau$ and $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[be^{i\varphi_y}e^{i(kz - \omega t)}\bigr] = b\cos(\tau + \delta)$. Expand: $E_y/b = \cos\tau\cos\delta - \sin\tau\sin\delta$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{E_y}{b} - \frac{E_x}{a}\cos\delta = -\sin\tau\sin\delta, \qquad \frac{E_x}{a}\sin\delta = \cos\tau\sin\delta .
> $$
>
> Squaring and adding eliminates $\tau$ and gives the equation of the curve. It is a quadratic form $Q(E_x, E_y) = \sin^2\delta$ with the symmetric matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1/a^2 & -\cos\delta/ab \\ -\cos\delta/ab & 1/b^2 \end{pmatrix}$, of determinant $\sin^2\delta/(a^2b^2) \ge 0$; for $\sin\delta \ne 0$ both eigenvalues are positive and the curve is an ellipse whose axes are the orthonormal eigenvectors of the matrix ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]). For $\sin\delta = 0$ the form is a perfect square, $(E_x/a \mp E_y/b)^2 = 0$: a segment.
>
> *Axes.* In coordinates rotated by $\psi$, a form $Ax^2 + 2Bxy + Cy^2$ acquires the cross term $\bigl[2B\cos 2\psi - (A - C)\sin 2\psi\bigr]x'y'$, which vanishes when $\tan 2\psi = 2B/(A - C)$. With $A = 1/a^2$, $C = 1/b^2$, $B = -\cos\delta/ab$ this is the stated formula. For $\delta = \pm\pi/2$ there is no cross term, so the axes are $x$ and $y$; for $a = b$, $\tan 2\psi = \pm\infty$.
>
> *Sense.* The $z$-component of $\vec E\times\dot{\vec E}$ tells which way the tip turns: positive means from $\hat x$ toward $\hat y$, counterclockwise for the observer facing the wave. With $\dot\tau = -\omega$,
>
> $$
> E_x\dot E_y - E_y\dot E_x = ab\,\omega\bigl[\cos\tau\sin(\tau + \delta) - \cos(\tau + \delta)\sin\tau\bigr] = ab\,\omega\sin\delta ,
> $$
>
> of constant sign. So $\sin\delta > 0$ is counterclockwise (left), $\sin\delta < 0$ clockwise (right). Check: $\hat x + i\hat y$ has $\delta = +\pi/2$, left, as in Def. §B8.1.2.

> [!remark] Remark: Linear and circular states as superpositions
> Adding the two circular amplitudes of [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-2|Def. §B8.1.2]] gives $(\hat x - i\hat y) + (\hat x + i\hat y) = 2\hat x$: equal right and left circular waves make a linear one; subtracting gives $\hat y$, and a relative phase between them turns the line (optical activity, [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]]). Conversely $\hat x \pm i\hat y$ is a sum of two linear waves a quarter period apart. Linear and circular states are two bases for the same two-dimensional space of amplitudes ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]]). Circularly polarized light carries angular momentum along $\hat k$; quantized, each photon ([[§A1.2 The Photoelectric Effect#^pr-a1-2-3|QM Principle §A1.2.3]]) carries $\pm\hbar$, positive for left-circular light in the naming of Def. §B8.1.2. Linear light, an equal mixture, carries none.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 §3, eq. (19) · Fowles §2.5*

## Energy flow

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.4: Irradiance of a Plane Wave
> For the plane wave of [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]] the time-averaged Poynting vector $\vec S = \vec E\times\vec H$ ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-1|EM Def. §A12.3.1]]) is
>
> $$
> \langle\vec S\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\vec E_0\times\vec H_0^*\bigr) = I\,\hat k, \qquad I = \frac{n}{2Z_0}\,\lvert\vec E_0\rvert^2 = \frac{n}{2Z_0}\bigl(a^2 + b^2\bigr) = \tfrac12 nc\varepsilon_0\bigl(\lvert E_{0x}\rvert^2 + \lvert E_{0y}\rvert^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The **irradiance** $I$ is proportional to $n$ times the squared amplitude, and it does not depend on the relative phase $\delta$: the irradiances of two orthogonal linear components add, whatever the state. Energy flows along $\hat k$ in an isotropic medium (not in a crystal, [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^rem-b8-3-1|§B8.3, Remark: Walk-off, double refraction and polarizing prisms]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.2, eqs. (2.17)–(2.23) and Problem 2.4*

^thm-b8-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each Cartesian component of $\vec E$ and $\vec H$ is a sinusoid of frequency $\omega$ with complex amplitude given by the corresponding component of $\vec E_0e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}$ or $\vec H_0e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}$. The Poynting vector is a sum of products of such components, and the time average of a product of two sinusoids is $\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\hat a\hat b^*)$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|Theorem §B1.1.6]], which holds unchanged in the optics convention because $\operatorname{Re}(\hat a\hat b^*) = \operatorname{Re}(\hat a^*\hat b)$); the factors $e^{\pm i\vec k\cdot\vec r}$ cancel for real $\vec k$. Hence $\langle\vec S\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\vec E_0\times\vec H_0^*)$. Insert $\vec H_0^* = (n/Z_0)\,\hat k\times\vec E_0^*$ and use $\vec E_0\times(\hat k\times\vec E_0^*) = \hat k\,(\vec E_0\cdot\vec E_0^*) - \vec E_0^*(\vec E_0\cdot\hat k) = \hat k\,\lvert\vec E_0\rvert^2$, since $\vec E_0\cdot\hat k = 0$. So $\langle\vec S\rangle = (n/2Z_0)\lvert\vec E_0\rvert^2\hat k$, with $\lvert\vec E_0\rvert^2 = \lvert E_{0x}\rvert^2 + \lvert E_{0y}\rvert^2 = a^2 + b^2$, free of $\delta$. Finally $1/Z_0 = c\varepsilon_0$.

^der-b8-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]], [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-1|EM Def. §A12.3.1]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|Theorem §B1.1.6]]

> [!remark] Remark: Irradiance and intensity
> For $n = 1$ this is the intensity $\tfrac12c\varepsilon_0E_0^2$ of [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]]. Fowles reserves "intensity" for power per solid angle and calls power per unit area the irradiance; level A and most physics texts call the same quantity intensity ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-2|EM Def. §A12.3.2]]). The factor $n$ matters at interfaces: a wave of the same $\lvert\vec E_0\rvert$ carries $n$ times more power in glass than in air, because its magnetic field is $n$ times larger.

## Unpolarized and partially polarized light

> [!definition] Definition §B8.1.3: Unpolarized and Partially Polarized Light; Degree of Polarization
> Light from thermal sources is not a single plane wave: its amplitude $\vec E_0$ drifts randomly, on the time scale of the coherence time ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^def-b10-2-2|Def. §B10.2.2]]), slowly compared with the period but far faster than any detector responds. It is **unpolarized** if no state of polarization is preferred on average, **partially polarized** if it is an incoherent mixture of an unpolarized part of irradiance $I_u$ and a polarized part of irradiance $I_p$. Its **degree of polarization** is
>
> $$
> P = \frac{I_p}{I_p + I_u}, \qquad 0 \le P \le 1 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.3, eq. (2.26)*

^def-b8-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.1.5: Degree of Polarization from a Rotating Analyzer
> If the polarized part of partially polarized light is linearly polarized, and an ideal linear polarizer ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^mod-a4-5-1|Model §A4.5.1]]) is turned through a full circle in the beam, the transmitted irradiance varies between $I_{\max}$ and $I_{\min}$, and
>
> $$
> P = \frac{I_{\max} - I_{\min}}{I_{\max} + I_{\min}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.3, eq. (2.27)*

^thm-b8-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The two parts are incoherent, so their transmitted irradiances add. The unpolarized part passes $\tfrac12I_u$ at every orientation ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]); the polarized part, linear at angle $\theta_0$, passes $I_p\cos^2(\theta - \theta_0)$ ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]]). So $I(\theta) = \tfrac12I_u + I_p\cos^2(\theta - \theta_0)$, with $I_{\max} = \tfrac12I_u + I_p$ and $I_{\min} = \tfrac12I_u$. Then $I_{\max} - I_{\min} = I_p$ and $I_{\max} + I_{\min} = I_u + I_p$.

^der-b8-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-3|Def. §B8.1.3]], [[§A4.5 Polarization#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]], [[§A4.5 Polarization#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Partial elliptical polarization
> If the polarized part is elliptical, a linear analyzer alone sees only the ellipse's axes: $I_{\max} - I_{\min}$ is then smaller than $I_p$ (zero for circular light, which a rotating polarizer cannot tell from unpolarized light). Measuring $P$ in general needs a retarder in front of the analyzer ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus|§B8.2]]). Skylight scattered at $90^\circ$ from the Sun is partially linearly polarized, with $P$ that can exceed $50\%$ (Fowles §2.3), i.e. $I_{\max}/I_{\min} > 3$.

^cau-b8-1-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.1.1: Reading Off a State of Polarization
> A wave has the real field $\vec E = E_0\bigl[\hat x\cos(kz - \omega t) + \hat y\,b\cos(kz - \omega t + \phi)\bigr]$. Since $b\cos(\tau + \phi) = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[be^{i\phi}e^{i\tau}\bigr]$, its complex amplitude is $\vec E_0 = E_0(\hat x + be^{i\phi}\hat y)$. By [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]:
> - $\phi = 0$, $b = 1$: $\delta = 0$, linear along the diagonal at $45^\circ$.
> - $\phi = 0$, $b = 2$: linear, at $\tan^{-1}2 = 63.4^\circ$ from $x$.
> - $\phi = \pi/2$, $b = -1$: $E_{0y} = -e^{i\pi/2} = e^{-i\pi/2}$, so $a = b$ in modulus and $\delta = -\pi/2$: right circular. Indeed $-\cos(\tau + \pi/2) = \sin\tau$, the field of Def. §B8.1.2.
> - $\phi = \pi/4$, $b = 1$: $\delta = \pi/4$, $\sin\delta > 0$, so **left elliptical**. With $a = b$ the axes are at $\pm45^\circ$. Along them, $u = (E_x + E_y)/\sqrt2 = \sqrt2\,E_0\cos(\delta/2)\cos(\tau + \delta/2)$ and $v = (E_y - E_x)/\sqrt2 = -\sqrt2\,E_0\sin(\delta/2)\sin(\tau + \delta/2)$: semi-axes $\sqrt2\cos 22.5^\circ\,E_0 = 1.307E_0$ along $+45^\circ$ and $\sqrt2\sin 22.5^\circ\,E_0 = 0.541E_0$ along $-45^\circ$, axis ratio $\tan 22.5^\circ = 0.414$.
>
> In general, for $a = b$ the axis ratio is $\tan(\delta/2)$ (for $0 \le \delta \le \pi$): the ellipse opens from a line at $\delta = 0$ to a circle at $\pi/2$.
>
> *Source: Fowles Problems 2.5–2.6*

^ex-b8-1-1

> [!example] Example §B8.1.2: Fields at a Laser Focus
> A ruby laser pulse of peak power $100\ \mathrm{MW}$ is focused in air ($n = 1$) to a spot $10\ \mu\mathrm m$ in diameter. Taking the power as spread uniformly over the spot, the irradiance is
>
> $$
> I = \frac{P}{\pi r^2} = \frac{1.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm W}{\pi\,(5.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm m)^2} = 1.27 \times 10^{18}\ \mathrm{W/m^2} .
> $$
>
> By [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-4|Theorem §B8.1.4]], whatever the polarization,
>
> $$
> \lvert\vec E_0\rvert = \sqrt{\frac{2Z_0I}{n}} = \sqrt{2(376.7\ \Omega)(1.27 \times 10^{18}\ \mathrm{W/m^2})} = 3.1 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{V/m}, \qquad \lvert\vec H_0\rvert = \frac{n\lvert\vec E_0\rvert}{Z_0} = 8.2 \times 10^7\ \mathrm{A/m} .
> $$
>
> Inside glass ($n = 1.5$) at the same irradiance the electric amplitude would be smaller by $\sqrt{1.5}$ and the magnetic one larger by $\sqrt{1.5}$. Fields this large ionize matter; they are where nonlinear optics begins.
>
> *Source: Fowles Problem 2.3 · PHY 300, Worksheet 8, question 1 ($Z_0$)*

^ex-b8-1-2
