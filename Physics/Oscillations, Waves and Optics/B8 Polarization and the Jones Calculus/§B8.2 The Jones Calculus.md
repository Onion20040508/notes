How does a train of polarizers and plates change the polarization of a beam, and which states pass through an element unchanged? Level A handled chains of polarizers by applying Malus's law sheet by sheet ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]], [[§A4.5 Polarization#^ex-a4-5-2|Example §A4.5.2]]), which works only for linear states and absorbing sheets. Here the complex amplitude of [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]] becomes a vector in $\mathbb C^2$, every linear element a $2 \times 2$ matrix, and a train of elements a matrix product. Orthogonal states, eigenstates of an element and lossless elements are then statements about the Hermitian inner product, eigenvectors and unitary matrices. Complex amplitudes follow the optics convention $e^{i(kz - \omega t)}$ ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-1|§B8.1, Caution: The optics sign convention]]).

## Jones vectors

> [!definition] Definition §B8.2.1: Jones Vector
> The **Jones vector** of a plane wave travelling along $+z$ with complex amplitude $\vec E_0 = \hat x E_{0x} + \hat y E_{0y}$ is the column
>
> $$
> J = \begin{pmatrix} E_{0x} \\ E_{0y} \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb C^2 .
> $$
>
> Nonzero multiples of $J$ describe the same state of polarization; $J^\dagger J = \lvert E_{0x}\rvert^2 + \lvert E_{0y}\rvert^2$ is proportional to the irradiance ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-4|Theorem §B8.1.4]]), and $J$ is **normalized** when $J^\dagger J = 1$. Normalized Jones vectors of the common states:
>
> | state | Jones vector |
> |---|---|
> | linear along $x$; along $y$ | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$; $\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ |
> | linear at angle $\theta$ from $x$ (e.g. $\pm45^\circ$) | $\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \end{pmatrix}$, $\ \dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \pm1 \end{pmatrix}$ |
> | right circular | $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{pmatrix}$ |
> | left circular | $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{pmatrix}$ |
>
> Handedness as in [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-2|Def. §B8.1.2]]. Coherent waves of the same frequency and direction superpose by adding their Jones vectors.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.5, eq. (2.37) · PHY 300, Midterm 2 formula sheet*

^def-b8-2-1

> [!definition] Definition §B8.2.2: Orthogonal States of Polarization
> Two states with Jones vectors $J_1 = (A_1, B_1)^T$ and $J_2 = (A_2, B_2)^T$ are **orthogonal** if
>
> $$
> \langle J_1, J_2\rangle = A_1A_2^* + B_1B_2^* = 0 ,
> $$
>
> i.e. $\vec E_{01}\cdot\vec E_{02}^* = 0$: they are orthogonal for the standard Hermitian inner product of $\mathbb C^2$ ([[§20 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-2|LADR Def. 6.2]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.5, eq. (2.40)*

^def-b8-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.1: Orthogonal Decomposition of Polarization States
> 1. Every state $J = (A, B)^T$ has exactly one orthogonal state, $J^\perp = (-B^{\ast}, A^{\ast})^T$ (up to a factor). Its polarization ellipse has the same shape and the same axes as that of $J$, with the major and minor axes exchanged, and the **opposite handedness**. For linear light, $J^\perp$ is linear at right angles; right and left circular are orthogonal.
> 2. For any orthonormal pair of states $e_1$, $e_2$ (normalized and orthogonal), every $J$ splits as
>    $$
>    J = \langle J, e_1\rangle\,e_1 + \langle J, e_2\rangle\,e_2, \qquad J^\dagger J = \lvert\langle J, e_1\rangle\rvert^2 + \lvert\langle J, e_2\rangle\rvert^2 ,
>    $$
>    so any light can be resolved into two orthogonally polarized waves whose irradiances add. Examples: $\hat x = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\bigl(e_R + e_L\bigr)$ with $e_{R,L} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, \mp i)^T$, half the irradiance in each; right circular light is half $x$- and half $y$-polarized.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.5 and Problem 2.8 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 §3*

^thm-b8-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1. $\langle J, J^\perp\rangle = A(-B^*)^* + B(A^*)^* = -AB + BA = 0$. In $\mathbb C^2$ the vectors orthogonal to a nonzero $J$ form a one-dimensional subspace (the orthogonal complement of a line), so $J^\perp$ is unique up to a factor. With $A = ae^{i\varphi_x}$, $B = be^{i\varphi_y}$, the components of $J^\perp$ have moduli $b$, $a$ and phases $\pi - \varphi_y$, $-\varphi_x$, so its relative phase is $\delta' = -\varphi_x - (\pi - \varphi_y) = \delta - \pi$. By [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]: $\sin\delta' = -\sin\delta$ (opposite handedness). For the shape, note that $J^\perp$ is $J^* = (A^*, B^*)^T$ turned through $90^\circ$ by the rotation $(u, v) \mapsto (-v, u)$. The amplitude $J^*$ traces the same curve as $J$, in the opposite sense, because $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[A^*e^{-i\omega t}\bigr] = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[Ae^{i\omega t}\bigr]$ is the same field with $t \to -t$. The rotation then turns that curve rigidly through $90^\circ$. So the ellipse of $J^\perp$ has the same axis lines as that of $J$, its major axis lying along the minor axis of $J$. For real $J = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, $J^\perp = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$; and $\langle(1, -i), (1, i)\rangle = 1 + (-i)(-i) = 1 - 1 = 0$.
> 2. An orthonormal pair in the two-dimensional space $\mathbb C^2$ is an orthonormal basis, and the expansion and the sum of squares are [[§21 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-30|LADR Thm. 6.30]] and [[§21 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-24|LADR Thm. 6.24]]. For $\hat x$: $\langle(1,0), e_{R,L}\rangle = 1/\sqrt2$.

## Jones matrices

> [!model] Model §B8.2.2: Linear Polarization Element
> A **linear polarization element** (polarizer, wave plate, rotator, …) acts on a fully polarized plane wave crossing it along $z$ by a fixed complex $2 \times 2$ matrix, its **Jones matrix** $M$:
>
> $$
> J_{\text{out}} = M\,J_{\text{in}}, \qquad J_{\text{out}} = M_N\cdots M_2M_1\,J_{\text{in}} \ \ \text{for elements } 1, 2, \dots, N \text{ in the order met.}
> $$
>
> If the element is turned about $z$ by the angle $\theta$, its matrix in the fixed $xy$ frame becomes $R(\theta)\,M\,R(-\theta)$, with the rotation matrix $R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$.
>
> - *Assumes:* monochromatic light, fully polarized; normal incidence; the element is linear and does not depolarize; the frame $(\hat x, \hat y, \hat z)$ is right-handed with $\hat z$ along the beam on both sides. Overall phase factors and normalizations of $M$ matter only for irradiances and for interference with other beams.
> - *Fails:* for unpolarized or partially polarized light, which has no Jones vector ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^rem-b8-2-2|Remark: Unpolarized light in the Jones calculus]]); for depolarizing elements (diffusers, scattering); for oblique incidence unless the angle is built into $M$ (the reflection matrix of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-5|Theorem §B9.2.5]], whose reflected beam travels the other way).
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.5, eqs. (2.38)–(2.39) · PHY 300, Worksheet 8, questions 3–7*

^mod-b8-2-2

> [!definition] Definition §B8.2.3: Linear Retarder; Fast and Slow Axes; Retardance
> A **linear retarder** (wave plate) has two perpendicular axes in its plane. Light polarized along either axis passes with its polarization unchanged, and the component along the **slow axis** emerges delayed in phase by the **retardance** $\Gamma$ relative to the component along the **fast axis**. With the fast axis along $x$, its Jones matrix is, up to an overall phase,
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & e^{i\Gamma} \end{pmatrix} ;
> $$
>
> $\Gamma = \pi/2$ is a **quarter-wave plate**, $\Gamma = \pi$ a **half-wave plate**. (In the optics convention a delay multiplies the complex amplitude by $e^{+i\Gamma}$: $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[e^{i\Gamma}e^{-i\omega t}\bigr] = \cos(\omega t - \Gamma)$ peaks a time $\Gamma/\omega$ later.) A plate of thickness $d$ with indices $n_s > n_f$ for light polarized along its slow and fast axes has $\Gamma = 2\pi(n_s - n_f)\,d/\lambda_0$ ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-3|Model §B8.3.3]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.4, eq. (2.33), and Table 2.1 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 §7*

^def-b8-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.3: Jones Matrices of Standard Elements
> 1. **Linear polarizer** with transmission axis along $u = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)^T$:
>    $$
>    P(\theta) = u\,u^T = \begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\theta & \cos\theta\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta\sin\theta & \sin^2\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad P(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad P(\pm45^\circ) = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \pm1 \\ \pm1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .
>    $$
> 2. **Retarder** of retardance $\Gamma$ with fast axis at $\theta$:
>    $$
>    W_\Gamma(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\theta + e^{i\Gamma}\sin^2\theta & (1 - e^{i\Gamma})\sin\theta\cos\theta \\ (1 - e^{i\Gamma})\sin\theta\cos\theta & \sin^2\theta + e^{i\Gamma}\cos^2\theta \end{pmatrix} .
>    $$
>    Up to overall phases: quarter-wave plate with fast axis along $x$: $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix}$; along $y$: $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{pmatrix}$; at $\pm45^\circ$: $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \mp i \\ \mp i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Half-wave plate with fast axis along $x$ or $y$: $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$.
> 3. **Ideal polarizer for an arbitrary state** $e$ (normalized), transmitting $e$ fully and absorbing the orthogonal state: $P_e = e\,e^\dagger$. For $e = e_R$, $e_L$ this is the right and left **circular polarizer**,
>    $$
>    \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ i & 1 \end{pmatrix} .
>    $$
> 4. **Isotropic phase changer** $e^{i\varphi}I$, and **rotator** $R(\rho)$, which turns every linear state by $\rho$ counterclockwise as seen facing the wave (optical activity, [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.5, Table 2.1 · PHY 300, Worksheet 8, question 6; Midterm 2 formula sheet*

^thm-b8-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Polarizers.* An ideal polarizer for the state $e$ keeps the component of $J$ along $e$ and removes the orthogonal one ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^mod-a4-5-1|Model §A4.5.1]] for linear $e$). By [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]] that component is $\langle J, e\rangle e = e\,(e^\dagger J)$, so $M = ee^\dagger$. For real $e = u$, $e^\dagger = u^T$, and $uu^T$ has entries $u_iu_j$. For $e_R = (1, -i)^T/\sqrt2$, $e_Re_R^\dagger = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \end{pmatrix} = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $e_L$ likewise. One also checks $P(\theta) = R(\theta)P(0)R(-\theta)$, the rotation rule of [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^mod-b8-2-2|Model §B8.2.2]] (PHY 300, Worksheet 8, question 6).
>
> *Rotation rule and retarders.* In a frame turned by $\theta$, the coordinates of $J$ are $R(-\theta)J$; the element acts there by its own matrix $M$, and $R(\theta)$ converts back. Applied to $D = \operatorname{diag}(1, e^{i\Gamma})$ of [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^def-b8-2-3|Def. §B8.2.3]], with $c = \cos\theta$, $s = \sin\theta$,
>
> $$
> R(\theta)\,D\,R(-\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} c & -s \\ s & c \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} c & s \\ -se^{i\Gamma} & ce^{i\Gamma} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} c^2 + s^2e^{i\Gamma} & cs(1 - e^{i\Gamma}) \\ cs(1 - e^{i\Gamma}) & s^2 + c^2e^{i\Gamma} \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> Quarter-wave, $e^{i\Gamma} = i$: at $\theta = 90^\circ$, $\operatorname{diag}(i, 1) = i\operatorname{diag}(1, -i)$; at $\theta = \pm45^\circ$ the matrix is $\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 + i & \pm(1 - i) \\ \pm(1 - i) & 1 + i \end{pmatrix} = \tfrac{1 + i}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \mp i \\ \mp i & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, since $(1 - i)/(1 + i) = -i$, and $\lvert 1 + i\rvert/2 = 1/\sqrt2$. Half-wave, $e^{i\Gamma} = -1$: $\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ at $\theta = 0$ and $\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1) = -\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ at $90^\circ$.
>
> *Rotator.* $R(\rho)(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)^T = (\cos(\theta + \rho), \sin(\theta + \rho))^T$ by the addition formulas.

^der-b8-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^mod-b8-2-2|Model §B8.2.2]], [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^def-b8-2-3|Def. §B8.2.3]], [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]], [[§A4.5 Polarization#^mod-a4-5-1|Model §A4.5.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Tables of Jones matrices
> - Tables differ by overall phases and normalizations ($\operatorname{diag}(1, i)$ and $e^{i\pi/4}\operatorname{diag}(e^{-i\pi/4}, e^{i\pi/4})$ are the same quarter-wave plate). These matter only when the beam is later recombined with another one.
> - Every entry containing $i$ changes sign in the $e^{+i\omega t}$ convention (Fowles's note to Table 2.1). A quarter-wave plate with fast axis along $x$ is $\operatorname{diag}(1, i)$ here, $\operatorname{diag}(1, -i)$ there; both turn $45^\circ$ light into the same physical (left-circular) state.
> - Matrices multiply in the reverse of the order in which the light meets the elements, and they do not commute ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^ex-b8-2-2|Example §B8.2.2]]).

## Eigenstates

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.4: Eigenstates of a Jones Matrix
> A state $J \ne 0$ emerges from an element with its polarization unchanged exactly when $J$ is an eigenvector of $M$ ([[§14 Invariant Subspaces#^ladr-5-8|LADR Def. 5.8]]), $MJ = \lambda J$; writing $\lambda = \lvert\lambda\rvert e^{i\psi}$, the amplitude is multiplied by $\lvert\lambda\rvert$ and the phase advanced by $\psi$. The eigenvalues are the roots of the secular equation $\det(M - \lambda I) = 0$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-51|LADR Thm. 9.51]]). In particular:
> - linear polarizer $P(\theta)$: its axis ($\lambda = 1$) and the perpendicular direction ($\lambda = 0$); for $P(45^\circ)$, $(1, 1)^T$ and $(1, -1)^T$;
> - retarder: linear light along its fast axis ($\lambda = 1$) and slow axis ($\lambda = e^{i\Gamma}$); for a quarter-wave plate the two emerge a quarter period apart;
> - circular polarizer $e_Re_R^\dagger$: $e_R$ ($\lambda = 1$) and $e_L$ ($\lambda = 0$);
> - rotator $R(\rho)$: the circular states, $R(\rho)\,e_R = e^{i\rho}e_R$ and $R(\rho)\,e_L = e^{-i\rho}e_L$; it has no linear eigenstate unless $\rho$ is a multiple of $\pi$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.5, eqs. (2.41)–(2.43) and Problem 2.13*

^thm-b8-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $MJ$ describes the same state as $J$ iff $MJ$ is a multiple of $J$ (Def. §B8.2.1), including $0$ (fully absorbed). A nonzero solution of $(M - \lambda I)J = 0$ exists iff $M - \lambda I$ is not invertible, iff its determinant vanishes. For a projector $ee^\dagger$ with $e$ normalized, $ee^\dagger e = e$ and $ee^\dagger e^\perp = 0$: this covers the polarizers. A diagonal matrix has the basis vectors as eigenvectors: this covers the retarder in its own axes, and by the rotation rule its eigenvectors are the rotated axes. For the rotator, with $c = \cos\rho$, $s = \sin\rho$:
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix} c & -s \\ s & c \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \mp i \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} c \pm is \\ s \mp ic \end{pmatrix} = (c \pm is)\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \mp i \end{pmatrix} ,
> $$
>
> since $(c \pm is)(\mp i) = \mp ic + s$. The secular equation $(c - \lambda)^2 + s^2 = 0$ gives $\lambda = c \pm is$, real only if $s = 0$.

^der-b8-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]], [[§14 Invariant Subspaces#^ladr-5-8|LADR Def. 5.8]], [[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-51|LADR Thm. 9.51]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.2.5: Lossless Elements Are Unitary
> An element transmits the full irradiance of every input state, $\lVert MJ\rVert = \lVert J\rVert$ for all $J$, if and only if its Jones matrix is **unitary**, $M^\dagger M = I$ ([[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-57|LADR Thm. 7.57]]). A lossless element then has two **orthogonal** eigenstates with eigenvalues of modulus $1$ ([[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-55|LADR Thm. 7.55]]): in the basis of those two states it is a retarder, delaying one state against the orthogonal one. Retarders and rotators are of this kind (a rotator is a "circular retarder" with eigenstates $e_R$, $e_L$). Ideal polarizers are the opposite extreme, Hermitian projectors $M = M^\dagger = M^2$, whose eigenstates are also orthogonal; an element whose matrix is not normal (for example a polarizer followed by a wave plate at an angle) has no orthonormal basis of eigenstates: its eigenstates are not orthogonal, or there is only one ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.5 (eigenvectors) and Table 2.1; the unitary characterization is the linear algebra of LADR §26 applied to them*

^thm-b8-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\lVert MJ\rVert = \lVert J\rVert$ for all $J$ is condition (c) of [[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-57|LADR Thm. 7.57]], equivalent to (d) $M^\dagger M = MM^\dagger = I$. A unitary operator on the complex space $\mathbb C^2$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors with $\lvert\lambda\rvert = 1$ ([[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-55|LADR Thm. 7.55]]); writing $\lambda_{1,2} = e^{i\alpha}, e^{i\beta}$, the element is $e^{i\alpha}$ times a retarder of retardance $\beta - \alpha$ between the two eigenstates. $\operatorname{diag}(1, e^{i\Gamma})$ and $R(\rho)$ satisfy $M^\dagger M = I$ by inspection. For $P_e = ee^\dagger$: $P_e^\dagger = ee^\dagger$ and $P_e^2 = e(e^\dagger e)e^\dagger = P_e$. *Non-normal case:* a polarizer along $x$ followed by a quarter-wave plate with fast axis at $45^\circ$ has $M = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -i \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvectors $(1, -i)^T$ ($\lambda = 1/\sqrt2$) and $(0, 1)^T$ ($\lambda = 0$), whose inner product is $-i \ne 0$.

^der-b8-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]], [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-4|Theorem §B8.2.4]], [[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-57|LADR Thm. 7.57]], [[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-55|LADR Thm. 7.55]], [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]

> [!remark] Remark: Eigenstates as normal modes
> The eigenstates of a Jones matrix play the role of the normal modes of coupled oscillators ([[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange|§B2.2]]): states that keep their form and only acquire a factor. A birefringent crystal has linear eigenstates for each direction of propagation, an optically active one circular eigenstates ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity|§B8.3]]); finding them is the first step in any calculation, because in their basis the element is diagonal.

^rem-b8-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Unpolarized light in the Jones calculus
> Unpolarized light has no Jones vector (Fowles §2.5). It can still be handled as a random mixture of Jones vectors, averaging irradiances (not amplitudes) at the end. PHY 300 represents it by $J = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, e^{i\phi})^T$ with $\phi$ changing randomly in time. Through a polarizer at $\theta$ the transmitted irradiance is $\lvert u^TJ\rvert^2 = \tfrac12\lvert\cos\theta + e^{i\phi}\sin\theta\rvert^2 = \tfrac12(1 + \sin 2\theta\cos\phi)$; its average over a uniformly distributed $\phi$ is $\tfrac12$ for every $\theta$, recovering [[§A4.5 Polarization#^thm-a4-5-3|Theorem §A4.5.3]]. The same mixture sent through a retarder and then a polarizer still gives $\tfrac12$: no linear lossless element can polarize unpolarized light, only an absorbing (or reflecting) one can.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 8, question 8*

^rem-b8-2-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.2.1: A Jones Vector and a Circular Polarizer
> *(a)* A beam has $J = (1 + i,\ 1 - i)^T$. Factor out the first component: $J = (1 + i)\,(1,\ \tfrac{1 - i}{1 + i})^T$, and $\tfrac{1 - i}{1 + i} = \tfrac{(1 - i)^2}{2} = -i$. So $J \propto (1, -i)^T$: **right circular**.
>
> *(b)* An element has $M = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} i & -1 \\ 1 & i \end{pmatrix}$ and receives $x$-polarized light. Since $M = \tfrac{i}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & i \\ -i & 1 \end{pmatrix} = i\,e_Re_R^\dagger$, it is a right circular polarizer times a phase ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]]). Directly, $M(1, 0)^T = \tfrac12(i, 1)^T = \tfrac{i}{2}(1, -i)^T$: right circular, with $\tfrac14(1 + 1) = \tfrac12$ of the incident irradiance. Any input gives right circular light, with irradiance $\lvert\langle J, e_R\rangle\rvert^2$ ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]]); the typed solution applies $M$ to $(0, 1)^T$, which also yields right circular light with half the irradiance, so the answer stands. By [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-4|Theorem §B8.2.4]], $e_R$ passes unchanged (eigenvalue $i$) and $e_L$ is blocked.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Midterm 2, questions 3 and 4 (with typed solutions)*

^ex-b8-2-1

> [!example] Example §B8.2.2: The Order of Elements Matters
> Light polarized along $x + y$, $J = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)^T$, meets a half-wave plate with its fast axis vertical, $W = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ up to sign, and then a linear polarizer along $x - y$, $P(-45^\circ) = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$:
>
> $$
> P(-45^\circ)\,W J = \frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} :
> $$
>
> the plate turns the polarization from $+45^\circ$ to $-45^\circ$, which the polarizer passes completely. In the opposite order, $W\,P(-45^\circ)J = 0$: the polarizer is crossed with the incoming light, and nothing reaches the plate. The products differ, $P(-45^\circ)W = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix} \ne WP(-45^\circ) = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$. Likewise a linear polarizer followed by a quarter-wave plate at $45^\circ$ makes circular light, while the reverse order makes linear light (Fowles Problem 2.10).
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 8, questions 4–5 · Fowles Problem 2.10*

> [!example] Example §B8.2.3: Chains of Polarizers
> For two linear polarizers, $P(\theta_2)P(\theta_1) = u_2(u_2^Tu_1)u_1^T = \cos(\theta_2 - \theta_1)\,u_2u_1^T$: the light leaves along $u_2$ with amplitude reduced by $\cos(\theta_2 - \theta_1)$, which is Malus's law ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]]) in matrix form. For a chain, the amplitude factors multiply: after the first sheet passes $I_1$,
>
> $$
> I = I_1\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}\cos^2(\theta_{j+1} - \theta_j) .
> $$
>
> *Three sheets in the lab.* With the first sheet at $\theta_1$, the third at $90^\circ + \varepsilon$ and the middle one at $\theta$, $I = I_1\cos^2(\theta - \theta_1)\sin^2(\theta - \varepsilon)$, since $\cos(90^\circ + \varepsilon - \theta) = \sin(\theta - \varepsilon)$. This is the fit model of the user's PHY 300 polarization report, $I = \tfrac12I_0C_3\cos^2(\theta + \alpha)\sin^2(\theta + \beta)$, with $\alpha = -\theta_1$ and $\beta = -\varepsilon$ the misalignments of the outer sheets and $C_3$ the losses of real sheets. The fit gave $\alpha = -0.62^\circ$ and $\beta = -0.48^\circ$: the outer polarizers were aligned to better than a degree, well inside the stated $2.5^\circ$ reading uncertainty.
>
> *Many sheets.* To turn light linearly polarized at $0$ by $90^\circ$ with $N$ sheets at $\pi/2N, 2\pi/2N, \dots, \pi/2$, each a step $\pi/2N$ beyond the previous direction, the transmitted fraction is $\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)$: $0.25$ for $N = 2$ (level A's middle sheet between crossed ones, with $I_1$ counted after the first sheet), $0.42$ for $N = 3$, $0.78$ for $N = 10$, $0.976$ for $N = 100$. Since $\ln\cos x \approx -x^2/2$, $\ln\bigl[\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)\bigr] \approx -\pi^2/4N \to 0$: a fine enough staircase of absorbing sheets becomes a lossless rotator, the matrix $R(\pi/2)$ of [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]].
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 8, question 7, and Polarization lab report (method and Part 3 fit)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Jones vectors live in the inner-product space $\mathbb C^2$ ([[§20 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-2|LADR Def. 6.2]]); decomposing light into orthogonal polarizations is expansion in an orthonormal basis ([[§21 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-30|LADR Thm. 6.30]]).
> - Lossless elements are unitary and diagonal in an orthonormal eigenbasis ([[§26 Isometries, Unitary Operators, and Matrix Factorization#^ladr-7-55|LADR Thm. 7.55]]); polarizers are orthogonal projections. The same algebra describes a spin-½ system in quantum mechanics, with the polarization states of one photon as the two basis states.
