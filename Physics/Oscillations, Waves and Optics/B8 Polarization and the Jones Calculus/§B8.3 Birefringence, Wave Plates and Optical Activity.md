Where do the retarders and rotators of the Jones calculus come from? Level A described birefringence, optical activity and stress patterns in words ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^rem-a4-5-7|§A4.5, Remark: Polarization in materials]]). This section derives them from one change to the dielectric of [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|Model §B8.1.1]]: the susceptibility becomes a tensor. A real symmetric tensor gives each direction of propagation two linearly polarized waves with different speeds (birefringence), and a slab of such a crystal is a wave plate ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^def-b8-2-3|Def. §B8.2.3]]). A Hermitian tensor with imaginary off-diagonal elements gives two circularly polarized waves (optical activity), and a magnetic field produces one (Faraday rotation). Complex amplitudes follow the optics convention ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-1|§B8.1, Caution: The optics sign convention]]).

**Setup.** The system is the anisotropic dielectric of [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-1|Model §B8.3.1]] (a real symmetric susceptibility tensor) and, for optical activity, the gyrotropic medium of [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-9|Model §B8.3.9]] (a Hermitian tensor); otherwise as in [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|Model §B8.1.1]]: classical, linear, lossless, non-magnetic, one frequency at a time. Plates are the ideal wave plate of [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-4|Model §B8.3.4]] at normal incidence. Optics sign convention ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-1|§B8.1, Caution: The optics sign convention]]); rotations are named as seen facing the oncoming light ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^cau-b8-3-2|Caution: Which way is dextrorotatory]]). The results are exact for these models at one wavelength; indices, retardances and rotatory powers vary with wavelength ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^law-b8-3-2|Law §B8.3.2]], [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^law-b8-3-7|Law §B8.3.7]]).

## Light in crystals

> [!model] Model §B8.3.1: Anisotropic Dielectric (Crystal)
> In a crystal the polarization is linear in the field but not parallel to it:
>
> $$
> \vec P = \varepsilon_0\,\chi\,\vec E, \qquad \vec D = \varepsilon_0(1 + \chi)\vec E ,
> $$
>
> with a real symmetric $3 \times 3$ **susceptibility tensor** $\chi$. There are therefore orthogonal **principal axes** in which $\chi = \operatorname{diag}(\chi_{11}, \chi_{22}, \chi_{33})$ ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]), and **principal indices** $n_j = \sqrt{1 + \chi_{jj}}$, $j = 1, 2, 3$. Otherwise the medium is as in [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|Model §B8.1.1]]: no free charges, non-magnetic, lossless, one frequency at a time.
>
> - *Picture:* a bound electron held by springs of different stiffness in different directions of the lattice, so that a field displaces it more easily along some directions than others; the isotropic version is the Lorentz model ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^mod-b13-1-2|Model §B13.1.2]]).
> - *Fails:* for absorbing crystals, where $\chi$ is complex and one polarization is absorbed more (dichroism: tourmaline, Polaroid, [[§A4.5 Polarization#^rem-a4-5-3|§A4.5, Remark: How a polarizing filter works]]); for optically active crystals such as quartz, whose tensor also has imaginary off-diagonal parts ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-9|Model §B8.3.9]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.7, eqs. (6.85)–(6.89), (6.96), Figure 6.7*

^mod-b8-3-1

> [!definition] Definition §B8.3.1: Optic Axis; Uniaxial and Biaxial Crystals; Ordinary and Extraordinary Index
> An **optic axis** is a direction of propagation for which the two waves the crystal allows ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-3|Theorem §B8.3.3]]) have the same phase velocity. If all three principal indices are equal the crystal is **optically isotropic** (cubic crystals: rock salt, diamond); if two are equal it is **uniaxial**, with one optic axis along the third principal axis (trigonal, tetragonal, hexagonal crystals); if all three differ it is **biaxial**, with two optic axes (orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic). In a uniaxial crystal the index of the two equal principal directions is the **ordinary index** $n_o$ and that of the optic axis the **extraordinary index** $n_e$; the crystal is **positive** if $n_e > n_o$ and **negative** if $n_e < n_o$. Measured indices of common crystals: [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^law-b8-3-2|Law §B8.3.2]].
>
> *Model:* [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-1|Model §B8.3.1]]; *applies:* lossless crystal, one wavelength.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.7, the classification after eq. (6.100)*

^law-b8-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.3.3: The Two Waves in a Uniaxial Crystal
> In the crystal of [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-1|Model §B8.3.1]] a plane wave $\vec E_0e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)}$ exists if and only if
>
> $$
> \vec k\times(\vec k\times\vec E_0) + \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\,(1 + \chi)\,\vec E_0 = 0 .
> $$
>
> For a uniaxial crystal with optic axis $\hat z$ and $\vec k$ at the angle $\theta$ to it, there are exactly two linearly polarized solutions:
> - the **ordinary wave**, with $\vec E$ perpendicular to the plane of $\vec k$ and the optic axis, and index $n_o$ for every $\theta$;
> - the **extraordinary wave**, with $\vec E$ in that plane, and index $n(\theta)$ given by
>   $$
>   \frac{1}{n(\theta)^2} = \frac{\cos^2\theta}{n_o^2} + \frac{\sin^2\theta}{n_e^2} ,
>   $$
>   which runs from $n_o$ along the axis to $n_e$ perpendicular to it.
>
> Their $\vec D$ vectors are perpendicular to $\vec k$ and to each other. In $\vec k$-space, $\lvert\vec k\rvert = n\omega/c$ traces a sphere (ordinary) and a spheroid of revolution about the optic axis (extraordinary) that touch on the axis, where both waves travel at $c/n_o$.
>
> *Model:* [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-1|Model §B8.3.1]]; *applies:* as in the opening, exact; the two-wave statement for a uniaxial crystal ($n_1 = n_2 = n_o$).
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.7, eqs. (6.91)–(6.100) and Figures 6.8–6.9*

^thm-b8-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\nabla \to i\vec k$, $\partial_t \to -i\omega$, Faraday's law gives $\vec k\times\vec E_0 = \mu_0\omega\vec H_0$ and the Ampère–Maxwell law $\vec k\times\vec H_0 = -\omega\vec D_0$, as in [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]] with $\varepsilon\vec E_0$ replaced by $\vec D_0 = \varepsilon_0(1 + \chi)\vec E_0$. Eliminating $\vec H_0$, $\vec k\times(\vec k\times\vec E_0) = -\mu_0\omega^2\vec D_0$, which is the stated equation since $\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2$; it also shows $\vec k\cdot\vec D_0 = 0$.
>
> Take principal axes with $\chi = \operatorname{diag}(n_o^2 - 1, n_o^2 - 1, n_e^2 - 1)$, write $q = \omega/c$, and turn the axes about $\hat z$ until $\vec k = (k_x, 0, k_z)$. Using $\vec k\times(\vec k\times\vec E) = \vec k(\vec k\cdot\vec E) - k^2\vec E$, the three components read
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> (n_o^2q^2 - k_z^2)\,E_x + k_xk_z\,E_z &= 0, \\
> (n_o^2q^2 - k_x^2 - k_z^2)\,E_y &= 0, \\
> k_xk_z\,E_x + (n_e^2q^2 - k_x^2)\,E_z &= 0 .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> The middle equation is decoupled. A wave with $E_y \ne 0$ (and $E_x = E_z = 0$) needs $k^2 = n_o^2q^2$: the ordinary wave, polarized along $\hat y$, perpendicular to the plane of $\vec k$ and $\hat z$. A wave with $(E_x, E_z) \ne 0$ needs the determinant of the outer pair to vanish:
>
> $$
> (n_o^2q^2 - k_z^2)(n_e^2q^2 - k_x^2) - k_x^2k_z^2 = n_o^2n_e^2q^4 - q^2\bigl(n_o^2k_x^2 + n_e^2k_z^2\bigr) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{k_x^2}{n_e^2} + \frac{k_z^2}{n_o^2} = q^2 ,
> $$
>
> an ellipse in the $k_xk_z$-plane, i.e. a spheroid about $\hat z$. With $k_x = nq\sin\theta$, $k_z = nq\cos\theta$ this is the formula for $n(\theta)$. Both $\vec D_0$ are perpendicular to $\vec k$; the ordinary one is along $\hat y$ and the extraordinary one lies in the $xz$-plane, so they are perpendicular. At $\theta = 0$, $n(0) = n_o$: the sphere and the spheroid touch, and $\hat z$ is the optic axis.

> [!remark] Remark: Walk-off, double refraction and polarizing prisms
> - In the extraordinary wave $\vec E$ is not perpendicular to $\vec k$ (only $\vec D$ is), so the Poynting vector $\vec E\times\vec H$ is not along $\vec k$: the energy of a narrow beam travels along the normal to the $\vec k$-surface at the tip of $\vec k$ (Fowles §6.7–6.8, proof in his reference [5]). Wave fronts move sideways along the beam.
> - At the surface of a crystal the tangential component of $\vec k$ is continuous, by the same phase-matching argument as for an isotropic medium ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-3|Theorem §B9.1.3]]). Each sheet of the $\vec k$-surface gives one refracted wave, so an unpolarized beam splits into two, polarized at right angles: **double refraction**, the double image seen through calcite. The ordinary wave obeys Snell's law with $n_o$; the extraordinary wave does not in general, since its index depends on its direction.
> - Polarizing prisms exploit this. In a Glan prism two calcite prisms are separated by a thin gap, and at the gap the ordinary wave is totally reflected while the extraordinary one passes ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^ex-b8-3-2|Example §B8.3.2]]); Wollaston and Rochon prisms instead send the two polarizations off at different angles. Calcite prisms are the most efficient linear polarizers (Fowles §2.3).
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.7–6.8, Figures 6.11–6.16 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 §6*

## Wave plates

> [!model] Model §B8.3.4: Ideal Wave Plate
> A slab of uniaxial crystal of thickness $d$, cut with the optic axis in its faces, receives light at normal incidence. Then $\vec k \perp$ optic axis ($\theta = 90^\circ$ in [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-3|Theorem §B8.3.3]]): light polarized along the axis travels with $n_e$, light polarized across it with $n_o$, both along $\vec k$ without walk-off. The slab is the linear retarder of [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^def-b8-2-3|Def. §B8.2.3]] with
>
> $$
> \Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_0}\,\lvert n_e - n_o\rvert\,d ;
> $$
>
> its fast axis is the optic axis in a negative crystal (calcite) and perpendicular to it in a positive one (quartz). A **quarter-wave plate** has $\lvert n_e - n_o\rvert d = (m + \tfrac14)\lambda_0$, a **half-wave plate** $(m + \tfrac12)\lambda_0$, with $m = 0$ for a zero-order plate.
>
> - *Assumes:* normal incidence; no reflection at the faces and no absorption; one wavelength $\lambda_0$.
> - *Fails:* $\Gamma$ is proportional to $1/\lambda_0$ (and $n_e - n_o$ itself depends on $\lambda_0$), so a plate is "quarter-wave" at one wavelength only, the more sharply the higher its order ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^ex-b8-3-1|Example §B8.3.1]]); oblique rays see a different $\Gamma$; each face reflects a few percent ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]]). Mica (biaxial) and stretched polymer films serve as well as uniaxial crystals.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.4, eq. (2.33) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 §7, eqs. (30)–(34)*

^mod-b8-3-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.3.5: What Wave Plates Do to Polarized Light
> Let the fast axis of a plate lie along $x$.
> 1. A **quarter-wave plate** turns linear light at the angle $\theta$ from its fast axis into elliptical light with axes along the plate's axes and semi-axes in the ratio $\lvert\tan\theta\rvert$, left-handed for $0 < \theta < 90^\circ$ and right-handed for $-90^\circ < \theta < 0$; for $\theta = \pm45^\circ$ the light is circular. Conversely it turns circular light into linear light at $\pm45^\circ$ to its axes.
> 2. A **half-wave plate** with fast axis at $\varphi$ turns linear light at $\theta$ into linear light at $2\varphi - \theta$, the mirror image in the fast axis (a rotation by $90^\circ$ when $\theta - \varphi = 45^\circ$), and reverses the handedness of circular and elliptical light.
> 3. A **retarder** of retardance $\Gamma$ with fast axis at $\varphi$, placed between crossed linear polarizers (the first along $x$), transmits the fraction
>    $$
>    \frac{I}{I_1} = \sin^2 2\varphi\,\sin^2\frac{\Gamma}{2}
>    $$
>    of the irradiance $I_1$ leaving the first polarizer.
>
> *Model:* [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-4|Model §B8.3.4]] and the elements of [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]]; *applies:* monochromatic, fully polarized light at normal incidence, exact at the design wavelength.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.4 and §2.5 (quarter-wave example after eq. (2.39)) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 §7 · PHY 300, Worksheet 8, measurement 4*

^thm-b8-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *1.* $\operatorname{diag}(1, i)\,(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)^T = (\cos\theta,\ i\sin\theta)^T$ ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]]). The components have moduli $\lvert\cos\theta\rvert$, $\lvert\sin\theta\rvert$ and relative phase $\delta = +\pi/2$ when $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ have the same sign, $-\pi/2$ otherwise. By [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]] the axes lie along $x$ and $y$, the handedness is as stated, and $\theta = \pm45^\circ$ gives equal moduli: $(1, \pm i)/\sqrt2$. The plate's matrix is invertible, with inverse $\operatorname{diag}(1, -i)$, which maps $(1, \pm i)$ back to $(1, \pm1)$.
>
> *2.* With $e^{i\Gamma} = -1$ the matrix of Theorem §B8.2.3 becomes $\begin{pmatrix} \cos 2\varphi & \sin 2\varphi \\ \sin 2\varphi & -\cos 2\varphi \end{pmatrix}$, a reflection of the plane in the line at angle $\varphi$. On $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)^T$ it gives $(\cos(2\varphi - \theta), \sin(2\varphi - \theta))^T$ by the addition formulas. On left-circular light, $(\cos 2\varphi + i\sin 2\varphi,\ \sin 2\varphi - i\cos 2\varphi)^T = e^{2i\varphi}(1, -i)^T$: right circular. In general, the matrix is $R(\varphi)\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)R(-\varphi)$; the rotations turn the ellipse rigidly and keep its handedness, while $\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$ changes the relative phase $\delta$ into $\delta + \pi$ and so reverses the sign of $\sin\delta$ (Theorem §B8.1.3).
>
> *3.* The light after the first polarizer is $(1, 0)^T$ (times $\sqrt{I_1}$). The second polarizer keeps the $y$-component of $W_\Gamma(\varphi)(1, 0)^T$, which is $\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,(1 - e^{i\Gamma})$. Since $\lvert 1 - e^{i\Gamma}\rvert^2 = 2 - 2\cos\Gamma = 4\sin^2(\Gamma/2)$, the transmitted fraction is $4\sin^2\varphi\cos^2\varphi\,\sin^2(\Gamma/2) = \sin^2 2\varphi\,\sin^2(\Gamma/2)$.

> [!law] Law §B8.3.6: Kerr and Pockels Electro-Optic Effects
> 1. *Kerr effect* (J. Kerr, 1875). An optically isotropic substance, a glass or a liquid, placed in a strong electric field $\vec E$ becomes birefringent: it behaves as a uniaxial crystal ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^def-b8-3-1|Def. §B8.3.1]]) whose optic axis is along $\vec E$, and the birefringence is proportional to the square of the field,
>
> $$
> n_\parallel - n_\perp = \lambda_0KE^2 ,
> $$
>
> where $n_\parallel$ and $n_\perp$ are the indices for light polarized along and across $\vec E$, $\lambda_0$ is the vacuum wavelength, and the Kerr constant $K$ is a property of the substance (table).
> 2. *Pockels effect.* In certain birefringent crystals, such as ADP (ammonium dihydrogen phosphate) and KDP (potassium dihydrogen phosphate), an applied electric field changes the indices by an amount directly proportional to the field.
>
> | liquid | $K$ ($\mathrm{cm/V^2}$) |
> |---|---|
> | benzene | $0.7 \times 10^{-12}$ |
> | carbon disulfide | $3.5 \times 10^{-12}$ |
> | nitrotoluene | $2.0 \times 10^{-10}$ |
> | nitrobenzene | $4.4 \times 10^{-10}$ |
>
> *Valid when:* the Kerr effect in optically isotropic solids and liquids, the Pockels effect in certain birefringent crystals; the tabulated $K$ are for liquids, at a wavelength and temperature Fowles does not state.
> *Source: Fowles §6.11, eq. (6.146) and Table 6.4*

^law-b8-3-6

> [!remark] Remark: Variable retarders: stress, Kerr and Pockels cells
> Part 3 of [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]] is the working principle of every device that controls light by making a material birefringent.
> - **Stress.** Glass and plastic become birefringent under strain, with $\Gamma$ varying from point to point. Between crossed polarizers each point transmits $\sin^2(\Gamma/2)$, and since $\Gamma \propto 1/\lambda_0$ different colours pass at different places: the coloured stress patterns of [[§A4.5 Polarization#^rem-a4-5-7|§A4.5, Remark: Polarization in materials]].
> - **Kerr effect.** A strong field $E$ makes an isotropic liquid or glass uniaxial ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^law-b8-3-6|Law §B8.3.6]]). A Kerr cell, usually filled with nitrobenzene, which has the largest $K$ of Fowles's liquids, set at $45^\circ$ between crossed polarizers is a shutter opened by a voltage, with $\Gamma = 2\pi KlE^2$ over a path $l$.
> - **Pockels effect.** In crystals such as KDP the change of index is linear in the field ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^law-b8-3-6|Law §B8.3.6]]). A quarter-wave plate added as "optical bias" moves the working point to $\Gamma = \pi/2 + \Gamma_E$, where $\sin^2(\Gamma/2) \approx \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\Gamma_E$ responds linearly to the signal.
> - Schwartz's rapatronic camera (exposures of microseconds, 1940s) used such a cell as a half-wave plate between crossed polarizers; 3D cinema separates the two eyes' images by left- and right-circular polarization, which, unlike linear, does not depend on how the viewer tilts the head.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.11, eq. (6.146), Table 6.4, Figures 6.20–6.22 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 §7*

## Optical activity

> [!definition] Definition §B8.3.2: Optical Activity; Specific Rotatory Power
> A medium is **optically active** if linearly polarized light passing through it stays linearly polarized while its direction of polarization turns, by an angle proportional to the path length. The angle per unit length is the **specific rotatory power** $\rho$. The medium is **dextrorotatory** if the rotation is clockwise as seen by an observer facing the oncoming light, **levorotatory** if counterclockwise ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^cau-b8-3-2|Caution: Which way is dextrorotatory]]). The variation of $\rho$ with wavelength is **rotatory dispersion**. Which substances are active, and the measured $\rho$ of quartz: [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^law-b8-3-7|Law §B8.3.7]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.9*

^def-b8-3-2

> [!law] Law §B8.3.7: Optically Active Substances; Rotatory Power of Quartz
> 1. Sodium chlorate, cinnabar, certain kinds of sugar, and crystalline quartz for light along its optic axis are optically active ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^def-b8-3-2|Def. §B8.3.2]]); fused quartz is not.
> 2. Quartz grows as right-handed and as left-handed crystals; right-handed quartz is dextrorotatory, left-handed quartz levorotatory. The sense is that of [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^def-b8-3-2|Def. §B8.3.2]], clockwise as seen by an observer facing the oncoming light, which is what Fowles's circular indices for right-handed quartz ($n_L > n_R$) give by [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-8|Theorem §B8.3.8]]; Fowles's verbal definition, read literally, describes the opposite sense ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^cau-b8-3-2|Caution: Which way is dextrorotatory]]).
> 3. The specific rotatory power of quartz along the optic axis falls steeply with wavelength (rotatory dispersion):
>
> | $\lambda_0$ (nm) | $400$ | $450$ | $500$ | $550$ | $600$ | $650$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $\rho$ (degrees per mm) | $49$ | $37$ | $31$ | $26$ | $22$ | $17$ |
>
> *Valid when:* quartz: light along the optic axis, the same magnitude for both kinds of quartz, with opposite senses; in other directions quartz is also birefringent, and its two waves are elliptically polarized (Fowles §6.9, Figure 6.19). [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-8|Theorem §B8.3.8]] relates $\rho$ to the circular indices, and [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^ex-b8-3-4|Example §B8.3.4]] checks the table against them.
> *Source: Fowles §6.9, Table 6.2 and the table of $n_R$, $n_L$ after eq. (6.121)*

^law-b8-3-7

> [!caution] Caution: Which way is dextrorotatory
> Fowles defines the sense in words: a substance is dextrorotatory "if the sense of rotation of the plane of polarization is to the right, as a right-handed screw pointing in the direction of propagation". A right-handed screw advancing along the beam turns clockwise as seen from behind, that is, counterclockwise for an observer facing the light. His Figure 6.17, captioned as the levorotatory case, draws the plane turning, in the figure's perspective, clockwise for an observer facing the light. Read literally, both give the opposite sense to [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^def-b8-3-2|Def. §B8.3.2]]. His calculation, eqs. (6.116)–(6.120), with his circular indices for right-handed quartz ($n_L > n_R$) gives a clockwise turn seen facing the light for the quartz he calls dextrorotatory, which is the sense of Def. §B8.3.2 ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-8|Theorem §B8.3.8]], [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^ex-b8-3-4|Example §B8.3.4]]). These notes use Def. §B8.3.2 throughout; check the sense when comparing with other sources.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.9, p. 185, Figure 6.17, eqs. (6.116)–(6.121)*

^cau-b8-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.3.8: Optical Activity Is Circular Birefringence
> If right- and left-circularly polarized light travel through a medium with indices $n_R$ and $n_L$, then linearly polarized light remains linearly polarized after a path $l$, with its direction turned clockwise (seen facing the light) by
>
> $$
> \theta = \frac{\pi l}{\lambda_0}\,(n_L - n_R), \qquad \rho = \frac{\pi}{\lambda_0}\,(n_L - n_R) ,
> $$
>
> dextrorotatory if $n_L > n_R$. In Jones form a length $l$ of the medium is $e^{i\bar k l}R(-\theta)$, $\bar k = \tfrac12(k_R + k_L)$, the rotator of [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-3|Theorem §B8.2.3]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.9, eqs. (6.116)–(6.121)*

^thm-b8-3-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Decompose $x$-polarized light into circular states ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]]): $(1, 0)^T = \tfrac12(1, -i)^T + \tfrac12(1, i)^T$. Each circular state is an eigenstate of the medium and simply acquires its own phase $e^{ik_Rl}$ or $e^{ik_Ll}$, with $k_{R,L} = 2\pi n_{R,L}/\lambda_0$. With $\bar k = \tfrac12(k_R + k_L)$ and $\alpha = \tfrac12(k_R - k_L)l$:
>
> $$
> \tfrac12(1, -i)^Te^{ik_Rl} + \tfrac12(1, i)^Te^{ik_Ll} = e^{i\bar kl}\Bigl[\tfrac12(1, -i)^Te^{i\alpha} + \tfrac12(1, i)^Te^{-i\alpha}\Bigr] = e^{i\bar kl}\,(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)^T ,
> $$
>
> since $\tfrac12(e^{i\alpha} + e^{-i\alpha}) = \cos\alpha$ and $\tfrac12(-ie^{i\alpha} + ie^{-i\alpha}) = \sin\alpha$. This is linear light turned by $\alpha$ from $x$ toward $y$, counterclockwise for the observer facing the wave; the clockwise angle is $\theta = -\alpha = \tfrac12(k_L - k_R)l = \pi l(n_L - n_R)/\lambda_0$. The same holds for any linear input, by linearity: both circular eigenstates of $R(-\theta)$ acquire exactly these phases ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-4|Theorem §B8.2.4]]).

^der-b8-3-8

*Uses:* [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-1|Theorem §B8.2.1]], [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^thm-b8-2-4|Theorem §B8.2.4]], [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^def-b8-3-2|Def. §B8.3.2]]

> [!model] Model §B8.3.9: Gyrotropic Medium
> A **gyrotropic** medium has, for propagation along $z$, the susceptibility tensor
>
> $$
> \chi = \begin{pmatrix} \chi_{11} & i\chi_{12} & 0 \\ -i\chi_{12} & \chi_{11} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \chi_{33} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \chi_{11}, \chi_{12}, \chi_{33} \ \text{real}:
> $$
>
> a Hermitian matrix, so the medium is still lossless, but not a real one. Crystalline quartz has a tensor of this kind for light near its optic axis; an isotropic dielectric in a static magnetic field along $z$ acquires one (Faraday effect, [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^law-b8-3-11|Law §B8.3.11]]; [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^rem-b8-3-3|Remark: Faraday rotation]]).
>
> - *Assumes:* a single direction of propagation; for natural optical activity the off-diagonal term belongs to that direction and reverses with it ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^cau-b8-3-1|Caution: Natural and magnetic rotation]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.9, eq. (6.122), and §6.10, eq. (6.139)*

^mod-b8-3-9

> [!theorem] Theorem §B8.3.10: Circular Waves in a Gyrotropic Medium
> In the medium of [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-9|Model §B8.3.9]], plane waves along $z$ are transverse, and the two that propagate unchanged are circularly polarized:
>
> $$
> \text{right } (1, -i):\ \ n_R = \sqrt{1 + \chi_{11} + \chi_{12}}, \qquad \text{left } (1, i):\ \ n_L = \sqrt{1 + \chi_{11} - \chi_{12}} .
> $$
>
> For small $\chi_{12}$, $n_R - n_L \approx \chi_{12}/n_o$ with $n_o = \sqrt{1 + \chi_{11}}$, and the specific rotatory power ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-8|Theorem §B8.3.8]]) is
>
> $$
> \rho \approx -\frac{\pi\chi_{12}}{n_o\lambda_0} ,
> $$
>
> proportional to the imaginary off-diagonal susceptibility.
>
> *Model:* [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-9|Model §B8.3.9]]; *applies:* propagation along $z$, lossless; $n_R$, $n_L$ exact, $\rho$ to first order in $\chi_{12}$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.9, eqs. (6.123)–(6.132)*

^thm-b8-3-10

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The wave equation of [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-3|Theorem §B8.3.3]] holds with this $\chi$ (its derivation did not use symmetry of $\chi$). For $\vec k = k\hat z$, $\vec k\times(\vec k\times\vec E) = -k^2(E_x, E_y, 0)$. The $z$-component gives $q^2(1 + \chi_{33})E_z = 0$, so $E_z = 0$: transverse. With $q = \omega/c$ and $A = q^2(1 + \chi_{11}) - k^2$, the other two are
>
> $$
> A\,E_x + iq^2\chi_{12}\,E_y = 0, \qquad -iq^2\chi_{12}\,E_x + A\,E_y = 0 ,
> $$
>
> an eigenvalue problem for the Hermitian matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 + \chi_{11} & i\chi_{12} \\ -i\chi_{12} & 1 + \chi_{11} \end{pmatrix}$, whose eigenvalues $1 + \chi_{11} \pm \chi_{12}$ are real and whose eigenvectors are orthogonal ([[§23 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-12|LADR Thm. 7.12]], [[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]). For $(E_x, E_y) = (1, -i)$ the first equation reads $A + q^2\chi_{12} = 0$, so $k^2 = q^2(1 + \chi_{11} + \chi_{12})$, and the second, $-iq^2\chi_{12} - iA = 0$, agrees. For $(1, i)$, $k^2 = q^2(1 + \chi_{11} - \chi_{12})$. Finally $n_R - n_L = (n_R^2 - n_L^2)/(n_R + n_L) = 2\chi_{12}/(n_R + n_L) \approx \chi_{12}/n_o$, and $\rho = \pi(n_L - n_R)/\lambda_0$.

^der-b8-3-10

*Uses:* [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-9|Model §B8.3.9]], [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-3|Theorem §B8.3.3]], [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-8|Theorem §B8.3.8]], [[§23 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-12|LADR Thm. 7.12]]

> [!definition] Definition §B8.3.3: Verdet Constant
> Let linearly polarized light cross a length $l$ of a medium placed in a static magnetic field $\vec B$ parallel to the beam, and let its plane of polarization turn by the angle $\theta$. The **Verdet constant** of the medium is
>
> $$
> V = \frac{\theta}{Bl} ,
> $$
>
> the rotation per unit field and unit path length. Fowles quotes it in minutes of arc per oersted per centimetre; $1'/(\mathrm{Oe\,cm}) = 291\ \mathrm{rad/(T\,m)}$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.10, eq. (6.134)*

^def-b8-3-3

> [!law] Law §B8.3.11: Faraday Rotation
> An isotropic dielectric in a static magnetic field $\vec B$ becomes optically active ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^def-b8-3-2|Def. §B8.3.2]]): linearly polarized light sent through it along the field emerges with its plane of polarization turned by an angle proportional to $B$ and to the path length $l$,
>
> $$
> \theta = VBl ,
> $$
>
> with a Verdet constant $V$ ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^def-b8-3-3|Def. §B8.3.3]]) characteristic of the material (Faraday, 1845). For yellow light, $589\ \mathrm{nm}$:
>
> | substance | $V$ (minutes of arc per $\mathrm{Oe\,cm}$) |
> |---|---|
> | fluorite | $0.0009$ |
> | diamond | $0.012$ |
> | crown glass | $0.015$–$0.025$ |
> | flint glass | $0.030$–$0.050$ |
> | sodium chloride | $0.036$ |
>
> *Valid when:* transparent isotropic solids, with $\vec B$ along the beam; the values hold at $589\ \mathrm{nm}$, and $V$ depends on the wavelength. In Fowles's bound-electron theory the rotation is proportional to $B$ for $\omega\omega_c \ll \lvert\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\rvert$, that is, away from the material's resonances ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^rem-b8-3-3|Remark: Faraday rotation]]).
> *Source: Fowles §6.10, eq. (6.134) and Table 6.3*

^law-b8-3-11

> [!remark] Remark: Faraday rotation
> Fowles explains Faraday rotation ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^law-b8-3-11|Law §B8.3.11]]) with the bound-electron model: the equation of motion $m\ddot{\vec r} + K\vec r = -e\vec E - e\,\dot{\vec r}\times\vec B$ couples the $x$ and $y$ motions, and solving it at frequency $\omega$ gives exactly the gyrotropic tensor of [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-9|Model §B8.3.9]], with
>
> $$
> \chi_{12} = \frac{Ne^2}{m\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\omega\,\omega_c}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 - \omega^2\omega_c^2}, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{K/m}, \quad \omega_c = \frac{eB}{m},
> $$
>
> proportional to $B$ for $\omega\omega_c \ll \lvert\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\rvert$. With the Verdet constants of the law, $10\ \mathrm{cm}$ of flint glass in $1\ \mathrm T$ ($10^4\ \mathrm{Oe}$) with $V = 0.03$ turns the polarization by $\theta = 0.03 \times 10^4 \times 10 = 3000' = 50^\circ$. The same model with $\chi_{11} \ne \chi_{33}$ also makes the medium slightly birefringent (Voigt effect), and liquids in strong magnetic fields show a quadratic magnetic birefringence (Cotton–Mouton effect). The equation of motion is the Lorentz oscillator of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^mod-b13-1-2|Model §B13.1.2]] with a magnetic force added; Fowles writes the response for $e^{-i\omega t}$, the convention of this chapter.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.10–6.11, eqs. (6.135)–(6.145)*

^rem-b8-3-3

> [!caution] Caution: Natural and magnetic rotation
> The two rotations differ when the light is sent back. In a Faraday rotator the off-diagonal term is set by $\vec B$, not by the direction of travel, so the rotation has the same sense in the laboratory frame on the way back: reflected through the medium a second time, the light is turned by $2\theta$ in total. In a naturally active medium (sugar solution, quartz) the rotation comes from the handedness of the molecules or the crystal and has a fixed sense relative to the direction of travel: on the way back it undoes itself, and the light returns with its original polarization. A Faraday rotator of $45^\circ$ between polarizers at $0^\circ$ and $45^\circ$ therefore passes light one way and blocks the reflection (an optical isolator), which no naturally active medium can do. Fowles's tensor (6.122) describes natural activity for one direction of propagation only. (This contrast is not drawn in the course sources.)

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B8.3.1: How Thick Is a Quarter-Wave Plate?
> Take Fowles's indices to hold at the sodium wavelength $\lambda_0 = 589\ \mathrm{nm}$. By [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-4|Model §B8.3.4]] a zero-order quarter-wave plate has $d = \lambda_0/(4\lvert n_e - n_o\rvert)$:
>
> $$
> \text{quartz } (\lvert n_e - n_o\rvert = 0.009):\ d = \frac{589\ \mathrm{nm}}{0.036} = 16.4\ \mu\mathrm m; \qquad \text{calcite } (0.172):\ d = 0.86\ \mu\mathrm m .
> $$
>
> Calcite's birefringence is so large that a zero-order plate would be impossibly thin to polish; practical plates are thicker, of order $m$, or made of weakly birefringent mica, quartz or polymer. Order matters for other wavelengths. Ignoring the dispersion of $n_e - n_o$, $\Gamma \propto 1/\lambda_0$. At the He–Ne wavelength $633\ \mathrm{nm}$ the zero-order quartz plate gives $\Gamma = 90^\circ \times 589/633 = 83.7^\circ$, still nearly quarter-wave. A tenth-order quartz plate ($m = 10$, $d = 10.25\lambda_0/0.009 = 0.671\ \mathrm{mm}$) gives $\Gamma = 360^\circ \times 10.25 \times 589/633 = 3434^\circ$, i.e. $194^\circ$ modulo $360^\circ$: near $633\ \mathrm{nm}$ it acts almost as a half-wave plate.
>
> *Source: Fowles §2.4, eq. (2.33), and Table 6.1 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 §7 (wavelength dependence)*

^ex-b8-3-1

> [!example] Example §B8.3.2: The Window of a Glan Prism
> In a Glan prism of calcite the optic axis is perpendicular to the plane of incidence at the inner face, so both waves obey Snell's law there (the cross-section of the $\vec k$-surface in that plane is two circles), the ordinary wave with $n_o = 1.658$ and $\vec E$ in the plane of incidence, the extraordinary wave with $n_e = 1.486$ and $\vec E$ along the axis. With an air gap, the ordinary wave is totally reflected if $\sin\theta \ge 1/n_o$ and the extraordinary wave is transmitted if $\sin\theta < 1/n_e$ ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]):
>
> $$
> \sin^{-1}\frac{1}{1.658} = 37.1^\circ \ \le\ \theta\ <\ \sin^{-1}\frac{1}{1.486} = 42.3^\circ .
> $$
>
> Fowles's apex angle of about $38.5^\circ$ (equal to $\theta$ for light entering normally) lies in this window. The beam that emerges is purely extraordinary, linearly polarized along the optic axis; the window of about $5^\circ$ limits the cone of rays the prism accepts.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.8, eqs. (6.113)–(6.115), Figure 6.15*

^ex-b8-3-2

> [!example] Example §B8.3.3: Half-Wave Plate or Third Polarizer between Crossed Polarizers
> The PHY 300 polarization lab (Worksheet 8) turns a third polarizer, and then a wave plate, between two crossed polarizers. For the polarizer at $\theta$ the transmitted fraction of $I_1$ is $\cos^2\theta\sin^2\theta = \tfrac14\sin^2 2\theta$ ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^ex-b8-2-3|Example §B8.2.3]]); for a half-wave plate with fast axis at $\varphi$ it is $\sin^2 2\varphi$ ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]], $\Gamma = \pi$). Both curves vanish at $0^\circ$, $90^\circ$, $180^\circ$ and peak at $45^\circ$ and $135^\circ$, but the plate's peak is four times higher: at $\varphi = 45^\circ$ it turns the polarization through $90^\circ$ without loss, while the middle polarizer can only project, losing half the irradiance at each of its two steps. A quarter-wave plate at $45^\circ$ passes $\sin^2(\pi/4) = \tfrac12$: it makes circular light, half of which the analyzer transmits.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 8, measurements 3–4*

^ex-b8-3-3

> [!example] Example §B8.3.4: Rotatory Power of Quartz from Its Circular Indices
> For right-handed quartz along the optic axis Fowles lists $n_L - n_R = 0.00011$, $0.00007$ and $0.00006$ at $396$, $589$ and $760\ \mathrm{nm}$. By [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-8|Theorem §B8.3.8]],
>
> $$
> \rho = \frac{\pi(n_L - n_R)}{\lambda_0} = \frac{\pi\,(1.1 \times 10^{-4})}{3.96 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mm}} = 0.873\ \mathrm{rad/mm} = 50^\circ/\mathrm{mm}
> $$
>
> at $396\ \mathrm{nm}$, and likewise $21^\circ/\mathrm{mm}$ at $589\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $14^\circ/\mathrm{mm}$ at $760\ \mathrm{nm}$, positive (clockwise, dextrorotatory) because $n_L > n_R$. Fowles's measured rotatory powers ($49^\circ/\mathrm{mm}$ at $400\ \mathrm{nm}$, $22^\circ/\mathrm{mm}$ at $600\ \mathrm{nm}$, [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^law-b8-3-7|Law §B8.3.7]]) agree within the one-digit precision of the index differences. An index difference of less than one part in $10^4$ turns the polarization through $90^\circ$ in about $4\ \mathrm{mm}$ of yellow light's path. Rotatory dispersion is strong: $\rho$ falls by a factor $3.5$ from violet to deep red, so white linearly polarized light leaves a quartz plate with its colours polarized in different directions.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.9, Table 6.2 and the table of $n_R$, $n_L$ after eq. (6.121)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Principal axes of the susceptibility tensor are the orthonormal eigenbasis of a real symmetric matrix ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]); the circular modes of a gyrotropic medium are the eigenvectors of a Hermitian one ([[§24 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-31|LADR Thm. 7.31]]).
> - The microscopic origin of $\chi$ (bound electrons, resonances, dispersion of $n_o$, $n_e$ and $\rho$) is the Lorentz model of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model|§B13.1]]; refraction at the surface of a crystal uses the phase matching of [[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction|§B9.1]].
> - The macroscopic theory of anisotropic and gyrotropic media in general: Electromagnetism level C (planned). Its static starting point, the susceptibility tensor of a constitutive relation, is defined in [[§C5.2 The Field of Polarized Matter#^def-c5-2-1|EM Def. §C5.2.1]].
