*Sources: Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lecture 11.*

A pure sinusoid fills all of space and all of time, so it cannot carry a message: nothing about it changes. Signals, pulses and particles are **wave packets**, sinusoids whose amplitude varies slowly. Level A met the simplest case, two tones that beat ([[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]), and quantum mechanics uses packets as particles ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^def-a3-3-2|QM Def. §A3.3.2]]). The Fourier transform ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function|§B4.4]]) gives the spectrum of a packet, a band around a carrier, and the duration–bandwidth trade-off ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-5|Theorem §B4.4.5]]). This section puts the transform to work on travelling waves: a packet is a superposition of travelling waves of nearby wave numbers, each moving according to the medium's dispersion relation; modulating a carrier places the spectrum of the message around the carrier; beats and amplitude modulation are packets of two and three components. How a packet moves when the medium is dispersive is [[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity|§B6.2]].

## Packets as superpositions

> [!definition] Definition §B6.1.1: Wave Packet; Carrier; Envelope; Bandwidth
> A **wave packet** moving toward $+x$ is a real wave of the form
>
> $$
> \psi(x,t) = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[E(x,t)\,e^{i(\omega_ct - k_cx)}\bigr],
> $$
>
> where the **carrier** $e^{i(\omega_ct - k_cx)}$ has wave number $k_c > 0$ and frequency $\omega_c$, and the complex **envelope** $E$ varies slowly on the scale of a wavelength and of a period. Equivalently, its spectrum is concentrated in a band of width $\Delta k \ll k_c$ around $k_c$ (in frequency, $\Delta\omega \ll \omega_c$ around $\omega_c$), the **bandwidth**. At a fixed place a packet is a signal $\operatorname{Re}[E(t)e^{i\omega_ct}]$, a tone whose amplitude and phase change slowly.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §1–2*

^def-b6-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: The Gaussian packet
> The standard example is the Gaussian packet $e^{-(x - x_0)^2/2\sigma_x^2}\,e^{\pm ik_cx}$, whose spectrum is a Gaussian of width $\sigma_k = 1/\sigma_x$ centred on $\pm k_c$ and multiplied by the phase $e^{-i(k \mp k_c)x_0}$ that records where the packet sits ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-5|Theorem §B4.4.5]]). A packet of a few wavelengths needs a broad band; a long one, a narrow band; the carrier only sets where the band sits. Schwartz's $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_k$ are widths of the **amplitudes**; the rms widths of $|f|^2$ and $|\tilde f|^2$ are $\sqrt2$ times smaller, with product $\tfrac12$, the smallest possible, and the full width at half maximum of the amplitude is $2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\sigma_x = 2.35\,\sigma_x$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §1*

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.1: A Packet Is a Superposition of Travelling Waves
> Let a medium have the dispersion relation $\omega(k)$ ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]]) with a branch of waves moving toward $+x$. Write the complex form of a packet at $t = 0$ as a superposition of spatial waves,
>
> $$
> \Psi(x,0) = \int\hat a(k)\,e^{-ikx}\,dk, \qquad \hat a(k) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int\Psi(x,0)\,e^{+ikx}\,dx ,
> $$
>
> with $\hat a$ concentrated at $k > 0$. Then the packet at time $t$ is $\psi = \operatorname{Re}\Psi$ with
>
> $$
> \Psi(x,t) = \int\hat a(k)\,e^{i(\omega(k)t - kx)}\,dk :
> $$
>
> each component travels as a sinusoidal wave of its own frequency $\omega(k)$. In a nondispersive medium, $\omega = ck$, this is $\Psi(x,t) = \Psi(x - ct, 0)$: the packet moves rigidly at $c$, which is d'Alembert's solution ([[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]) seen through the transform.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §4, eqs. (13)–(19)*

^thm-b6-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\hat a$ is the spatial transform of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|Def. §B4.4.1]] evaluated at $-k$, so the first formula is Fourier inversion ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|Theorem §B4.4.1]]); the sign is chosen so that the component $\hat a(k)e^{-ikx}$ continues in time as $e^{i(\omega t - kx)}$, a wave toward $+x$ in the convention of these notes ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^cau-b4-4-1|Caution: Conventions]]). Each component $e^{i(\omega(k)t - kx)}$ solves the equation of motion by the definition of $\omega(k)$; the equation is linear, so the superposition does, and its real part is the physical wave ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]). At $t = 0$ it is the given packet; taking every component on the branch toward $+x$ fixes the initial velocity as well, which a second-order equation requires. If $\omega = ck$, $e^{i(ckt - kx)} = e^{-ik(x - ct)}$ and the integral is $\Psi(x - ct, 0)$.

^der-b6-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|Def. §B4.4.1]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|Theorem §B4.4.1]], [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]

> [!caution] Caution: Sign conventions for packets
> Schwartz (Lecture 11) and quantum mechanics write the components as $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ and synthesise with $e^{+ikx}$; these notes use $e^{i(\omega t - kx)}$ and therefore $e^{-ikx}$. The two are complex conjugates of each other and have the same real part, so the physical packet, the dispersion relation and all velocities agree; the signs of intermediate phases (such as $e^{\pm i(k - k_c)x_0}$ in the spectrum of a displaced packet) are opposite, and formulas taken from one convention into the other must be conjugated.

## Modulation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.1.2: Amplitude Modulation and Sidebands
> Let a slowly varying real signal $g(t)$ have spectrum $\tilde g(\omega)$, concentrated in $|\omega| \lesssim \Delta\omega$.
> 1. The modulated carrier $g(t)\cos\omega_ct$ has spectrum $\tfrac12\bigl[\tilde g(\omega - \omega_c) + \tilde g(\omega + \omega_c)\bigr]$: two copies of the message spectrum, at $\pm\omega_c$. A modulated carrier occupies the band from $\omega_c - \Delta\omega$ to $\omega_c + \Delta\omega$, twice as wide as the message.
> 2. Modulation by a single tone, with **modulation depth** $m$:
>
> $$
> \bigl[1 + m\cos\omega_mt\bigr]\cos\omega_ct = \cos\omega_ct + \frac m2\cos(\omega_c + \omega_m)t + \frac m2\cos(\omega_c - \omega_m)t ,
> $$
>
>    a carrier with two **sidebands** at $\omega_c \pm \omega_m$, each of amplitude $m/2$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §2*

^thm-b6-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) Write $\cos\omega_ct = \tfrac12(e^{i\omega_ct} + e^{-i\omega_ct})$ and apply linearity and the frequency-shift rule $e^{\pm i\omega_ct}g(t) \mapsto \tilde g(\omega \mp \omega_c)$ of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]]. (2) $\cos a\cos b = \tfrac12[\cos(a + b) + \cos(a - b)]$ with $a = \omega_ct$, $b = \omega_mt$ turns $m\cos\omega_mt\cos\omega_ct$ into the two sidebands; the term $1\cdot\cos\omega_ct$ is the carrier.

^der-b6-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Beats are the two-line packet
> With the carrier suppressed, $\cos\omega_mt\cos\omega_ct = \tfrac12\bigl[\cos(\omega_c - \omega_m)t + \cos(\omega_c + \omega_m)t\bigr]$: only the two sidebands remain. Read backwards, this is the beat identity ([[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]): two tones at $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are a carrier at their mean modulated at half their difference. Beats are therefore the simplest wave packet, with a periodic envelope that falls to zero; three lines (Theorem §B6.1.2, part 2, with $m < 1$) give a periodic envelope that does not; a single isolated packet needs a continuous band ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^rem-a3-3-2|QM Remark: From two waves to one packet]]). In space and time two travelling waves form groups that move at $\Delta\omega/\Delta k$ ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-2|QM Theorem §A3.3.2]]), the two-component case of [[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^thm-b6-1-1|Theorem §B6.1.1]] and the starting point of [[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity|§B6.2]]. Two weakly coupled oscillators exchanging energy are the same beat in a mechanical system ([[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange|§B2.2]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §2*

^rem-b6-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Carriers and channels
> A message of bandwidth $\Delta\omega$ needs a band $2\Delta\omega$ wherever it is put (Theorem §B6.1.2), and pulses of width $\sigma_t$ need $\Delta\omega \sim 1/\sigma_t$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^rem-b4-4-1|Remark: The general inequality, and the quantum case]]). The carrier adds two things. It moves the message to a frequency the medium transmits well (radio waves through air, near-infrared light through glass). And it lets many messages share one medium, each on its own carrier, as long as their bands do not overlap. For the envelope to be well defined the carrier must oscillate many times per pulse, $\omega_c \gg \Delta\omega$. AM radio, cell phones (GHz carriers) and optical fibres (carriers near $2 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$) all work this way; in a dispersive medium the pulses also spread as they travel ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity|§B6.2]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §2 and §6*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.1.1: A Note Switched On and Off
> A $40\ \mathrm{Hz}$ note is turned on and off once a second by multiplying it by $\sin(2\pi\cdot 1\ \mathrm{Hz}\cdot t)$:
>
> $$
> \sin(2\pi\,1t)\,\sin(2\pi\,40t) = \tfrac12\bigl[\cos(2\pi\,39t) - \cos(2\pi\,41t)\bigr] \qquad (t \text{ in s}),
> $$
>
> by $\sin a\sin b = \tfrac12[\cos(a - b) - \cos(a + b)]$. The spectrum has no line at $40\ \mathrm{Hz}$ at all, only $39$ and $41\ \mathrm{Hz}$; one hears a $40\ \mathrm{Hz}$ tone beating at $2\ \mathrm{Hz}$, since the envelope $|\sin 2\pi t|$ peaks twice per second ([[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]). Modulating instead by $1 + m\sin(2\pi t)$ with $m < 1$, so that the note never switches off completely, restores the $40\ \mathrm{Hz}$ line between the sidebands ([[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^thm-b6-1-2|Theorem §B6.1.2]]). Either way the signal is periodic and carries no information; adding a few more lines within a few hertz of $40\ \mathrm{Hz}$ produces an irregular envelope, which can.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §2*

^ex-b6-1-1

> [!example] Example §B6.1.2: Pulses on a Carrier
> A message is sent as Gaussian pulses of amplitude width $\sigma_t = 10\ \mathrm s$, one every $100\ \mathrm s$. What band does it need, and is a carrier of $1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ or of $0.1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ adequate?
>
> Each pulse has $\sigma_\omega = 1/\sigma_t = 0.10\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ ([[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^rem-b6-1-1|Remark: The Gaussian packet]]), that is $\sigma_f = \sigma_\omega/2\pi = 0.016\ \mathrm{Hz}$; the spectrum is negligible beyond about $3\sigma_f = 0.05\ \mathrm{Hz}$ from the carrier. The information rate is one pulse per $100\ \mathrm s$, $0.01\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
> - With $f_c = 1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ there are $f_c\sigma_t = 10$ carrier cycles per pulse width, and the envelope, which is what a receiver detects, reproduces the pulses faithfully.
> - With $f_c = 0.1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ a pulse contains only about one carrier cycle ($f_c\sigma_t = 1$, $\omega_c/\sigma_\omega = 6.3$): the signal is a single wiggle rather than a burst of oscillations, and a receiver that finds the envelope by smoothing the squared signal over a few carrier cycles, as in Schwartz's plots, blurs the pulse. The condition $\omega_c \gg \sigma_\omega$ is only marginally met.
>
> (Schwartz quotes the width as "$\gamma = 1/\sigma = 0.1\ \mathrm{Hz}$"; $1/\sigma_t$ is an angular frequency, $0.1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and the band in hertz is $2\pi$ times narrower.)
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §2*

^ex-b6-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The spectrum of a modulated signal is the frequency-shift rule of the Fourier transform ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]]); the sharp duration–bandwidth inequality, with equality for Gaussians, is the Heisenberg inequality of Fourier analysis, which quantum mechanics turns into [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|QM Theorem §A3.5.1]].
> - Superposing travelling waves with weights $\hat a(k)$ is the continuum version of superposing normal modes ([[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]]); on an infinite medium the modes are labelled by a continuous $k$.
