On an ideal string every wave travels at the same speed, so a pulse keeps its shape ([[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]). In most media the speed depends on the wavelength: level A met this as the dispersion of light by glass ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^def-a4-3-2|Def. §A4.3.2]], [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]]), [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain|§B3.1]] defined the dispersion relation $\omega(k)$ and found it for the bead chain ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]], [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]), and quantum mechanics found that the crests of a matter wave and the particle move at different speeds ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-4|QM Theorem §A3.3.4]]). This section is the general home of phase and group velocity. It collects the dispersion relations of the common media, proves that a packet evolved exactly by Fourier synthesis ([[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^thm-b6-1-1|Theorem §B6.1.1]]) moves, to first order, with its envelope at the group velocity $d\omega/dk$ and its crests at the phase velocity $\omega/k$, and computes how it spreads at second order, which is where dispersion gets its name.

## Dispersion relations

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.1: Dispersion Relations from the Equation of Motion
> For a linear equation with constant coefficients, the dispersion relation ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]]) follows by substituting $e^{i(\omega t - kx)}$, which replaces $\partial_t$ by $i\omega$ and $\partial_x$ by $-ik$, and the equation becomes an algebraic condition on $(k, \omega)$. In particular:
> 1. the wave equation $\partial_t^2\psi = c^2\,\partial_x^2\psi$ gives $\omega^2 = c^2k^2$;
> 2. a wave equation with a restoring term, $\partial_t^2\psi = c^2\,\partial_x^2\psi - \omega_p^2\,\psi$, gives
>
> $$
> \omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2 ;
> $$
>
>    it describes a row of pendulums of length $\ell$ coupled by springs ($\omega_p^2 = g/\ell$) and light in a plasma ($\omega_p$ the plasma frequency, [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-2|Def. §B13.1.2]]).
>
> The bead chain, a difference equation in space, gives $\omega = 2\omega_0|\sin(ka/2)|$ in the same way ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]); its long-wave expansion $\omega \approx \omega_0ak\,[1 - (ka)^2/24]$ is the dispersion of the corrected continuum equation of [[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §3, eq. (8) · Series I §8.3, §9.3*

^thm-b6-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\partial_t e^{i(\omega t - kx)} = i\omega\,e^{i(\omega t - kx)}$ and $\partial_x e^{i(\omega t - kx)} = -ik\,e^{i(\omega t - kx)}$, so each derivative becomes a factor and the common exponential, never zero, divides out. (1) $-\omega^2 = -c^2k^2$. (2) $-\omega^2 = -c^2k^2 - \omega_p^2$. For the pendulums, the springs give the $c^2\,\partial_x^2$ term as in [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]], and gravity adds to each bob the restoring acceleration $-(g/\ell)\psi$ of a pendulum ([[§A1.4 Pendulums|§A1.4]]). For the chain, see the derivation of [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]].

## Phase velocity and group velocity

> [!definition] Definition §B6.2.1: Phase Velocity; Group Velocity
> For a dispersion relation $\omega(k)$, the **phase velocity** and the **group velocity** at wave number $k$ are
>
> $$
> v_p(k) = \frac{\omega(k)}{k}, \qquad v_g(k) = \frac{d\omega}{dk}(k) .
> $$
>
> On a graph of $\omega$ against $k$, $v_p$ is the slope of the chord from the origin and $v_g$ the slope of the tangent. The two agree for all $k$ exactly when $\omega = ck$. The matter-wave case is [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^def-a3-3-1|QM Def. §A3.3.1]] and [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^def-a3-3-2|QM Def. §A3.3.2]]; the relation $v_g = v_p + k\,dv_p/dk$ is [[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-3|QM Theorem §A3.3.3]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §4–5, eqs. (12) and (25)*

> [!model] Model §B6.2.2: Dispersive Medium Described by ω(k)
> A **dispersive medium described by $\omega(k)$** is a linear, homogeneous, lossless medium (real $\omega$ for real $k$) in which waves toward $+x$ follow one smooth, increasing branch $\omega(k)$, $k > 0$, and nothing else about the medium matters for wave propagation. Schwartz's catalogue, with the ranges of validity:
>
> | medium | $\omega(k)$ | $v_p = \omega/k$ | $v_g = d\omega/dk$ | valid when |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | ideal string, sound, light in vacuum | $ck$ | $c$ | $c$ | linear, lossless |
> | bead chain | $2\omega_0\sin(ka/2)$ | $\omega/k$ | $\omega_0a\cos(ka/2)$ | $0 \le ka \le \pi$ |
> | pendulum chain, light in a plasma | $\sqrt{\omega_p^2 + c^2k^2}$ | $> c$ | $c^2k/\omega < c$ | |
> | deep water | $\sqrt{gk}$ | $\sqrt{g/k}$ | $\tfrac12v_p$ | depth $\gg$ wavelength |
> | shallow water, depth $d$ | $\sqrt{gd}\,k$ | $\sqrt{gd}$ | $\sqrt{gd}$ | depth $\ll$ wavelength |
> | capillary ripples | $\sqrt{\sigma k^3/\rho}$ | $\sqrt{\sigma k/\rho}$ | $\tfrac32v_p$ | short waves, surface tension $\sigma$ dominates |
> | light in glass | $ck/n(k)$ | $c/n$ | $c/(n - \lambda\,dn/d\lambda)$ | transparent range ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2\|Law §A4.3.2]]) |
> | free matter wave | $\hbar k^2/2m$ | $\hbar k/2m$ | $\hbar k/m$ | non-relativistic ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-4\|QM Theorem §A3.3.4]]) |
>
> Absorption (complex $k$), several branches and waves in several dimensions lie outside the model.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §3 and §6 · Series I §8.3*

^mod-b6-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.3: The Envelope Moves at the Group Velocity
> Let the spectrum $\hat a(k)$ of a packet be concentrated within $\Delta k$ of a carrier $k_c$, write $\omega_c = \omega(k_c)$ and $E(x) = \Psi(x,0)\,e^{ik_cx}$ for its envelope at $t = 0$. If $\omega(k) = \omega_c + v_g\,(k - k_c)$ exactly, then
>
> $$
> \Psi(x,t) = E(x - v_gt)\;e^{i(\omega_ct - k_cx)}, \qquad v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk}(k_c) :
> $$
>
> the envelope moves rigidly at the group velocity, while the crests of the carrier move at the phase velocity $v_p = \omega_c/k_c$, sliding through the envelope at the relative speed $v_p - v_g$. For a general smooth $\omega(k)$ this holds approximately as long as the neglected phase $\tfrac12|\omega''(k_c)|\,\Delta k^2\,t$ stays small compared with $1$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §5, eqs. (20)–(28)*

^thm-b6-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $k = k_c + q$ in [[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^thm-b6-1-1|Theorem §B6.1.1]] and use $\omega = \omega_c + v_gq$:
>
> $$
> \Psi(x,t) = \int\hat a(k_c + q)\,e^{i[(\omega_c + v_gq)t - (k_c + q)x]}\,dq = e^{i(\omega_ct - k_cx)}\int\hat a(k_c + q)\,e^{-iq(x - v_gt)}\,dq .
> $$
>
> At $t = 0$ the same computation gives $\Psi(x,0) = e^{-ik_cx}\int\hat a(k_c + q)e^{-iqx}\,dq$, so the last integral is $E$ evaluated at $x - v_gt$. The phase $\omega_ct - k_cx$ is constant along $x = v_pt + \text{const}$. In general $\omega(k) = \omega_c + v_gq + \tfrac12\omega''(k_c)q^2 + \ldots$ (Taylor's theorem, [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]); the first neglected term changes the phase of the components by at most about $\tfrac12|\omega''|\Delta k^2t$, which is harmless while it is small. Schwartz writes the result as $e^{-ik_ct(v_g - v_p)}f(x - v_gt)$ with $f$ the full initial packet; with his convention $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ ([[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^cau-b6-1-1|Caution: Sign conventions for packets]]) the prefactor should read $e^{+ik_ct(v_g - v_p)}$, as one checks at $x = v_gt$, where the full phase must be $k_cv_gt - \omega_ct$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B6.2.4: Spreading of a Gaussian Packet
> Let $\omega(k) = \omega_c + v_g\,(k - k_c) + \tfrac12\Gamma\,(k - k_c)^2$ with $\Gamma = \omega''(k_c)$, and start from the Gaussian packet $\Psi(x,0) = e^{-(x - x_0)^2/2\sigma^2}e^{-ik_cx}$. Then
>
> $$
> \Psi(x,t) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 - i\Gamma t}}\,\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(x - x_0 - v_gt)^2}{2(\sigma^2 - i\Gamma t)}\Bigr]\,e^{i(\omega_ct - k_cx)}
> $$
>
> (principal square root), whose envelope is a Gaussian centred at $x_0 + v_gt$ with width and height
>
> $$
> \sigma(t) = \sigma\sqrt{1 + \frac{\Gamma^2t^2}{\sigma^4}}, \qquad |\Psi|_{\max} = \Bigl(1 + \frac{\Gamma^2t^2}{\sigma^4}\Bigr)^{-1/4} .
> $$
>
> The packet keeps its centre speed $v_g$ but broadens and flattens, with $\int|\Psi|^2dx$ constant. It hardly changes for $t \ll \tau = \sigma^2/|\Gamma|$; for $t \gg \tau$ it grows linearly, $\sigma(t) \approx |\Gamma|\,t/\sigma = (|\Gamma|\sigma_k)\,t$ with $\sigma_k = 1/\sigma$: the spread of group velocities across the band, times the time. If $\Gamma = 0$ there is no spreading.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §6, eqs. (29)–(36)*

^thm-b6-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the Gaussian, shift and modulation rules of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]], with $k \to -k$ as in [[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^thm-b6-1-1|Theorem §B6.1.1]], $\hat a(k_c + q) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\sigma^2q^2/2}\,e^{iqx_0}$. As in the previous derivation, now with the quadratic term,
>
> $$
> \Psi(x,t) = e^{i(\omega_ct - k_cx)}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-\alpha q^2/2}\,e^{-iqX}\,dq, \qquad \alpha = \sigma^2 - i\Gamma t, \quad X = x - x_0 - v_gt .
> $$
>
> *Lemma.* For $\operatorname{Re}\alpha > 0$, $J(X) = \int e^{-\alpha q^2/2 - iqX}dq = \sqrt{2\pi/\alpha}\;e^{-X^2/2\alpha}$. Differentiating in $X$ and integrating by parts as in [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^der-b4-4-2|the derivation of Theorem §B4.4.2]] gives $J'(X) = -(X/\alpha)J(X)$, so $J(X) = J(0)e^{-X^2/2\alpha}$. $J(0) = \int e^{-\alpha q^2/2}dq$ equals $\sqrt{2\pi/\alpha}$ for real $\alpha > 0$; both sides are analytic in $\alpha$ on $\operatorname{Re}\alpha > 0$, so they agree there (analytic continuation; Mathematical Methods, planned). Hence $\Psi = e^{i(\omega_ct - k_cx)}\,(\sigma/\sqrt\alpha)\,e^{-X^2/2\alpha}$.
>
> *Width and height.* $1/\alpha = (\sigma^2 + i\Gamma t)/(\sigma^4 + \Gamma^2t^2)$, so $|e^{-X^2/2\alpha}| = \exp\bigl[-X^2\sigma^2/2(\sigma^4 + \Gamma^2t^2)\bigr] = e^{-X^2/2\sigma(t)^2}$ with $\sigma(t)^2 = (\sigma^4 + \Gamma^2t^2)/\sigma^2$; and $|\sigma/\sqrt\alpha| = \sigma/(\sigma^4 + \Gamma^2t^2)^{1/4}$. Then $\int|\Psi|^2dx = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sqrt{\sigma^4 + \Gamma^2t^2}}\,\sqrt\pi\,\sigma(t) = \sqrt\pi\,\sigma$, constant. Schwartz's eq. (34) omits the prefactor $\sigma/\sqrt\alpha$, without which the packet would broaden at constant height and gain weight.

> [!remark] Remark: Why dispersion is called dispersion
> To first order in $k - k_c$ every dispersion relation looks linear and packets move rigidly ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^thm-b6-2-3|Theorem §B6.2.3]]); the curvature $\Gamma = \omega''$ is what makes a medium disperse packets. Different parts of the band have group velocities $v_g + \Gamma(k - k_c)$, so a packet built from a band $\sigma_k$ separates into its components at the rate $|\Gamma|\sigma_k$. A packet keeps its shape exactly only if $\omega$ is linear in $k$; if also $\omega(0) = 0$, crests and envelope move together. Short packets have broad bands and spread fastest ($\tau = \sigma^2/|\Gamma|$): in an optical fibre, pulses that are too short or too close together smear into each other, which limits the data rate. Silica fibres have both low loss and low dispersion near $1.3$–$1.5\ \mu\mathrm m$, the telecom band.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §6*

^rem-b6-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Faster than light?
> For $\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2$ (light in a plasma), $v_p\,v_g = (\omega/k)(c^2k/\omega) = c^2$, so $v_p > c$ while $v_g < c$. Nothing physical travels at $v_p$: a single infinite sinusoid carries no signal, and any signal is a packet ([[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^rem-b6-1-3|Remark: Carriers and channels]]), whose envelope moves at $v_g$. For a free electron, $\omega = \hbar k^2/2m$ gives $\Gamma = \hbar/m$ and spreading time $\tau = m\sigma^2/\hbar$; the more tightly a packet is localized, the faster it spreads ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^rem-a3-3-3|QM Remark: Matter waves are dispersive, so free packets spread]]). Schwartz notes that an electron localized to within its reduced Compton wavelength would spread at about $c$, where the non-relativistic dispersion relation has already failed.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §3 and §6*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B6.2.1: Waves on the Bead Chain
> For the chain, $\omega = 2\omega_0\sin(ka/2)$ for $0 \le ka \le \pi$ ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]]), so
>
> $$
> v_p = \frac{2\omega_0\sin(ka/2)}{k}, \qquad v_g = \omega_0a\cos\frac{ka}{2}, \qquad \frac{v_g}{v_p} = \frac{ka}{2}\cot\frac{ka}{2} \le 1 .
> $$
>
> For long waves, $ka \to 0$, both tend to $\omega_0a$, the string's $c$. At $ka = \pi/2$ (four beads per wavelength) $v_g/v_p = \pi/4 = 0.79$; at $ka = 2\pi/3$, $v_p = 0.83\,\omega_0a$ and $v_g = 0.50\,\omega_0a$ (figure above). At $ka = \pi$, the shortest wavelength $2a$, $v_g = 0$ although $v_p = 2\omega_0a/\pi$: the zigzag mode, with each bead opposite its neighbours, is a standing pattern that transports no energy. Packets on the chain slow down as their wavelength approaches the bead spacing, the "memory of the graininess" the series points to in the frequency ceiling $2\omega_0$.
>
> *Source: Series I §8.3, §9.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §5*

^ex-b6-2-1

> [!example] Example §B6.2.2: Deep Water, and a Medium with ω = ak²
> (a) In a medium with $\omega = \sqrt{ak}$ ($a$ in $\mathrm{m/s^2}$), $v_p = \sqrt{a/k}$ and $v_g = \tfrac12\sqrt a\,k^{-1/2} = \tfrac12v_p$. If the medium carries light and its index is defined by $v_p = c/n$, with the vacuum wave number $k_0 = \omega/c$: from $\omega = \sqrt{ak} = ck_0$, $k = c^2k_0^2/a$, so
>
> $$
> n = \frac{c}{v_p} = \frac{ck}{\omega} = \frac{c}{ck_0}\cdot\frac{c^2k_0^2}{a} = \frac{c^2}{a}\,k_0 .
> $$
>
> With $a = g$ this is deep water ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^mod-b6-2-2|Model §B6.2.2]]). An ocean swell of wavelength $100\ \mathrm m$ has $k = 0.0628\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $v_p = \sqrt{g/k} = 12.5\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (period $8.0\ \mathrm s$) and $v_g = 6.2\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Groups of swell, and the energy of a distant storm, arrive at $6.2\ \mathrm{m/s}$; inside each group the crests run forward at twice that speed, appearing at the back and vanishing at the front (figure above). Longer waves are faster, so the long swell from a storm arrives first.
>
> (b) In a medium with $\omega = ak^2$ ($a$ in $\mathrm{m^2/s}$), $v_p = ak$ and $v_g = 2ak = 2v_p$: crests run backward through the packet, as for matter waves, whose $\omega = \hbar k^2/2m$ has this form with $a = \hbar/2m$. With $k = \sqrt{ck_0/a}$, $n = c/(ak) = \sqrt{c/(ak_0)}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Midterm 2 (a) and Midterm 2 practice (b), with solutions · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §3*

^ex-b6-2-2

> [!example] Example §B6.2.3: Group Index of Crown Glass
> For light in glass, $k = 2\pi n/\lambda$ and $\omega = 2\pi c/\lambda$ with $\lambda$ the vacuum wavelength, so
>
> $$
> v_g = \frac{d\omega/d\lambda}{dk/d\lambda} = \frac{-2\pi c/\lambda^2}{2\pi\bigl(\lambda\,dn/d\lambda - n\bigr)/\lambda^2} = \frac{c}{n - \lambda\,dn/d\lambda} = \frac{c}{n_g} ,
> $$
>
> which defines the **group index** $n_g$. For normal dispersion ($dn/d\lambda < 0$, [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]]), $n_g > n$ and pulses travel slower than the crests. For crown glass the table gives $n = 1.518$ at $580\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $dn/d\lambda \approx (1.512 - 1.524)/(660 - 470)\ \mathrm{nm} = -6.3 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$, so
>
> $$
> n_g = 1.518 + (580)(6.3 \times 10^{-5}) = 1.555, \qquad v_g = \frac{c}{n_g} = 1.93 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad v_p = \frac{c}{n} = 1.98 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> Through $1\ \mathrm{km}$ of such glass a pulse arrives $L(n_g - n)/c = 0.12\ \mu\mathrm s$ later than the phase velocity would suggest. Since $dn/d\lambda$ itself changes with $\lambda$ (Cauchy's formula, [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^rem-a4-3-3|Remark: Where dispersion comes from]]), $\Gamma \neq 0$ and pulses in glass also spread; the three-decimal table is too coarse to measure that change.
>
> *Source: data from UP3 Table 1.2 (via [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]]); the group velocity from Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §5*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Expanding $\omega(k)$ to first and second order is Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]); the Gaussian integral with complex width needs analytic continuation in the width (Mathematical Methods, planned), and the real Gaussian integral is [[§15 Multivariable Integration#^ex-15-7|452 Ex. §15.7]].
> - In quantum mechanics $v_g = d\omega/dk = dE/dp$ is the particle velocity ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-4|QM Theorem §A3.3.4]]), and the spreading of [[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^thm-b6-2-4|Theorem §B6.2.4]] with $\Gamma = \hbar/m$ is the spreading of a free Gaussian wave function.
> - Dispersion of light from the driven-oscillator model of bound electrons: Chapter B13 (planned) ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^rem-a4-3-3|Remark: Where dispersion comes from]]). In two and three dimensions $\vec k$ is a vector ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]) and the group velocity generalises to $\nabla_{\vec k}\,\omega$, which need not be parallel to $\vec k$ on the anisotropic lattice of [[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^rem-b7-1-1|Remark: The Laplacian has no preferred direction]] (not developed here).
