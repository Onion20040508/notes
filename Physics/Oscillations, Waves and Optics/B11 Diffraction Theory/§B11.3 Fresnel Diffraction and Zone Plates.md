*Sources: Fowles, Introduction to Modern Optics, §5.5.*

What does the light look like close behind an aperture, or at the edge of a shadow, where the curvature of the wave fronts cannot be neglected ([[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^def-b11-1-2|Def. §B11.1.2]])? Level A left this near-field case out ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^mod-a7-1-1|Model §A7.1.1]]); it is the diffraction one actually sees around ordinary shadows ([[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^def-a4-4-2|Def. §A4.4.2]]). This section keeps the quadratic term of the path length. For points on the axis of a circular opening the integral can be done exactly, and Fresnel's zone construction becomes a theorem: it explains why an unobstructed wave is "half the first zone", why the centre of a round shadow is bright, and how a plate of rings focuses light. For rectangular openings the integral reduces to the Fresnel integrals and their graph, the Cornu spiral, which gives the fringes at a straight edge.

## Fresnel zones

> [!definition] Definition §B11.3.1: Fresnel Zones
> A point source $S$ and a receiving point $P$ lie on a line perpendicular to a plane, at distances $h'$ and $h$ from it, meeting it at $O$. For a point $Q$ of the plane at distance $R$ from $O$, in the paraxial (Fresnel) approximation ([[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-5|Theorem §B11.1.5]] with $\vec x_P = \vec x_S = 0$),
>
> $$
> r + r' = h + h' + \frac{R^2}{2L}, \qquad \frac1L = \frac1h + \frac1{h'} .
> $$
>
> The **Fresnel zones** (half-period zones) of $P$ are the rings on which $r + r'$ exceeds $h + h'$ by between $(n - 1)\lambda/2$ and $n\lambda/2$, $n = 1, 2, \dots$ Their outer radii are
>
> $$
> R_n = \sqrt{n\lambda L},
> $$
>
> and every zone has the same area $\pi R_n^2 - \pi R_{n-1}^2 = \pi\lambda L$. An opening of radius $a$ centred on $O$ exposes $N_F = a^2/(\lambda L)$ zones, its Fresnel number.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5, eqs. (5.35)–(5.36)*

^def-b11-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.3.1: On-Axis Field of a Circular Aperture
> In the paraxial approximation (aperture radius $a \ll h, h'$; obliquity factor $\approx 1$), a circular opening of radius $a$ centred on $O$ produces at $P$
>
> $$
> U(P) = U_{\text{u}}\,\bigl(1 - e^{i\pi N_F}\bigr), \qquad I(P) = 4I_{\text{u}}\sin^2\Bigl(\frac{\pi N_F}{2}\Bigr), \qquad N_F = \frac{a^2}{\lambda L},
> $$
>
> where $U_{\text{u}} = U_0e^{ik(h + h')}/(h + h')$ is the unobstructed spherical wave at $P$ and $I_{\text{u}} = \lvert U_{\text{u}}\rvert^2$. Equivalently, the $n$-th Fresnel zone alone contributes $U_n = 2(-1)^{n-1}U_{\text{u}}$: all zones contribute equally, with alternating signs. An opening of an odd number of zones gives four times the unobstructed irradiance at $P$, one of an even number gives darkness.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5, eq. (5.37) and the discussion of the circular aperture; the closed form is derived here from Fowles eq. (5.12)*

^thm-b11-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]] set $K = 1$, $1/(rr') = 1/(hh')$, and use the phase of [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^def-b11-3-1|Def. §B11.3.1]]. With rings of area $2\pi R\,dR$,
>
> $$
> U(P) = -\frac{i}{\lambda}\,\frac{U_0e^{ik(h + h')}}{hh'}\int_0^a e^{ikR^2/2L}\,2\pi R\,dR = -\frac{i}{\lambda}\,\frac{U_0e^{ik(h + h')}}{hh'}\cdot\frac{2\pi L}{ik}\bigl(e^{ika^2/2L} - 1\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The prefactor is $-\frac{i}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{2\pi L}{ik} = -L$, and $L/(hh') = 1/(h + h')$, so $U(P) = U_{\text{u}}\bigl(1 - e^{ika^2/2L}\bigr)$ with $ka^2/2L = \pi a^2/(\lambda L) = \pi N_F$. Then $\lvert1 - e^{i\phi}\rvert^2 = 2 - 2\cos\phi = 4\sin^2(\phi/2)$. The $n$-th zone is the difference of the openings with $N_F = n$ and $N_F = n - 1$: $U_n = U_{\text{u}}\bigl(e^{i\pi(n-1)} - e^{i\pi n}\bigr) = 2(-1)^{n-1}U_{\text{u}}$.

^der-b11-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]], [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^def-b11-3-1|Def. §B11.3.1]], [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-5|Theorem §B11.1.5]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.3.2: The Free Wave Is Half the First Zone
> The disturbance at $P$ with no screen at all equals half the contribution of the first Fresnel zone alone:
>
> $$
> U_{\text{u}} = \tfrac12U_1 .
> $$
>
> Hence the first zone alone, an opening of radius $\sqrt{\lambda L}$, gives twice the free amplitude and four times the free irradiance at $P$. The Fresnel–Kirchhoff formula, applied to an unobstructed wave front, reproduces the wave itself: the factor $-i/\lambda$ of [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]] is exactly what this requires.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5, eq. (5.38), and Problem 5.17*

^thm-b11-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The first statement is [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-1|Theorem §B11.3.1]] for $n = 1$: $U_1 = 2U_{\text{u}}$. Without a screen the sum runs over all zones, and the paraxial formula alone is ambiguous there, since $1 - e^{i\pi N_F}$ keeps oscillating as $N_F \to \infty$. What settles it is that the true zone contributions shrink slowly with $n$: the obliquity factor and the factor $1/(rr')$, set to constants above, decrease for the far zones. Write the alternating sum with magnitudes $m_n = \lvert U_n\rvert$, slowly decreasing to $0$, as Fowles does:
>
> $$
> m_1 - m_2 + m_3 - \dots = \frac{m_1}{2} + \Bigl(\frac{m_1}{2} - m_2 + \frac{m_3}{2}\Bigr) + \Bigl(\frac{m_3}{2} - m_4 + \frac{m_5}{2}\Bigr) + \dots
> $$
>
> Each bracket compares $m_n$ with the mean of its neighbours and nearly vanishes when the decrease is slow and smooth, so the sum is $m_1/2$. Equivalently, the oscillating term $e^{i\pi N_F}$ averages away when the outer edge is blurred over many zones (a slowly varying cutoff). Both give $U_{\text{u}} = \frac12U_1$, consistent with $U_1 = 2U_{\text{u}}$.

^der-b11-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-1|Theorem §B11.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: How big the zones are
> For $h = h' = 50\ \mathrm{cm}$ and $\lambda = 600\ \mathrm{nm}$, $L = 25\ \mathrm{cm}$, $R_1 = \sqrt{\lambda L} = 0.39\ \mathrm{mm}$, and the hundredth zone has radius only $3.9\ \mathrm{mm}$. In ordinary situations an opening contains thousands of zones, the oscillating term averages away, and the light behaves like rays. Wave optics becomes visible when an edge, an obstacle or an opening is comparable to the first few zones. This is the zone version of the criterion of [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^def-b11-1-2|Def. §B11.1.2]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5*

^rem-b11-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.3.3: The Bright Spot behind a Disk
> On the axis behind an opaque disk of radius $a$, in the paraxial approximation,
>
> $$
> U(P) = U_{\text{u}}\,e^{i\pi N_F}, \qquad I(P) = I_{\text{u}} :
> $$
>
> at the centre of the geometrical shadow of a disk there is a bright spot, as bright as if the disk were absent (the **Poisson–Arago spot**).
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5*

^thm-b11-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The disk and the circular opening of the same radius are complementary screens. By Babinet's principle ([[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]]) and [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-1|Theorem §B11.3.1]], $U_{\text{disk}} = U_{\text{u}} - U_{\text{u}}(1 - e^{i\pi N_F}) = U_{\text{u}}e^{i\pi N_F}$, of modulus $\lvert U_{\text{u}}\rvert$. In Fowles's words: start the zone construction at the edge of the disk; the free zones then sum to half the first unobstructed zone, whose contribution has the same size as the free wave's first zone.

^der-b11-3-3

*Uses:* [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]], [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-1|Theorem §B11.3.1]], [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-2|Theorem §B11.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Shadows of irregular objects
> Poisson derived the bright spot in 1818 as an absurd consequence of Fresnel's wave theory; Arago looked and found it. For a disk the spot needs an edge circular to a small fraction of the first zone. Behind an irregular obstacle the zones seen from $P$ are partly blocked: from a point in the illuminated region the first zones are free and the far ones partly covered, so the sum, dominated by the first zones, is hardly changed; from a point deep in the shadow the central zones are covered and the partly blocked outer ones cancel almost completely. So the shadow is dark and the light region light, roughly as geometrical optics says, and fringes appear around the shadow only where the edge is smooth on the scale of the first zone.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5*

^rem-b11-3-2

## The zone plate

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.3.4: Zone Plate
> A **zone plate** is a screen with transparent rings at the radii $\sqrt{n\lambda f}$, alternate rings (say the even ones) being opaque. For light of wavelength $\lambda$ it focuses like a lens of focal length
>
> $$
> f = \frac{R_1^2}{\lambda} : \qquad \frac{1}{h} + \frac{1}{h'} = \frac1f
> $$
>
> for a source at $h'$ and its image at $h$. With $N$ open zones the amplitude at the image point is $2N$ times, and the irradiance $4N^2$ times, the unobstructed value (paraxial approximation). The focal length is inversely proportional to the wavelength: a zone plate is strongly chromatic.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5, eqs. (5.39)–(5.40)*

^thm-b11-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The ring radii are the Fresnel-zone radii $R_n = \sqrt{n\lambda L}$ of [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^def-b11-3-1|Def. §B11.3.1]] exactly for those pairs $S$, $P$ with $L = f$, i.e. $1/h + 1/h' = 1/f$. For such a pair each open zone contributes $2(-1)^{n-1}U_{\text{u}}$ ([[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-1|Theorem §B11.3.1]]); with only the odd zones open, all contributions have the same sign and add to $2NU_{\text{u}}$. For other pairs the open rings do not coincide with zones, and their contributions partly cancel. Since $f = R_1^2/\lambda$, a plate made for one wavelength has a different focal length for every other.

^der-b11-3-4

*Uses:* [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^def-b11-3-1|Def. §B11.3.1]], [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-1|Theorem §B11.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: A lens made of rings
> The zone plate is the image-forming element of the wave picture: a glass lens equalises the optical paths to the focus, a zone plate removes the zones whose paths are out of step by half a wavelength. It is made photographically from a drawing of rings and works for radiation where lenses are hard to make, such as X-rays. Unlike a lens it also has weaker foci: at the distance $f/3$ every ring covers three Fresnel zones, of which two cancel, and the plate again concentrates light, more weakly; likewise at $f/5, \dots$. A plate whose transmission varies sinusoidally with $R^2$ instead of in steps has, besides the undiffracted light, only the focus at $f$ and a virtual one at $-f$: the analogue of a sinusoidal grating, which has only the orders $0$ and $\pm1$ ([[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography|§B11.4]]); the hologram of a point source is such a plate.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5 (zone plates made photographically; chromatic); the secondary foci and the sinusoidal plate are added here*

## Rectangular apertures, Fresnel integrals and the Cornu spiral

> [!definition] Definition §B11.3.2: Fresnel Integrals; Cornu Spiral
> The **Fresnel integrals** are
>
> $$
> C(s) = \int_0^s\cos\Bigl(\frac{\pi w^2}{2}\Bigr)dw, \qquad S(s) = \int_0^s\sin\Bigl(\frac{\pi w^2}{2}\Bigr)dw, \qquad \int_0^se^{i\pi w^2/2}\,dw = C(s) + iS(s) .
> $$
>
> Both are odd functions of $s$ and tend to $\pm\frac12$ as $s \to \pm\infty$. The **Cornu spiral** is the curve $s \mapsto \bigl(C(s), S(s)\bigr)$ in the plane. Since $dC^2 + dS^2 = ds^2$, $s$ is its arc length, and its direction at $s$ makes the angle $\pi s^2/2$ with the $C$-axis, so its curvature $\pi s$ grows in proportion to the arc length; it winds into the points $Z_\pm = (\pm\frac12, \pm\frac12)$. The integral from $s_1$ to $s_2$ is the chord from the point $s_1$ to the point $s_2$ of the spiral.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5, eqs. (5.45)–(5.46) and Table 5.2*

^def-b11-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.3.5: Fresnel Diffraction by a Rectangular Aperture
> Let the rectangle $x_1 < x < x_2$, $y_1 < y < y_2$ in the aperture plane be open, with coordinates measured from the foot $O$ of the line $SP$, and set
>
> $$
> u = x\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda L}}, \qquad v = y\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda L}} .
> $$
>
> In the paraxial approximation,
>
> $$
> U(P) = \frac{U_{\text{u}}}{(1 + i)^2}\,\Bigl[C(u) + iS(u)\Bigr]_{u_1}^{u_2}\Bigl[C(v) + iS(v)\Bigr]_{v_1}^{v_2} .
> $$
>
> Special cases: a long **slit** ($u_1 = -\infty$, $u_2 = \infty$),
>
> $$
> U(P) = \frac{U_{\text{u}}}{1 + i}\Bigl[C(v) + iS(v)\Bigr]_{v_1}^{v_2},
> $$
>
> and a **straightedge** (also $v_1 = -\infty$), with the edge at $v_2 = v$:
>
> $$
> U(P) = \frac{U_{\text{u}}}{1 + i}\Bigl\{\bigl[C(v) + \tfrac12\bigr] + i\bigl[S(v) + \tfrac12\bigr]\Bigr\} .
> $$
>
> Moving $P$ across the pattern is the same as moving the aperture edges the other way.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5, eqs. (5.41)–(5.49)*

^thm-b11-3-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> As in [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-1|Theorem §B11.3.1]], with $R^2 = x^2 + y^2$ the integrand factorises:
>
> $$
> U(P) = -\frac{i}{\lambda}\,\frac{U_0e^{ik(h + h')}}{hh'}\int_{x_1}^{x_2}e^{ikx^2/2L}\,dx\int_{y_1}^{y_2}e^{iky^2/2L}\,dy .
> $$
>
> With $x = u\sqrt{\lambda L/2}$, $kx^2/2L = \pi u^2/2$ and $dx = \sqrt{\lambda L/2}\,du$; likewise for $y$. The product of the two Jacobians is $\lambda L/2$, so the prefactor becomes $-\frac{i}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\lambda L}{2}\cdot\frac{U_0e^{ik(h + h')}}{hh'} = -\frac i2U_{\text{u}} = \frac{U_{\text{u}}}{2i} = \frac{U_{\text{u}}}{(1 + i)^2}$, using $L/(hh') = 1/(h + h')$ and $(1 + i)^2 = 2i$. An infinite range gives $[C + iS]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = (\frac12 + \frac i2) - (-\frac12 - \frac i2) = 1 + i$; this yields the slit, and with $[C + iS]_{-\infty}^{v} = C(v) + \frac12 + i\bigl(S(v) + \frac12\bigr)$ the straightedge. With no screen, $U = U_{\text{u}}(1 + i)^2/(1 + i)^2 = U_{\text{u}}$, as it must.

^der-b11-3-5

*Uses:* [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]], [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^def-b11-3-1|Def. §B11.3.1]], [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^def-b11-3-2|Def. §B11.3.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.3.6: Fresnel Diffraction at a Straightedge
> With $v$ the position of the edge in the units of [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-5|Theorem §B11.3.5]] ($v > 0$: $P$ in the illuminated region, $v < 0$: in the geometrical shadow), the irradiance is
>
> $$
> \frac{I}{I_{\text{u}}} = \frac12\Bigl\{\bigl[C(v) + \tfrac12\bigr]^2 + \bigl[S(v) + \tfrac12\bigr]^2\Bigr\} .
> $$
>
> - At the geometrical shadow edge ($v = 0$) the amplitude is half and the irradiance a quarter of the unobstructed value.
> - In the shadow ($v \to -\infty$) the irradiance falls monotonically, as $I/I_{\text{u}} \approx 1/(2\pi^2v^2)$.
> - In the illuminated region it oscillates about $I_{\text{u}}$ with decreasing amplitude and decreasing spacing; the highest maximum, $1.37\,I_{\text{u}}$, is just inside the light at $v = 1.22$, and the following minimum, $0.78\,I_{\text{u}}$, at $v = 1.87$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5, eqs. (5.49)–(5.50) and Fig. 5.25*

^thm-b11-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Square the modulus of the straightedge formula of [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-5|Theorem §B11.3.5]], with $\lvert1 + i\rvert^2 = 2$. At $v = 0$, $C = S = 0$ and $I/I_{\text{u}} = \frac12(\frac14 + \frac14) = \frac14$. On the spiral, $\lvert U\rvert/\lvert U_{\text{u}}\rvert$ is the length of the chord from $Z_- = (-\frac12, -\frac12)$ to the point $v$, divided by the length $\sqrt2$ of the chord $Z_-Z_+$. As $v$ decreases from $0$ the point runs into $Z_-$ and the chord shrinks steadily. Quantitatively, integrating by parts, $\int_{-\infty}^ve^{i\pi w^2/2}dw = \frac{e^{i\pi v^2/2}}{i\pi v} + O(\lvert v\rvert^{-3})$ for $v \to -\infty$, so $I/I_{\text{u}} \approx \frac12\cdot\frac{1}{\pi^2v^2}$. As $v$ increases the point winds around $Z_+$ on circles of radius about $1/(\pi v)$; the chord from $Z_-$ is alternately longer and shorter than $Z_-Z_+$, at an angular rate $\pi v^2/2$ that grows with $v$. The extreme values $1.37$ at $v = 1.217$ and $0.78$ at $v = 1.873$ come from the tabulated integrals (or a numerical evaluation).

*Uses:* [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-5|Theorem §B11.3.5]], [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^def-b11-3-2|Def. §B11.3.2]]

*Left: the Cornu spiral $(C(s), S(s))$ for $-6 \le s \le 6$, computed from the Fresnel integrals, with a few values of $s$ marked; it winds into $Z_\pm = \pm(\frac12, \frac12)$ (red dots). The red arrow, from $Z_-$ to the point $v = 1.22$, is the straightedge amplitude at its brightest fringe; it is longer than the chord $Z_-Z_+$ of the unobstructed wave. > [!remark] Remark: The spiral is a phasor diagram
> Level A added the wavelets across a slit as a chain of equal phasors, each turned by the same angle, which curls into a circular arc ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^der-a7-1-3|derivation of Theorem §A7.1.3]]). In the Fresnel case each strip $dv$ of the aperture contributes a phasor of length $dv$ at the angle $\pi v^2/2$: the turning rate grows along the chain, and the arc becomes the Cornu spiral. The resultant from any part of the aperture is the chord between the corresponding points. The far zones, near $Z_\pm$, wind on tighter and tighter circles and contribute little: the light at $P$ comes from the first few zones, which is why the paraxial approximation works even though the integrals run to infinity. In the Fraunhofer limit the opening covers only a short piece of the spiral near its straight middle, and one is back to a single circular arc.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5 (graphical evaluation with the Cornu spiral)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B11.3.1: An Annular Opening
> A point source ($\lambda = 500\ \mathrm{nm}$) is $1\ \mathrm m$ in front of a circular hole of radius $1\ \mathrm{mm}$, in whose centre sits an opaque disk of radius $0.5\ \mathrm{mm}$; $P$ is $1\ \mathrm m$ behind. What is the irradiance at $P$ compared with no screen? Here $L = 0.5\ \mathrm m$ and $\lambda L = 2.5 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, so the hole edge is at $N_F = (10^{-3})^2/(2.5 \times 10^{-7}) = 4$ and the disk edge at $N_F = 1$. By [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-1|Theorem §B11.3.1]] the ring is the hole minus the disk-sized opening:
>
> $$
> U = U_{\text{u}}\bigl[(1 - e^{4\pi i}) - (1 - e^{\pi i})\bigr] = U_{\text{u}}\bigl(e^{\pi i} - e^{4\pi i}\bigr) = -2U_{\text{u}}, \qquad I = 4I_{\text{u}} .
> $$
>
> In zones: zones $2$, $3$, $4$ are open and contribute $-2$, $+2$, $-2$ times $U_{\text{u}}$; two cancel and one remains. Blocking the centre of the hole has made $P$ four times brighter than with no screen at all.
>
> *Source: Fowles, Problem 5.15 (the OCR text garbles the disk radius; $0.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ is assumed)*

^ex-b11-3-1

> [!example] Example §B11.3.2: A Zone Plate in White Light
> With Fowles's numbers, $h = h' = 50\ \mathrm{cm}$ and $\lambda = 600\ \mathrm{nm}$, the first zone has radius $R_1 = \sqrt{\lambda L} = 0.387\ \mathrm{mm}$. A zone plate with this $R_1$ has, by [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-4|Theorem §B11.3.4]], $f = R_1^2/\lambda = 25\ \mathrm{cm}$ at $600\ \mathrm{nm}$, so it images a point $50\ \mathrm{cm}$ away onto a point $50\ \mathrm{cm}$ behind it. In violet light ($400\ \mathrm{nm}$) the same plate has $f = 37.5\ \mathrm{cm}$, in deep red ($700\ \mathrm{nm}$) $f = 21.4\ \mathrm{cm}$. A glass lens of the same focal length changes $f$ by a few percent over the visible spectrum ([[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^rem-a5-3-3|Remark: Oblique parallel rays; chromatic aberration]]); the zone plate by a factor $1.75$, and in the opposite sense (blue focuses farther).
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.5 (zone radii for $h = h' = 50\ \mathrm{cm}$, $\lambda = 600\ \mathrm{nm}$); the plate is constructed here*

^ex-b11-3-2

> [!example] Example §B11.3.3: A Radio Source Disappearing behind the Moon
> A radio telescope observes a distant point source at $\lambda = 20\ \mathrm{cm}$ while the Moon's limb, treated as a straight edge, passes in front of it. The source is at infinity, so $L = h = 3.84 \times 10^8\ \mathrm m$, the Earth–Moon distance, and the unit of $v$ in [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-6|Theorem §B11.3.6]] corresponds to
>
> $$
> \sqrt{\frac{\lambda L}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{(0.20)(3.84 \times 10^8)}{2}}\ \mathrm m = 6.2\ \mathrm{km}
> $$
>
> at the Moon. The recorder traces the straightedge pattern as the edge moves. From the first maximum ($v = 1.22$) to the first minimum ($v = 1.87$) the edge moves $0.655 \times 6.2\ \mathrm{km} = 4.1\ \mathrm{km}$.
