Level A found the single-slit pattern by adding phasors ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-3|Theorem §A7.1.3]]), the double slit as interference times diffraction ([[§A7.2 Double-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-2-1|Theorem §A7.2.1]]), the grating from $N$ point sources ([[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]], [[§A7.3 Diffraction Gratings#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]), and could only motivate the factor $1.22$ of a circular aperture ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-1|Theorem §A7.4.1]]). In the Fraunhofer limit of the Fresnel–Kirchhoff formula ([[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^def-b11-1-2|Def. §B11.1.2]]) all of these are one integral of a phase factor over the opening. This section evaluates it for the rectangle, the circle (with the Bessel function $J_1$, which turns the $1.22$ into a theorem) and arrays of apertures, and makes the resolution of gratings and lenses quantitative.

## The Fraunhofer integral

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.1: Fraunhofer Diffraction Integral
> A plane monochromatic wave falls normally on an aperture $\mathcal A$ in the plane $z = 0$, with points $(x, y)$; a lens of focal length $f$ behind it collects the diffracted light in its focal plane, with points $(X, Y)$. Light leaving the aperture in the direction with transverse direction cosines
>
> $$
> \sin\theta_x = \frac{X}{f}, \qquad \sin\theta_y = \frac{Y}{f}
> $$
>
> arrives at $(X, Y)$, and in [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^mod-b11-1-1|Model §B11.1.1]] with small diffraction angles
>
> $$
> U(X, Y) = C\iint_{\mathcal A}e^{-ik(x\sin\theta_x + y\sin\theta_y)}\,dx\,dy ,
> $$
>
> with a constant $C$. The same holds without the lens on a screen at distance $d$ in the far field, $d \gg a^2/\lambda$, with $f$ replaced by $d$ and $C$ by a constant times a phase factor that does not depend on the aperture. Oblique incidence at direction sines $(\sin\theta_{x0}, \sin\theta_{y0})$ replaces $\sin\theta_x$ by $\sin\theta_x - \sin\theta_{x0}$ (and likewise in $y$): the whole pattern shifts.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.4, eq. (5.16), and §5.6, eqs. (5.50)–(5.51) · PHY 300, Worksheet 11 and Final practice exam (formula sheet)*

^thm-b11-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Start from [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]]. For small angles the obliquity factor $K$ hardly changes over the aperture, and neither does $1/(rr')$; both go into $C$. Only the phase $k(r + r')$ varies fast. The incident wave is plane, so its phase is the same over the aperture, and the diffracted light that reaches a point of the focal plane is a parallel bundle: by geometrical optics a lens brings all rays of one direction to one point of its focal plane ([[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^rem-a5-3-3|Remark: Oblique parallel rays]]), at $X = f\tan\theta_x \approx f\sin\theta_x$, and the optical paths from a plane perpendicular to the bundle to that point are all equal. Two rays of the bundle, from the origin $O$ and from $Q = (x, y)$, therefore differ in path only before they reach that plane: the ray from $Q$ starts ahead by the projection of $\vec{OQ}$ on the ray direction $\hat s$, so its path is shorter by $\vec{OQ}\cdot\hat s = x\sin\theta_x + y\sin\theta_y$. This gives the factor $e^{-ik(x\sin\theta_x + y\sin\theta_y)}$. Without a lens, [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-5|Theorem §B11.1.5]] with $d' = \infty$ gives the same linear term, $-\vec\xi\cdot\vec x_P/d$, once the quadratic term is negligible; the terms independent of $\vec\xi$ form the phase factor. For oblique incidence the incident phase across the aperture is $e^{+ik(x\sin\theta_{x0} + y\sin\theta_{y0})}$, which combines with the diffraction phase.

> [!caution] Caution: The sign in the exponent
> Fowles, Schwartz and the PHY 300 sheets write the Fraunhofer kernel with $e^{+ik(\dots)}$. That amounts to computing the field at $(-X, -Y)$: the pattern is reflected through the axis. For the intensity of an aperture that is symmetric under $(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$, or of any real transmission function ([[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography|§B11.4]]), nothing changes. The sign matters only when one asks on which side a tilted or phase-shifting aperture throws its light; the minus sign above follows from the geometry with the optics convention $e^{+ikr}$ and matches the transform of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|Def. §B4.4.1]].

## Rectangle and slit

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.2: Rectangular Aperture and Slit
> A rectangular aperture of width $a$ along $x$ and $b$ along $y$ gives
>
> $$
> U = C\,ab\,\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}, \qquad I = I_0\Bigl(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\Bigr)^2\Bigl(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\Bigr)^2, \qquad \alpha = \tfrac12ka\sin\theta_x, \quad \beta = \tfrac12kb\sin\theta_y ,
> $$
>
> with $I_0 = \lvert C\rvert^2a^2b^2$. The pattern is the product of two single-slit patterns, with dark lines at $\alpha = \pm\pi, \pm2\pi, \dots$ and $\beta = \pm\pi, \pm2\pi, \dots$; its scale in each direction is inversely proportional to the aperture's width in that direction. For a long slit ($a \gg b$) the light is confined to the line $\theta_x \approx 0$, along which $I = I_0(\sin\beta/\beta)^2$, with first zeros at $\sin\theta_y = \pm\lambda/b$: the result of [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-3|Theorem §A7.1.3]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.4, eqs. (5.17)–(5.21) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19, §4 · PHY 300, Worksheet 11 (question 3)*

^thm-b11-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The integrand of [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]] factorises, and so does the integral over a rectangle:
>
> $$
> \int_{-b/2}^{b/2}e^{-iky\sin\theta_y}\,dy = \frac{e^{-ikb\sin\theta_y/2} - e^{ikb\sin\theta_y/2}}{-ik\sin\theta_y} = \frac{2\sin\bigl(\tfrac12kb\sin\theta_y\bigr)}{k\sin\theta_y} = b\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta},
> $$
>
> and likewise in $x$. Square the modulus. At $\theta_x = \theta_y = 0$ both factors tend to $1$, so $I_0$ is the central irradiance. For $a \gg b$ the factor $(\sin\alpha/\alpha)^2$ falls to zero already at $\sin\theta_x = \lambda/a$, a negligible angle, so all the light lies on the line $\theta_x \approx 0$.

^der-b11-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Bright and narrow together
> The central amplitude is $C$ times the **area** of the opening, so the central irradiance grows as the area squared, while the angular size of the pattern shrinks in proportion to the width. The total power, the integral of $I$ over the focal plane, grows only in proportion to the area, as it must: the light through the opening is concentrated into a smaller spot. A narrow slit gives a dim, wide pattern; widening it makes the pattern brighter and narrower (Fowles), the behaviour that level A described as "wide slits, narrow peaks" ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^rem-a7-1-3|Remark: Wide slits, narrow peaks]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.4*

## The circular aperture

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.3: Circular Aperture; Airy Pattern
> A circular aperture of radius $R$ (diameter $D = 2R$) gives a circularly symmetric pattern
>
> $$
> U = C\,\pi R^2\,\frac{2J_1(\rho)}{\rho}, \qquad I = I_0\left[\frac{2J_1(\rho)}{\rho}\right]^2, \qquad \rho = kR\sin\theta = \frac{\pi D\sin\theta}{\lambda},
> $$
>
> where $\theta$ is the angle from the axis and $J_1$ is the Bessel function of the first kind of order one,
>
> $$
> J_1(\rho) = \sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^m}{m!\,(m+1)!}\Bigl(\frac\rho2\Bigr)^{2m+1} = \frac\rho2 - \frac{\rho^3}{16} + \dots, \qquad \frac{2J_1(\rho)}{\rho} \to 1 \ (\rho \to 0) .
> $$
>
> The bright central **Airy disk** extends to the first zero of $J_1$, $\rho = 3.832$, i.e.
>
> $$
> \sin\theta_1 = \frac{3.832}{\pi}\,\frac{\lambda}{D} = 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D} .
> $$
>
> The further dark rings lie at $\sin\theta = 2.23\lambda/D$, $3.24\lambda/D$, …, and the bright rings between them have $1.75\%$, $0.42\%$ and $0.16\%$ of the central irradiance. About $84\%$ of the power falls inside the first dark ring.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.4, eqs. (5.22)–(5.25) and Table 5.1 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19, §4*

^thm-b11-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By symmetry the pattern depends only on $\theta$; take the direction in the $y$–$z$ plane, so $\sin\theta_x = 0$, $\sin\theta_y = \sin\theta$. Cut the disk into strips parallel to $x$: the strip at height $y$ has length $2\sqrt{R^2 - y^2}$, and all its points have the same phase. [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]] gives, with $y = Ru$,
>
> $$
> U = C\int_{-R}^{R}e^{-iky\sin\theta}\,2\sqrt{R^2 - y^2}\,dy = 2CR^2\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-i\rho u}\sqrt{1 - u^2}\,du .
> $$
>
> Expand the exponential in its power series, which converges uniformly on $[-1, 1]$, and integrate term by term. Odd powers of $u$ integrate to zero. For even powers, the substitution $u = \sin\varphi$ and the Wallis integrals $W_{2m} = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\sin^{2m}\varphi\,d\varphi = \pi\,\frac{(2m)!}{4^m(m!)^2}$, with $W_{2m+2} = \frac{2m+1}{2m+2}W_{2m}$, give
>
> $$
> \int_{-1}^{1}u^{2m}\sqrt{1 - u^2}\,du = W_{2m} - W_{2m+2} = \frac{W_{2m}}{2m+2} = \frac{\pi\,(2m)!}{2^{2m+1}\,m!\,(m+1)!} .
> $$
>
> Hence
>
> $$
> \int_{-1}^{1}e^{-i\rho u}\sqrt{1 - u^2}\,du = \sum_{m}\frac{(-1)^m\rho^{2m}}{(2m)!}\cdot\frac{\pi\,(2m)!}{2^{2m+1}\,m!\,(m+1)!} = \frac{\pi}{\rho}\sum_m\frac{(-1)^m}{m!\,(m+1)!}\Bigl(\frac\rho2\Bigr)^{2m+1} = \frac{\pi J_1(\rho)}{\rho},
> $$
>
> and $U = 2\pi CR^2J_1(\rho)/\rho = C\pi R^2\cdot 2J_1(\rho)/\rho$. The positions of the zeros and maxima of $J_1(\rho)/\rho$ are found numerically ($3.8317$, $7.0156$, $10.1735$ for the zeros); dividing by $\pi$ gives the multiples of $\lambda/D$. The power fraction is a numerical integral of $I$ over the disk $\rho < 3.832$, weighted by $\rho\,d\rho$. The properties of Bessel functions used here as numbers are Mathematical Methods, planned.

> [!remark] Remark: The layer change for the factor 1.22
> Level A stated the diffraction limit $1.22\lambda/D$ with only a heuristic argument: a circle is a slit whose edges count less ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^der-a7-4-1|derivation of Theorem §A7.4.1]]). The strip decomposition above is that argument made exact: the edge strips have weight $\sqrt{R^2 - y^2}$, the integral produces $J_1$, and $1.22 = 3.832/\pi$ is the first zero of $J_1$ divided by $\pi$. The pattern of a circle is wider than that of a square of side $D$ because the circle has less area far from its centre line: its effective width is smaller. The image of a star in a telescope, of a laser focused by a lens, of a point under a microscope, is an Airy pattern whose scale is set by $\lambda/D$ of the limiting aperture.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.4 ("Optical resolution")*

^rem-b11-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.4: Rayleigh Limit of a Slit and a Circular Aperture
> Two mutually incoherent point sources of equal brightness, seen through a slit of width $b$ or a circular aperture of diameter $D$, are just resolved by the Rayleigh criterion ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^def-a7-4-1|Def. §A7.4.1]]) at the angular separations
>
> $$
> \Delta\theta_{\text{slit}} = \frac{\lambda}{b}, \qquad \Delta\theta_{\text{circle}} = 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D} \qquad (\text{small angles}).
> $$
>
> At this separation the combined irradiance midway between the two peaks is $8/\pi^2 = 0.81$ of the peak value for the slit and $0.735$ for the circle (the $73\%$ quoted without proof in [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^rem-a7-4-1|§A7.4, Remark: A convention, not a law]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.4 ("Optical resolution"; the slit value $8/\pi^2$ stated there, its proof left as an exercise) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19, §4*

^thm-b11-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A source at angle $\theta_0$ shifts the pattern so that it is centred on $\theta_0$ ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]], oblique incidence). Incoherent sources add in irradiance. Rayleigh's criterion puts the centre of one pattern on the first zero of the other: $\beta = \pi$ for the slit ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-2|Theorem §B11.2.2]]), $\sin\theta = \lambda/b$; $\rho = 3.832$ for the circle ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]]). At each peak the other pattern is zero, so the peak value is $I_0$. Midway, each pattern is evaluated at half the separation. For the slit that is $\beta = \pi/2$, and the sum is $2I_0\bigl(\sin\frac\pi2/\frac\pi2\bigr)^2 = 8I_0/\pi^2 = 0.811\,I_0$. For the circle it is $\rho = 1.916$, and $2\bigl[2J_1(1.916)/1.916\bigr]^2 = 0.735$, numerically.

## Arrays of apertures: double slit and grating

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.5: Array Theorem; Double Slit and Grating
> Let an aperture consist of $N$ identical openings, the $j$-th being a copy of one opening translated by the vector $(x_j, y_j)$. Its Fraunhofer field is the field $U_1$ of a single opening times an **array factor**:
>
> $$
> U(X, Y) = U_1(X, Y)\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{-ik(x_j\sin\theta_x + y_j\sin\theta_y)} .
> $$
>
> For $N$ parallel slits of width $b$ whose centres are a distance $h$ apart (level A: $D$ and $d$), with $\beta = \tfrac12kb\sin\theta$ and $\gamma = \tfrac12kh\sin\theta$,
>
> $$
> I = I_0\Bigl(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\Bigr)^2\left(\frac{\sin N\gamma}{N\sin\gamma}\right)^2, \qquad N = 2: \quad I = I_0\Bigl(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\Bigr)^2\cos^2\gamma ,
> $$
>
> normalised to $I_0$ at $\theta = 0$. The single-slit factor is the envelope. The array factor has **principal maxima** of value $1$ where $\gamma = n\pi$, i.e. at the grating equation $h\sin\theta = n\lambda$ (order $n$), zeros at $\gamma = \pm\pi/N, \pm2\pi/N, \dots$ in between (except at the principal maxima), and $N - 2$ weak secondary maxima between neighbouring orders, near $\gamma = 3\pi/2N, 5\pi/2N, \dots$
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.4, eqs. (5.26)–(5.31) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19, §2 and §4 · PHY 300, Worksheet 11 and Final practice exam (formula sheet)*

^thm-b11-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Substituting $(x, y) = (x_j + x', y_j + y')$ in the integral over the $j$-th opening ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]]) pulls out $e^{-ik(x_j\sin\theta_x + y_j\sin\theta_y)}$ and leaves the integral over the single opening; sum over $j$. For slits at $y_j = jh$, $j = 0, \dots, N - 1$, the array factor is a geometric series with ratio $q = e^{-2i\gamma}$:
>
> $$
> \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{-2ij\gamma} = \frac{1 - e^{-2iN\gamma}}{1 - e^{-2i\gamma}} = e^{-i(N-1)\gamma}\,\frac{\sin N\gamma}{\sin\gamma} .
> $$
>
> The single-slit factor is $b\sin\beta/\beta$ ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-2|Theorem §B11.2.2]]). Taking the squared modulus, and dividing by the value $N^2b^2\lvert C\rvert^2$ at $\theta = 0$, gives $I$; for $N = 2$, $\sin2\gamma/(2\sin\gamma) = \cos\gamma$. When $\gamma = n\pi$ every term of the series equals $1$ and the factor is $1$ (the limit of $\sin N\gamma/(N\sin\gamma)$); otherwise it vanishes when $N\gamma$ is a multiple of $\pi$; between two such zeros it has a maximum, of value about $1/(N\sin\gamma)^2 \ll 1$ away from the principal maxima.

^der-b11-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]], [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-2|Theorem §B11.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the array theorem adds
> Level A multiplied "interference of point sources" by "diffraction of one slit" as a plausible recipe ([[§A7.2 Double-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-2-1|Theorem §A7.2.1]]); here the product is exact, for openings of any shape in any arrangement, because translating an opening only multiplies its Fraunhofer field by a phase. Schwartz arrives at the same result from the other end: holes in a screen radiate like the antennas of an array (his Lecture 18), and a wide slit is the limit of $N$ holes with $Nd$ fixed ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^rem-a7-1-1|Remark: The same result as a limit of many slits]]). The envelope suppresses orders that fall on its zeros: missing orders ([[§A7.2 Double-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]]). In [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography|§B11.4]] the array factor becomes the transform of a row of delta functions.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19, §2 and §4*

^rem-b11-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.2.6: Width of the Orders and Resolving Power of a Grating
> For a grating of $N$ slits with spacing $h$, at normal incidence:
> - the principal maximum of order $n$, at angle $\theta$, has angular half-width (peak to first zero)
>
> $$
> \Delta\theta = \frac{\lambda}{N h\cos\theta} ;
> $$
>
> - its **angular dispersion** is $\dfrac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \dfrac{n}{h\cos\theta}$;
> - so two wavelengths $\lambda$ and $\lambda + \Delta\lambda$ are resolved by the Rayleigh criterion when
>
> $$
> \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} \le nN \qquad (\text{resolving power } \mathcal{R} = nN) .
> $$
>
> Since $n = h\sin\theta/\lambda$, also $\mathcal R = W\sin\theta/\lambda$ with $W = Nh$ the width of the ruled area: the resolving power is less than the number of wavelengths that fit across the grating, however the grating is ruled.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.4, eqs. (5.32)–(5.34) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19, §3*

^thm-b11-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-5|Theorem §B11.2.5]] the first zero next to the principal maximum $\gamma = n\pi$ lies at $N\gamma = Nn\pi \pm \pi$, i.e. $\Delta\gamma = \pi/N$. Since $\gamma = \frac12kh\sin\theta$, $\Delta\gamma = \frac12kh\cos\theta\,\Delta\theta = \frac{\pi h}{\lambda}\cos\theta\,\Delta\theta$, so $\Delta\theta = \lambda/(Nh\cos\theta)$. Differentiating $h\sin\theta = n\lambda$ gives $h\cos\theta\,d\theta = n\,d\lambda$. Rayleigh's criterion asks that the angular separation of the two orders, $n\,\Delta\lambda/(h\cos\theta)$, be at least the half-width $\lambda/(Nh\cos\theta)$, i.e. $\Delta\lambda \ge \lambda/(nN)$. Finally $nN = (h\sin\theta/\lambda)N = W\sin\theta/\lambda < W/\lambda$.

^der-b11-2-6

*Uses:* [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-5|Theorem §B11.2.5]], [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^def-a7-4-1|Def. §A7.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Comparison with level A, and real gratings
> [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-3|Theorem §A7.4.3]] obtained $mN$ by locating the zero beside a maximum. Separating width and dispersion shows what each contributes: more lines make the orders narrower, higher order or finer ruling spreads the wavelengths further, and only the product, which is the ruled width in wavelengths, counts. A typical grating with $600$ lines/mm over $10\ \mathrm{cm}$ has $N = 60\,000$, and good gratings reach about $90\%$ of the theoretical $nN$. Grooves with a sawtooth (blazed) profile send most of the light into one order; most spectroscopic gratings work in reflection, and concave ones also focus.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.4*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B11.2.1: The Diagonal of a Rectangular Pattern
> How bright are the maxima of a square aperture's pattern off the axes? Along the diagonal $\alpha = \beta$, [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-2|Theorem §B11.2.2]] gives $I/I_0 = (\sin\alpha/\alpha)^4$, whose maxima lie where $\tan\alpha = \alpha$, as for the slit. The first is at $\alpha = 1.430\pi$, with
>
> $$
> \frac{I}{I_0} = (0.0472)^2 = 2.2 \times 10^{-3},
> $$
>
> twenty times weaker than the first maxima on the axes ($0.0472$). The light of a rectangular aperture is concentrated along two perpendicular lines through the centre, parallel to the edges: a cross. This is the origin of the spikes on star images taken through apertures with straight edges (here the $1/\alpha^2$ decay along each axis wins over the $1/(\alpha\beta)^2$ decay elsewhere).
>
> *Source: Fowles, Problem 5.6*

^ex-b11-2-1

> [!example] Example §B11.2.2: Resolving the Sodium D Lines
> The sodium D lines are at $589.0\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $589.6\ \mathrm{nm}$. In first order, [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-6|Theorem §B11.2.6]] requires
>
> $$
> N \ge \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = \frac{589.3}{0.6} = 982\ \text{lines}.
> $$
>
> Spread $982$ lines over a width $W = 2.0\ \mathrm{cm}$, so $h = 20.4\ \mu\mathrm m$, and focus with $f = 20\ \mathrm{cm}$. Then $\sin\theta = \lambda/h = 0.0289$, the dispersion is $d\theta/d\lambda = 1/(h\cos\theta) = 4.91 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{rad/m}$, and the two lines are $\Delta\theta = (4.91 \times 10^4)(0.6 \times 10^{-9}) = 2.9 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad}$ apart, i.e. $f\Delta\theta = 5.9\ \mu\mathrm m$ apart in the focal plane. That is exactly the half-width $f\lambda/(W\cos\theta)$ of each line's image: at the Rayleigh limit the separation equals the diffraction width, whatever lens is used. To see the doublet comfortably one needs a few times more lines, not a longer lens.
>
> *Source: Fowles, Problem 5.10*

^ex-b11-2-2

> [!example] Example §B11.2.3: Can a Grating Resolve the Modes of a Laser?
> A helium–neon laser ($\lambda = 633\ \mathrm{nm}$, $\nu = c/\lambda = 4.74 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$) emits several longitudinal modes $450\ \mathrm{MHz}$ apart. Since $\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert/\lambda = \Delta\nu/\nu$, resolving them needs
>
> $$
> nN \ge \frac{\nu}{\Delta\nu} = \frac{4.74 \times 10^{14}}{4.5 \times 10^8} = 1.05 \times 10^6 .
> $$
>
> A grating with $1000$ lines/mm has $h = 1.00\ \mu\mathrm m < 2\lambda$, so only the first order exists ($\sin\theta = 0.633$), and it needs $N = 1.05 \times 10^6$ lines: a ruled width of $1.05\ \mathrm m$. By the bound $\mathcal R < W/\lambda$ no ruling does much better, since $W > 0.66\ \mathrm m$ in any case. Mode structure is measured instead with a Fabry–Perot interferometer, whose resolving power comes from many reflections rather than many lines ([[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-5|Theorem §B10.4.5]], [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^rem-b10-4-1|Remark: Fabry–Perot against grating]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles, Problem 5.13*
