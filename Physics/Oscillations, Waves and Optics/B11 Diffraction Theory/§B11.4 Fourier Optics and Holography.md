The Fraunhofer integral of [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]] is a Fourier transform. Once the aperture is allowed to transmit partially, or to shift the phase, the far-field pattern becomes the two-dimensional transform ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|Def. §B4.4.1]]) of its transmission, and every rule for transforms becomes a statement about diffraction. Imaging is then a transform followed by a second transform, and anything placed in between, a stop, a filter, a phase plate, acts on the object's spatial frequencies as an electrical filter acts on a signal's frequencies ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-3|Def. §B4.4.3]]). This gives the resolution limit of coherent imaging, spatial filtering and phase contrast. Holography, described qualitatively at level A ([[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^def-a7-5-2|Def. §A7.5.2]], [[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^rem-a7-5-3|Remark: Recording and reconstruction]]), is the same mathematics run backwards: record an interference pattern, and its diffraction reconstructs the wave.

## The diffraction pattern as a Fourier transform

> [!definition] Definition §B11.4.1: Aperture Function; Spatial Frequencies
> The **aperture function** (transmission function) $g(x, y)$ of a thin object in the plane $z = 0$ is the ratio of the transmitted to the incident complex amplitude at $(x, y)$: $g = 0$ where the object is opaque, $\lvert g\rvert < 1$ where it absorbs partially, and $g = e^{i\varphi(x, y)}$ for a transparent object that retards the wave by the phase $\varphi$. In the focal plane of a lens of focal length $f$ the point $(X, Y)$ corresponds to the **spatial frequencies**
>
> $$
> \mu = \frac{kX}{f} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\sin\theta_x, \qquad \nu = \frac{kY}{f} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\sin\theta_y ,
> $$
>
> wave numbers (radians per unit length) across the object plane.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.6, eqs. (5.52)–(5.53) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19, §5*

^def-b11-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.1: Fraunhofer Pattern as a Fourier Transform
> An object with aperture function $g$, lit normally by a plane wave, produces in the focal plane the field
>
> $$
> U(\mu, \nu) = C\iint g(x, y)\,e^{-i(\mu x + \nu y)}\,dx\,dy = (2\pi)^2\,C\,\tilde g(\mu, \nu),
> $$
>
> the two-dimensional Fourier transform of $g$ in the convention of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|Def. §B4.4.1]]. The diffraction pattern is a Fourier analysis of the object: the light at $(\mu, \nu)$ measures how much of the object varies across it as $e^{i(\mu x + \nu y)}$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.6, eq. (5.54) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19, §5, eqs. (28)–(29) · PHY 300, Worksheet 11 (question 3)*

^thm-b11-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the derivation of [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]] the element $dx\,dy$ at $(x, y)$ now sends out a secondary wave of amplitude proportional to $g(x, y)\,dx\,dy$ instead of $dx\,dy$; the phase factor $e^{-ik(x\sin\theta_x + y\sin\theta_y)} = e^{-i(\mu x + \nu y)}$ is unchanged. Integrating over the plane ($g = 0$ outside the object) gives the first form; comparison with the two-dimensional transform of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|Def. §B4.4.1]], which carries the factor $1/(2\pi)^2$, gives the second.

^der-b11-4-1

*Uses:* [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|Def. §B4.4.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Which wave number?
> The transform variable is $\mu = k\sin\theta_x$, not the wave number $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ of the light. Schwartz stresses the point: the farther from the centre one looks, the shorter the period of the object structure being analysed, whatever the wavelength. Since $\lvert\sin\theta_x\rvert \le 1$, only spatial frequencies up to $k$ leave the object as travelling waves, and the small-angle formula is accurate only for $\mu, \nu \ll k$: structure finer than about a wavelength does not reach the far field ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^rem-a7-1-3|Remark: Wide slits, narrow peaks]] for the slit). In Schwartz's Lecture 19 the slit check, his eq. (32), should read $E(y) \propto \tilde T(2\pi y/L\lambda)$; with $\tilde T(k) = a\,\mathrm{sinc}(ka/2)$ this gives the $\mathrm{sinc}(\pi ay/\lambda L)$ of his eq. (30).

^cau-b11-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.2: Rules for Diffraction Patterns
> For the Fraunhofer field $U$ of an object $g$ ([[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-1|Theorem §B11.4.1]]):
> 1. **Translation.** Moving the object by $(x_0, y_0)$ multiplies $U$ by $e^{-i(\mu x_0 + \nu y_0)}$: the irradiance pattern does not move.
> 2. **Scaling.** Enlarging the object by a factor $s$, $g(x/s, y/s)$, turns $U(\mu, \nu)$ into $s^2\,U(s\mu, s\nu)$: the pattern shrinks by $s$ and its centre brightens by $s^4$.
> 3. **Inversion symmetry.** If $g$ is real (no phase structure), $U(-\mu, -\nu) = U(\mu, \nu)^*$, so the irradiance is symmetric under $(X, Y) \to (-X, -Y)$, even when the object is not: a triangular hole gives a pattern with sixfold symmetry.
> 4. **Convolution.** If $g$ is the convolution $g_1 * g_2$, $(g_1 * g_2)(x) = \int g_1(x - x')\,g_2(x')\,dx'$ (in each variable), then $U \propto U_1U_2$. The array theorem ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-5|Theorem §B11.2.5]]) is the case where $g_2$ is a set of delta functions marking the positions of identical openings.
> 5. **Energy.** $\iint\lvert U\rvert^2\,d\mu\,d\nu = (2\pi)^2\lvert C\rvert^2\iint\lvert g\rvert^2\,dx\,dy$: the power in the pattern is the power transmitted by the object.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.6 (the grating as a Fourier series; transforms "rectangular aperture … product of two one-dimensional integrals") · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19, §5 (the grating as a sum of delta functions); the list is assembled here from the rules of §B4.4*

^thm-b11-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> 1–3 are the shift, scaling and reality rules of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]], applied in each variable; for the scaling, $\iint g(x/s, y/s)e^{-i(\mu x + \nu y)}dx\,dy = s^2\iint g(x', y')e^{-i(s\mu x' + s\nu y')}dx'\,dy'$, and the central value grows by $s^2$, its square by $s^4$. For 4, in one variable,
>
> $$
> \int(g_1 * g_2)(x)\,e^{-i\mu x}dx = \iint g_1(x - x')g_2(x')\,e^{-i\mu(x - x')}e^{-i\mu x'}\,dx'\,dx = \int g_1(t)e^{-i\mu t}dt\int g_2(x')e^{-i\mu x'}dx',
> $$
>
> substituting $t = x - x'$ in the inner integral (the order of integration may be exchanged for absolutely integrable $g_1$, $g_2$). With $g_2 = \sum_j\delta(x - x_j)$, $g_1 * g_2 = \sum_jg_1(x - x_j)$ and $\int g_2e^{-i\mu x}dx = \sum_je^{-i\mu x_j}$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|Theorem §B4.4.3]]), the array factor. 5 is the Parseval–Plancherel identity ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|Theorem §B4.4.4]]) in two variables.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.3: Diffraction by a Periodic Object
> Let the object be periodic in $y$ with period $h$ and expand it in a complex Fourier series ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^def-b4-1-2|Def. §B4.1.2]]), $g(y) = \sum_mc_m\,e^{2\pi imy/h}$. Over an illuminated width $W \gg h$, the pattern consists of sharp **orders** at
>
> $$
> \nu = \frac{2\pi m}{h} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h\sin\theta = m\lambda ,
> $$
>
> the grating equation, with amplitudes proportional to $c_m$ and widths set by $W$ ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-6|Theorem §B11.2.6]]). Each diffraction order is one Fourier component of the object. In particular:
> - a **sinusoidal amplitude grating** $g = g_0 + g_1\cos(2\pi y/h)$ produces only the orders $0$ and $\pm1$, with amplitudes $g_0$ and $g_1/2$;
> - a grating of slits of width $b$ has $c_m = \frac{b}{h}\,\frac{\sin(\pi mb/h)}{\pi mb/h}$: the single-slit envelope sampled at the orders, so that orders at its zeros are missing (for $b = h/2$, all even orders except $0$).
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.6, eqs. (5.55)–(5.56) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19, §5*

^thm-b11-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By linearity ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]]) the field is the sum of the fields of the components $c_me^{2\pi imy/h}$. Over a width $W$, the integral of $e^{2\pi imy/h}e^{-i\nu y}$ is $W\,\frac{\sin[(\nu - 2\pi m/h)W/2]}{(\nu - 2\pi m/h)W/2}$, a peak at $\nu = 2\pi m/h$ of height $W$ and width of order $2\pi/W$; as $W \to \infty$ it becomes $2\pi\delta(\nu - 2\pi m/h)$. With $\nu = k\sin\theta$, $\nu = 2\pi m/h$ is $h\sin\theta = m\lambda$. For the cosine, $g_1\cos(2\pi y/h) = \frac{g_1}{2}(e^{2\pi iy/h} + e^{-2\pi iy/h})$: only $c_0$ and $c_{\pm1}$ are nonzero. For slits of width $b$ centred at $y = 0, \pm h, \dots$: $c_m = \frac1h\int_{-b/2}^{b/2}e^{-2\pi imy/h}\,dy = \frac{b}{h}\,\frac{\sin(\pi mb/h)}{\pi mb/h}$, which vanishes for $m \neq 0$ when $mb/h$ is a nonzero integer.

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^def-b4-1-2|Def. §B4.1.2]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]], [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-1|Theorem §B11.4.1]]

> [!example] Example §B11.4.1: Apodizing a Slit
> Coat a slit of width $b$ so that its amplitude transmission falls from the centre to the edges as $g(y) = \cos(\pi y/b)$ for $\lvert y\rvert < b/2$. Its pattern is
>
> $$
> U(\nu) = \int_{-b/2}^{b/2}\cos\frac{\pi y}{b}\,e^{-i\nu y}\,dy = \frac{2\pi b\,\cos(\nu b/2)}{\pi^2 - \nu^2b^2}
> $$
>
> (write the cosine as two exponentials and integrate each). Compared with the uniform slit, $U = b\sin(\nu b/2)/(\nu b/2)$:
> - the first zero moves from $\nu b = 2\pi$ to $\nu b = 3\pi$: the central peak is $1.5$ times wider;
> - the first side lobe drops from $4.7\%$ to $0.50\%$ of the central irradiance (from $-13$ to $-23\ \mathrm{dB}$), and the far lobes fall as $1/\nu^4$ instead of $1/\nu^2$, because $g$ is now continuous at the edges ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]] for the analogous decay of Fourier coefficients).
>
> This trade, a slightly wider core for much weaker wings, is **apodization**. It helps a telescope find a faint star next to a bright one, whose diffraction rings would otherwise swamp it. A raised-cosine profile $\frac12 + \frac12\cos(2\pi y/b)$ goes further, to a first side lobe of $0.07\%$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.6, eqs. (5.57)–(5.58), and Problem 5.24; side-lobe values computed here*

## Imaging as two transforms

> [!model] Model §B11.4.4: Coherent Optical Processor
> A coherently lit object $g(x, y)$ stands in the front focal plane of a lens of focal length $f$; a second identical lens stands a distance $2f$ behind the first, and the image is observed in its back focal plane (the "$4f$ arrangement"). Assumptions:
> - each lens carries the field in its front focal plane to its Fourier transform in its back focal plane, phases included ([[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-1|Theorem §B11.4.1]]; the focal-plane positions make the extra phase factor of [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]] constant);
> - paraxial light, lenses free of aberrations and large enough to pass everything of interest;
> - everything placed in the common focal plane, the **Fourier plane**, acts by multiplying the field there by a complex **transfer function** $T(\mu, \nu)$. Stops, filters and lens aberrations are all described by $T$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.6 ("Spatial filtering", eq. (5.59) and Figs. 5.30, 5.33); the $4f$ placement is added here to make the second transform exact*

^mod-b11-4-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.5: Coherent Image and Transfer Function
> In [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^mod-b11-4-4|Model §B11.4.4]] the field in the image plane is
>
> $$
> g'(x', y') \;\propto\; \iint T(\mu, \nu)\,U(\mu, \nu)\,e^{-i(\mu x' + \nu y')}\,d\mu\,d\nu = (2\pi)^2\,C\,(g * t)(-x', -y'),
> $$
>
> where $U$ is the transform of $g$ and $t$ is the function whose transform is $T$, $t(x, y) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^2}\iint T\,e^{i(\mu x + \nu y)}\,d\mu\,d\nu$. With $T = 1$ the image is $g(-x', -y')$: an exact, inverted copy of the object. In general the image is the object smeared by the **amplitude point-spread function** $t$, the image of a single point; each spatial frequency of the object reaches the image multiplied by $T(\mu, \nu)$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.6, eq. (5.59); the convolution form is derived here*

^thm-b11-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The first lens produces $U(\mu, \nu)$ in the Fourier plane ([[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-1|Theorem §B11.4.1]]); the plane multiplies it by $T$; the second lens transforms again, with $\mu, \nu$ now playing the role of the coordinates, $x', y'$ that of the frequencies, which gives the integral. With $T = 1$, $\iint U(\mu, \nu)e^{-i(\mu x' + \nu y')}d\mu\,d\nu = (2\pi)^2C\iint\tilde g(\mu, \nu)e^{i(\mu(-x') + \nu(-y'))}d\mu\,d\nu = (2\pi)^2C\,g(-x', -y')$ by Fourier inversion ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|Theorem §B4.4.1]]): two forward transforms return the function reflected through the origin. In general $TU = (2\pi)^2C\,T\tilde g$ is $(2\pi)^2C$ times the transform of $g * t$ (convolution rule, [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-2|Theorem §B11.4.2]], with $\tilde t = T/(2\pi)^2$ and the $(2\pi)^2$ of the two-dimensional convention), so the same inversion gives $(g * t)(-x', -y')$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.6: Resolution Limit of Coherent Imaging
> Let the Fourier plane of [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^mod-b11-4-4|Model §B11.4.4]] contain a slit stop that passes $\lvert Y\rvert < a$, i.e. the spatial frequencies $\lvert\nu\rvert < \nu_{\max} = ka/f$ (a **low-pass filter**). A grating object of period $h$ appears in the image as a periodic pattern only if
>
> $$
> h > h_{\min} = \frac{2\pi}{\nu_{\max}} = \frac{\lambda f}{a} = \frac{\lambda}{\sin\alpha},
> $$
>
> where $\alpha$ is the half-angle under which the stop is seen from the object, $\sin\alpha \approx a/f$. Finer gratings give a uniformly lit image. Detail of size $h$ is imaged only if at least the first diffraction orders it produces pass through the system.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.6 ("the finite size of the aperture at the $\mu\nu$ plane limits the spatial frequencies"; $\nu_{\max} = kb/f$ for a stop of width $2b$)*

^thm-b11-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-3|Theorem §B11.4.3]] the grating's light is concentrated in orders at $\nu = 2\pi m/h$, the $m$-th carrying the component $c_me^{2\pi imy/h}$. The stop sets $T = 1$ for $\lvert\nu\rvert < \nu_{\max}$ and $0$ otherwise. If $2\pi/h \ge \nu_{\max}$, only the zero order passes, and by [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-5|Theorem §B11.4.5]] the image field is the constant $c_0$: no trace of the period. If $2\pi/h < \nu_{\max}$, at least the orders $0$ and $\pm1$ pass, and the image contains $c_0 + 2\lvert c_1\rvert\cos(2\pi y'/h + \dots)$, a pattern of period $h$. Finally $\nu_{\max} = (2\pi/\lambda)(a/f)$.

^der-b11-4-6

*Uses:* [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-3|Theorem §B11.4.3]], [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-5|Theorem §B11.4.5]], [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^def-b11-4-1|Def. §B11.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Comparison with the Rayleigh limit
> This is Abbe's view of the microscope: the objective must catch the diffracted orders, and with a cone of half-angle $\alpha$ in a medium of index $n$ the limit becomes $h_{\min} = \lambda_0/(n\sin\alpha) = \lambda_0/\mathrm{NA}$. The Rayleigh estimate of level A, $0.61\lambda_0/\mathrm{NA}$ for two points ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-2|Theorem §A7.4.2]]), concerns self-luminous, incoherent points; the factor differs because the criteria and the illumination differ, not the physics. Both say that no lens resolves detail much finer than the wavelength divided by the numerical aperture.

^rem-b11-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Spatial filtering
> Masks in the Fourier plane edit the image ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^rem-b4-4-2|Remark: Filtering images]] does the same numerically).
> - A **low-pass** filter (an opening around the axis) removes fine detail: the sharp steps of a grating come out rounded, with ripples (figure).
> - A **high-pass** filter (an opaque spot on the axis) removes the slowly varying part: only the edges, which carry the high spatial frequencies, remain visible.
> - The **pinhole spatial filter** of laser work focuses a beam with a short lens onto a pinhole that passes only the low spatial frequencies; the fringes and speckle from dust and imperfect optics, which sit at higher frequencies, are blocked, and a second lens recollimates a clean beam.
>
> The analogy with electrical filters is exact: object and image are input and output signals, $T$ is the transfer function.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.6 ("Spatial filtering", Fig. 5.31)*

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.7: Phase Contrast
> A transparent object with a weak phase structure, $g(y) = e^{i\varphi(y)}$ with $\lvert\varphi\rvert \ll 1$ and $\varphi$ of zero average (a constant phase is irrelevant), gives in the processor of [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^mod-b11-4-4|Model §B11.4.4]] with $T = 1$ an image of uniform irradiance, $\lvert g'\rvert^2 = 1 + O(\varphi^2)$: it is invisible. If a **phase plate** that adds a quarter wave of optical path, multiplying the field by $e^{i\pi/2} = i$, is put on the axis of the Fourier plane, where the undiffracted light is focused, the image irradiance becomes
>
> $$
> \lvert g'\rvert^2 = 1 + 2\varphi + O(\varphi^2) :
> $$
>
> the phase variations appear as proportional variations of brightness. (With a plate that removes a quarter wave instead, $1 - 2\varphi$.)
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.6, eqs. (5.61)–(5.64)*

^thm-b11-4-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Expand $g = 1 + i\varphi + O(\varphi^2)$. By [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-1|Theorem §B11.4.1]] the transform of the constant $1$ is concentrated at $\nu = 0$ (for a wide object, a narrow peak; Fowles's $U_a$), while the transform of $i\varphi$ has no component exactly at $\nu = 0$, since $\varphi$ has zero average, and is spread over the frequencies of the structure ($iU_b$). With $T = 1$ the image is $1 + i\varphi$, of modulus squared $1 + \varphi^2$. The phase plate multiplies only the central peak by $e^{ik\lambda/4} = i$ (extra path $\lambda/4$; with the optics convention $e^{+ikr}$ an extra path $\delta$ multiplies the field by $e^{ik\delta}$, [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^cau-b11-1-1|Caution: The optics convention]]). The image becomes $i + i\varphi = i(1 + \varphi)$, so $\lvert g'\rvert^2 = (1 + \varphi)^2 = 1 + 2\varphi + \varphi^2$. A plate removing $\lambda/4$ gives $-i + i\varphi = -i(1 - \varphi)$ and $1 - 2\varphi$. The physical result does not depend on the complex convention: in the other convention both the object's phase and the plate's factor change sign.

^der-b11-4-7

*Uses:* [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^mod-b11-4-4|Model §B11.4.4]], [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-1|Theorem §B11.4.1]], [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-5|Theorem §B11.4.5]]

> [!remark] Remark: What Zernike's trick does
> In $1 + i\varphi$ the diffracted light is a quarter period out of step with the direct light, and quadrature components add in power, not in amplitude: $\lvert1 + i\varphi\rvert^2$ changes only at second order. Shifting one of the two by a quarter period puts them in step, and the interference becomes first order in $\varphi$. Fowles's analogy is the conversion of phase modulation into amplitude modulation in radio by a $90^\circ$ shift of the carrier. Zernike's phase-contrast microscope (Nobel Prize 1953) shows living cells, nearly transparent and differing from their surroundings only in refractive index, without staining.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.6*

## Holography

> [!model] Model §B11.4.8: Linear Hologram Recording
> A photographic plate in the plane $(x, y)$ is exposed to two mutually coherent waves of one wavelength: the object wave $U(x, y) = a(x, y)\,e^{i\phi(x, y)}$ scattered by the subject, and a plane **reference wave** $U_R = a_0\,e^{i(\mu x + \nu y)}$ with $\mu = k\sin\alpha$, $\nu = k\sin\beta$ fixed by its direction. The developed plate is a **hologram** whose amplitude transmission, over the range of exposures used, is a linear function of the recorded irradiance, $t = t_0 + \kappa I$ (with $\kappa > 0$ for a positive, $\kappa < 0$ for a negative transparency). Object, reference and plate must stay still to a fraction of a wavelength during the exposure, and the path differences must stay within the coherence length of the light ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^def-b10-2-2|Def. §B10.2.2]]); in practice the light comes from one laser, split in two ([[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^rem-a7-5-3|Remark: Recording and reconstruction]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.7, eqs. (5.65)–(5.67)*

^mod-b11-4-8

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.4.9: Recording and Reconstruction of a Wave Front
> In [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^mod-b11-4-8|Model §B11.4.8]] the plate records
>
> $$
> I = \lvert U + U_R\rvert^2 = a^2 + a_0^2 + 2aa_0\cos\bigl[\phi(x, y) - \mu x - \nu y\bigr],
> $$
>
> a fringe pattern whose local spacing and position encode the object wave's direction and phase, and whose contrast encodes its amplitude. Lit again by the reference wave, the hologram transmits (up to the constant $t_0U_R$ and the factor $\kappa$)
>
> $$
> U_RI = (a^2 + a_0^2)\,U_R \;+\; a_0^2\,U \;+\; a_0^2\,e^{2i(\mu x + \nu y)}\,U^* .
> $$
>
> The first term is the attenuated reference beam, going straight on. The second is the **object wave itself**, times a constant: an observer looking through the hologram sees a virtual image of the subject in its original place, in three dimensions and with parallax. The third is the complex conjugate of the object wave, deflected to the other side of the direct beam; it converges to a **real image**.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.7, eqs. (5.68)–(5.69)*

^thm-b11-4-9

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\lvert U + U_R\rvert^2 = \lvert U\rvert^2 + \lvert U_R\rvert^2 + UU_R^* + U^*U_R$, with $UU_R^* = aa_0e^{i(\phi - \mu x - \nu y)}$ and $U^*U_R$ its conjugate; their sum is $2aa_0\cos(\phi - \mu x - \nu y)$. Multiplying by $U_R$: $\lvert U_R\rvert^2U_R = a_0^2U_R$, $\lvert U\rvert^2U_R = a^2U_R$, $UU_R^*U_R = a_0^2U$, $U^*U_R^2 = a_0^2e^{2i(\mu x + \nu y)}U^*$. Beyond the plate each term propagates as the wave that has these values on the plane. The term $a_0^2U$ has exactly the values the object wave had there, so it continues as that wave (the field beyond a plane is fixed by its values on the plane, [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]]). In $U^*$ every phase is reversed: a wave that diverged from a point of the subject becomes one that converges to a point, and the extra factor $e^{2i(\mu x + \nu y)}$ tilts it by about twice the reference angle.

^der-b11-4-9

*Uses:* [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^mod-b11-4-8|Model §B11.4.8]], [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the result
> - **A hologram is a grating.** For a subject that is a single point at infinity, $U$ is a plane wave and $I$ a sinusoidal grating; by [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-3|Theorem §B11.4.3]] it has only the orders $0$ and $\pm1$, which are the three beams above. A general subject gives a superposition of such gratings with varying spacing and direction, Fowles's "carrier modulated in amplitude and phase".
> - **Why off-axis.** In Gabor's original arrangement the reference travelled along the object wave ($\mu = \nu = 0$), and the three beams overlapped. Tilting the reference by more than the angular spread of the object wave separates them; this needs the coherence of a laser.
> - **Negative or positive.** A negative transparency ($\kappa < 0$) changes the sign of the reconstructed wave, a $180^\circ$ phase shift the eye cannot detect: the image looks the same.
> - **Holographic interferometry.** Two exposures of a surface before and after a small deformation reconstruct two waves that interfere, covering the image with fringes that map the displacement in units of $\lambda/2$; double-pulse lasers do this for vibrating objects, a standard method of non-destructive testing. Colour holograms use three laser wavelengths.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.7*

^rem-b11-4-4

> [!example] Example §B11.4.2: The Hologram of a Line
> The subject is a narrow bright strip parallel to the $x$ axis, a distance $d = 10\ \mathrm{cm}$ in front of the plate opposite $y = 0$; the reference wave falls normally ($\mu = \nu = 0$), $\lambda = 632.8\ \mathrm{nm}$. The object wave on the plate is a cylindrical wave of phase $\phi = k\sqrt{d^2 + y^2}$, so the plate records fringes $\cos\phi$ whose local spatial frequency is $d\phi/dy = ky/\sqrt{d^2 + y^2} = k\sin\theta(y)$, with $\theta(y)$ the angle at which light from the strip reaches the plate. The local spacing is
>
> $$
> \Lambda(y) = \frac{\lambda}{\sin\theta(y)} = \lambda\,\frac{\sqrt{d^2 + y^2}}{y} :
> $$
>
> | $y$ | $0$ | $1\ \mathrm{cm}$ | $5\ \mathrm{cm}$ | $10\ \mathrm{cm}$ |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $\sin\theta$ | $0$ | $0.0995$ | $0.447$ | $0.707$ |
> | $\Lambda$ | $\infty$ | $6.36\ \mu\mathrm m$ | $1.41\ \mu\mathrm m$ | $0.89\ \mu\mathrm m$ |
>
> A one-dimensional grating whose spacing shrinks away from the centre: the line analogue of a zone plate ([[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates#^thm-b11-3-4|Theorem §B11.3.4]]). Lit normally, each strip of the hologram diffracts its first orders at $\sin\theta' = \pm\lambda/\Lambda(y) = \pm\sin\theta(y)$ ([[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography#^thm-b11-4-3|Theorem §B11.4.3]]): at $5.7^\circ$, $26.6^\circ$ and $45^\circ$ for the three values of $y$. One set of rays continues the rays that came from the strip and appears to diverge from a line at distance $d$ in front of the plate, the virtual image; the other set is its mirror image and converges to a line at distance $d$ behind the plate, the real image. Since the recorded fringes are sinusoidal (linear recording), there are no second or higher orders.
>
> *Source: Fowles, Problems 5.22–5.23 (the geometry of the strip is interpreted here)*
