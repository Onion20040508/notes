How much light reaches a point behind an aperture, and with what phase? Level A answered with Huygens's principle, a construction for wave fronts that is assumed ([[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^pr-a4-4-1|Principle §A4.4.1]]), extended by hand to wavelets that carry phases and interfere ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^mod-a7-1-1|Model §A7.1.1]]). This section derives that picture from the wave equation. Green's theorem turns any solution of the Helmholtz equation into an integral over a closed surface (Kirchhoff's integral theorem, exact); two assumptions about the field in the aperture (Kirchhoff's boundary conditions) then give the Fresnel–Kirchhoff formula, which is Huygens's principle with the amplitude, phase and direction of each wavelet fixed. The layer changes: the principle becomes a theorem inside a stated model. The same formula gives Babinet's principle and the criterion that separates the two regimes of diffraction ([[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^def-a4-4-2|Def. §A4.4.2]]) treated in the rest of the chapter, Fraunhofer ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns|§B11.2]]) and Fresnel ([[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates|§B11.3]]).

## The scalar wave and Kirchhoff's theorem

> [!definition] Definition §B11.1.1: Optical Disturbance; Helmholtz Equation
> In scalar diffraction theory a monochromatic light wave of angular frequency $\omega$ in a homogeneous medium is described by one complex function, the **optical disturbance** $U(\vec r)$: the wave is $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[U(\vec r)\,e^{-i\omega t}\bigr]$, and the **irradiance** is taken proportional to $\lvert U\rvert^2$. Substituting $U(\vec r)e^{-i\omega t}$ into the wave equation $\nabla^2u = \frac{1}{v^2}\,\partial_t^2u$ ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-b7-1-1|Def. §B7.1.1]]) gives the **Helmholtz equation**
>
> $$
> \nabla^2U + k^2U = 0, \qquad k = \frac{\omega}{v} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} .
> $$
>
> Its outgoing spherical-wave solution, the field of a point source at the origin, is $U = U_0\,e^{ikr}/r$ for $r > 0$ ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^thm-b7-1-5|Theorem §B7.1.5]], with the profile $F(s) = U_0e^{iks}$).
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.2*

^def-b11-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: The optics convention
> From Chapter B8 on, complex fields carry the time factor $e^{-i\omega t}$ and the spatial factor $e^{+ikr}$, as in Fowles, Schwartz's optics lectures and quantum mechanics. Chapters B1–B7 used $e^{+i\omega t}$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^cau-b1-1-1|§B1.1, Caution: Where the real-part rule fails, and sign conventions]]); the full statement of the switch and its consequences is in [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-1|§B8.1, Caution: The optics sign convention, opposite to Chapters B1–B7]]. In this chapter it matters for the phase factor $-i$ of the Fresnel–Kirchhoff formula below (with $e^{+i\omega t}$ it would be $+i$), and for which way phase plates and tilted reference waves shift things in [[§B11.4 Fourier Optics and Holography|§B11.4]]. Irradiances, being $\lvert U\rvert^2$, do not depend on the convention.

^cau-b11-1-1

> [!model] Model §B11.1.1: Scalar Diffraction Theory
> Light passes from a monochromatic point source $S$ through an aperture $\mathcal A$ in a plane opaque screen to a receiving point $P$ (figure). The model assumes:
> 1. **Scalar approximation.** The light is described by one optical disturbance $U$ obeying the Helmholtz equation ([[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^def-b11-1-1|Def. §B11.1.1]]); the vector character of the electromagnetic field ([[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|EM Principle §A12.1.3]]) and the coupling of its components at the edges are ignored.
> 2. **Kirchhoff's boundary conditions.** In the opening, $U$ and $\partial U/\partial n$ have the values of the incident wave $U_{\text{inc}} = U_0e^{ikr'}/r'$, as if the screen were absent; on the dark side of the screen, and on a far-away surface closing the region around $P$, they contribute nothing.
> 3. **Many wavelengths away.** The distances $r'$ from $S$ and $r$ to $P$ are large compared with $\lambda$: $kr, kr' \gg 1$.
>
> It works well when the aperture is large compared with the wavelength and the angles of diffraction are moderate; within about a wavelength of the edges the true field differs from the assumed one, which matters only for apertures not much larger than $\lambda$. Each Cartesian component of $\vec E$ does satisfy the wave equation in a homogeneous medium ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|EM Theorem §A12.2.4]]), which is why the scalar theory is a good first approximation.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.2 (assumptions (1) and (2), and the scalar approximation)*

^mod-b11-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.2: Kirchhoff Integral Theorem
> Let $U$ satisfy the Helmholtz equation, with continuous second derivatives, in a region $V$ bounded by a closed surface $\Sigma$, and let $P$ be a point inside $V$. Then, with $r$ the distance from $P$ and $\partial/\partial n$ the derivative along the outward normal of $\Sigma$,
>
> $$
> U(P) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint_\Sigma\left[\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\,\frac{\partial U}{\partial n} - U\,\frac{\partial}{\partial n}\Bigl(\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\Bigr)\right]dA .
> $$
>
> The value of a wave at any interior point is fixed by the values of the wave and of its normal derivative on the boundary. No approximation is involved.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.2, eqs. (5.1)–(5.6)*

^thm-b11-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $G = e^{ikr}/r$, the outgoing spherical wave centred on $P$; for $r > 0$ it satisfies $\nabla^2G + k^2G = 0$. $G$ is infinite at $P$, so cut out a small ball of radius $\varepsilon$ around $P$ and apply Green's second identity ([[§17 The Divergence Theorem in Higher Dimensions and Green's Identities#^thm-17-3|452 Thm. §17.3]]) to $U$ and $G$ in the remaining region $V_\varepsilon$:
>
> $$
> \int_{V_\varepsilon}\bigl(U\nabla^2G - G\nabla^2U\bigr)\,dV = \oint_{\partial V_\varepsilon}\Bigl(U\frac{\partial G}{\partial n} - G\frac{\partial U}{\partial n}\Bigr)dA .
> $$
>
> The left side is $\int\bigl(U(-k^2G) - G(-k^2U)\bigr)dV = 0$. The boundary of $V_\varepsilon$ is $\Sigma$ together with the small sphere, on which the outward normal of $V_\varepsilon$ points toward $P$, so $\partial/\partial n = -\partial/\partial r$, and $dA = \varepsilon^2d\Omega$. There $\partial_rG = (ik - 1/\varepsilon)\,e^{ik\varepsilon}/\varepsilon$, and the small-sphere integral is
>
> $$
> \oint\Bigl[-U\Bigl(ik - \frac1\varepsilon\Bigr)\frac{e^{ik\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon} + \frac{e^{ik\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\,\frac{\partial U}{\partial r}\Bigr]\varepsilon^2\,d\Omega = \oint e^{ik\varepsilon}\Bigl[U(1 - ik\varepsilon) + \varepsilon\,\frac{\partial U}{\partial r}\Bigr]d\Omega \;\longrightarrow\; 4\pi\,U(P)
> $$
>
> as $\varepsilon \to 0$, because $U$ is continuous and $\partial U/\partial r$ bounded near $P$. Hence $0 = \oint_\Sigma(U\partial_nG - G\partial_nU)\,dA + 4\pi U(P)$, which is the statement.

## The Fresnel–Kirchhoff formula

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.3: Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Formula
> In [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^mod-b11-1-1|Model §B11.1.1]], let $r'$ be the distance from the source $S$ to a point $Q$ of the aperture and $r$ the distance from $Q$ to $P$; let $\chi'$ be the angle between the incident direction $S \to Q$ and the forward normal $\hat z$ of the screen, and $\chi$ the angle between $Q \to P$ and $\hat z$ (figure). Then
>
> $$
> U(P) = -\frac{i}{\lambda}\iint_{\mathcal A}U_{\text{inc}}(Q)\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\,K(\chi, \chi')\,dA, \qquad U_{\text{inc}}(Q) = U_0\,\frac{e^{ikr'}}{r'}, \qquad K = \frac{\cos\chi + \cos\chi'}{2},
> $$
>
> or, written out,
>
> $$
> U(P) = -\frac{ikU_0}{4\pi}\iint_{\mathcal A}\frac{e^{ik(r + r')}}{r\,r'}\,\bigl(\cos\chi + \cos\chi'\bigr)\,dA .
> $$
>
> The time dependence is $U(P)\,e^{-i\omega t}$. $K$ is the **obliquity factor**.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.2, eqs. (5.7)–(5.12) · PHY 300, Final practice exam (formula sheet)*

^thm-b11-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-2|Theorem §B11.1.2]] to the region on the receiving side of the screen, closed by the aperture, the dark side of the screen and a distant surface. By Kirchhoff's boundary conditions only the aperture contributes, with $U = U_{\text{inc}}$. On the aperture the outward normal of the region points back toward the source, $\hat n = -\hat z$. Write $\hat s'$ for the unit vector from $S$ to $Q$ and $\hat s$ for the unit vector from $Q$ to $P$; then $\hat n\cdot\hat s' = -\cos\chi'$, and $r$ grows when $Q$ moves along $-\hat s$, so $\partial r/\partial n = \hat n\cdot(-\hat s) = \cos\chi$. Differentiating the two spherical waves,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial U_{\text{inc}}}{\partial n} = \Bigl(ik - \frac{1}{r'}\Bigr)U_{\text{inc}}\,(-\cos\chi'), \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial n}\frac{e^{ikr}}{r} = \Bigl(ik - \frac1r\Bigr)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\cos\chi .
> $$
>
> With $kr, kr' \gg 1$ the terms $1/r$ and $1/r'$ are negligible beside $k$, and the integrand of Kirchhoff's theorem becomes
>
> $$
> \frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\,ik\,U_{\text{inc}}(-\cos\chi') - U_{\text{inc}}\,ik\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\cos\chi = -ik\,U_{\text{inc}}\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\,(\cos\chi + \cos\chi') .
> $$
>
> Dividing by $4\pi$ and using $k/4\pi = 1/(2\lambda)$ gives both forms.

*Uses:* [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-2|Theorem §B11.1.2]], [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^mod-b11-1-1|Model §B11.1.1]], [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^def-b11-1-1|Def. §B11.1.1]]

![[ph-wo-b11-1-1.svg]]
*The geometry of the Fresnel–Kirchhoff formula. Light from the point source $S$ reaches the aperture point $Q$ after a distance $r'$, at the angle $\chi'$ to the forward normal $\hat z$; the secondary wave from $Q$ reaches $P$ after a distance $r$, at the angle $\chi$. The integral runs over the open part of the screen only.*

> [!remark] Remark: Huygens's principle, now a theorem
> Read the formula as a sum of wavelets ([[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^pr-a4-4-1|Principle §A4.4.1]]): each element $dA$ of the aperture, reached by the primary wave with amplitude $U_{\text{inc}}(Q)$, sends out a secondary spherical wave $e^{ikr}/r$ of strength $-\frac{i}{\lambda}U_{\text{inc}}(Q)K\,dA$, and $P$ receives the superposition. Level A had to postulate the wavelets, their equal strengths and the forward-only rule, and [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^mod-a7-1-1|Model §A7.1.1]] added the phases by hand. The theorem fixes all of it, within the model:
> - **Amplitude** $1/\lambda$ per unit area. Short wavelengths make strong wavelets, but they also cancel more efficiently, and the sum over the whole wave front reproduces the incident wave exactly ([[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates|§B11.3]]).
> - **Phase** $-i = e^{-i\pi/2}$: with the time factor $e^{-i\omega t}$, the secondary waves run a quarter period ahead of the primary wave. Without this factor the wavelets would not add up to the unobstructed wave.
> - **Direction.** For a plane wave at normal incidence $\chi' = 0$ and $K = (1 + \cos\chi)/2$: equal to $1$ straight ahead and $0$ straight back. The wavelets have no backward wave, which Huygens had to forbid by decree.
>
> Schwartz's version of the same insight: on the far side of the screen the wave equation is solved with the field in the hole as boundary data, so "the receiver does not care what happened on the other side of the screen"; the hole acts as a source.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19, §1*

^rem-b11-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Other ways of writing the obliquity factor
> Fowles and the PHY 300 formula sheet write the bracket as $\cos(\hat n, \vec r) - \cos(\hat n, \vec r')$ (on the sheet $\cos\theta - \cos\theta'$), with the normal pointing back toward the source and $\vec r$ drawn from $P$ to $Q$. Their $\cos(\hat n, \vec r)$ is $\cos\chi$ and their $\cos(\hat n, \vec r')$ is $-\cos\chi'$, so the brackets agree. With the forward angles used here, the factor is $+1$ for straight-through light; with a wrong sign one gets the absurd result that a wide-open aperture blocks all light.

^cau-b11-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: How good are the assumptions?
> Kirchhoff's boundary conditions are not exact: near the edge the screen disturbs the field, and Fowles notes that their validity is "open to considerable debate". They work because, for an aperture many wavelengths across, the edge strip where they fail is a small fraction of the area, and because the results agree with experiment. The far surface closing the region contributes nothing if the field there is an outgoing wave; and the vector theory, rigorous for only a few simple shapes, lies beyond this level (Chapter C1, planned).
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.2*

## Babinet's principle

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.4: Babinet's Principle
> Two screens are **complementary** when the openings of one are exactly the opaque parts of the other. If $U_1(P)$ and $U_2(P)$ are the disturbances produced by complementary screens and $U_0(P)$ the disturbance with no screen at all, then
>
> $$
> U_1(P) + U_2(P) = U_0(P) .
> $$
>
> In particular, at every point where the unobstructed wave vanishes, $U_1 = -U_2$, and the two screens give the **same irradiance** there.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.2, eq. (5.13)*

^thm-b11-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The Fresnel–Kirchhoff integral ([[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]]) is additive in the region of integration, and the openings of the two screens together make up the whole plane. So $U_1 + U_2$ is the integral over the whole plane, which is the field without a screen. Where $U_0 = 0$, $\lvert U_1\rvert^2 = \lvert U_2\rvert^2$; the two fields differ only in sign, a phase of $180^\circ$ that the irradiance does not see.

^der-b11-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where the unobstructed field vanishes
> $U_0 = 0$ holds wherever the undisturbed beam does not reach: outside a collimated beam, or anywhere in the focal plane of a lens except the image of the source. There a small opaque particle scatters exactly like a hole of the same size and shape. Fowles's example: fog droplets scatter a searchlight beam into the same diffraction halo as a screen full of holes of the droplets' size. It is also how a thin wire or a hair is measured with a laser ([[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^ex-b11-1-2|Example §B11.1.2]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.2*

## Fraunhofer and Fresnel diffraction

> [!theorem] Theorem §B11.1.5: Path Length across the Aperture
> Put the aperture in the plane $z = 0$ with a point $\vec\xi$ of it measured from an origin $O$ in the opening; let $P$ lie at distance $d$ behind the plane, at transverse position $\vec x_P$, and $S$ at distance $d'$ in front, at transverse position $\vec x_S$. For $\lvert\vec\xi\rvert$, $\lvert\vec x_P\rvert \ll d$ and $\lvert\vec\xi\rvert$, $\lvert\vec x_S\rvert \ll d'$,
>
> $$
> r + r' = (r + r')_{\vec\xi = 0} \;-\; \vec\xi\cdot\Bigl(\frac{\vec x_P}{d} + \frac{\vec x_S}{d'}\Bigr) \;+\; \frac{\lvert\vec\xi\rvert^2}{2}\Bigl(\frac1d + \frac1{d'}\Bigr) \;+\; \dots
> $$
>
> The linear term tilts the phase across the aperture according to the directions of $S$ and $P$; the quadratic term measures the curvature of the wave fronts.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.3, eqs. (5.14)–(5.15)*

^thm-b11-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $r = \sqrt{d^2 + \lvert\vec x_P - \vec\xi\rvert^2}$. By Taylor's theorem ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), $\sqrt{d^2 + q} = d + q/(2d) + O(q^2/d^3)$, so
>
> $$
> r = d + \frac{\lvert\vec x_P\rvert^2}{2d} - \frac{\vec\xi\cdot\vec x_P}{d} + \frac{\lvert\vec\xi\rvert^2}{2d} + \dots, \qquad r' = d' + \frac{\lvert\vec x_S\rvert^2}{2d'} - \frac{\vec\xi\cdot\vec x_S}{d'} + \frac{\lvert\vec\xi\rvert^2}{2d'} + \dots
> $$
>
> The terms without $\vec\xi$ form $(r + r')_{\vec\xi = 0}$; collect the rest.

^der-b11-1-5

*Uses:* [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §B11.1.2: Fraunhofer and Fresnel Diffraction; Fresnel Number
> Let $a$ be the largest distance from $O$ to the edge of the aperture and define the length $L$ by
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{L} = \frac1d + \frac1{d'} .
> $$
>
> The **Fresnel number** is $N_F = a^2/(\lambda L)$. Diffraction is of the **Fraunhofer** type when the quadratic term of [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-5|Theorem §B11.1.5]] stays small compared with a wavelength over the whole opening, $\frac{a^2}{2}\bigl(\frac1d + \frac1{d'}\bigr) \ll \lambda$, i.e. $N_F \ll 1$: incident and diffracted waves are effectively plane across the aperture, and the phase is linear in $\vec\xi$. Otherwise ($N_F \gtrsim 1$) it is of the **Fresnel** type, and the curvature of the wave fronts must be kept. For an obstacle, $a$ is its size. There is no sharp boundary between the two.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.3, eq. (5.15) (the name "Fresnel number" is added here)*

^def-b11-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the criterion
> - For a plane incident wave ($d' \to \infty$), $L = d$ and the Fraunhofer region is $d \gg a^2/\lambda$: a $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ hole in red light ($a^2/\lambda \approx 0.4\ \mathrm m$) needs a screen several metres away, a $10\ \mathrm{cm}$ telescope objective kilometres. Fraunhofer's arrangement avoids the distance: a collimating lens makes the incident wave plane and a second lens brings each direction to a point of its focal plane, where the Fraunhofer pattern appears at any aperture size ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns|§B11.2]]).
> - $N_F$ counts the half-period zones that the aperture exposes as seen from $P$ ([[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates|§B11.3]]). Fraunhofer diffraction is the case of less than one zone, Fresnel diffraction the case of a few, and geometrical optics the limit of very many.
> - The expansion also needs the quartic terms of $r$ to be negligible. Keeping the quadratic term but dropping these is the Fresnel (paraxial) approximation used in [[§B11.3 Fresnel Diffraction and Zone Plates|§B11.3]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.3–5.4*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B11.1.1: Fresnel or Fraunhofer?
> A pinhole source ($\lambda = 600\ \mathrm{nm}$) stands $d' = 10\ \mathrm m$ from a hole of diameter $1\ \mathrm{mm}$, so $a = 0.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ and $a^2 = 2.5 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2}$.
> - Screen at $d = 1\ \mathrm{cm}$: $1/L = 100 + 0.1\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $L = 9.99\ \mathrm{mm}$, and $N_F = 2.5 \times 10^{-7}/(6.0 \times 10^{-7} \times 9.99 \times 10^{-3}) = 42$. About forty Fresnel zones are open: Fresnel diffraction, close to geometrical optics, a bright disk the size of the hole with fine fringes at its rim.
> - Screen at $d = 2\ \mathrm m$: $1/L = 0.5 + 0.1 = 0.6\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $L = 1.67\ \mathrm m$, $N_F = 0.25$. The quadratic path term at the rim is $a^2/(2L) = 7.5 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm m = \lambda/8$: Fraunhofer diffraction to a fair approximation. The pattern is then an Airy disk whose first dark ring has the angular radius $1.22\lambda/D = 7.3 \times 10^{-4}$, i.e. $1.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ on the screen, well outside the rim of the geometric image of the hole (radius $0.6\ \mathrm{mm}$): the light is spread by diffraction, not cast as a shadow.
>
> *Source: Fowles, Problem 5.1*

^ex-b11-1-1

> [!example] Example §B11.1.2: Measuring a Hair
> A helium–neon laser beam ($\lambda = 633\ \mathrm{nm}$) crosses a hair, and a screen $L = 2.00\ \mathrm m$ away shows, on either side of the bright central spot of the beam, a row of dark fringes spaced $\Delta y = 15\ \mathrm{mm}$. Outside the direct beam the unobstructed field is zero, so by Babinet's principle ([[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-4|Theorem §B11.1.4]]) the hair diffracts like a slit of the same width $D$, whose minima are $\lambda L/D$ apart ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-2|Theorem §A7.1.2]] with small angles). Hence
>
> $$
> D = \frac{\lambda L}{\Delta y} = \frac{(633 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm m)(2.00\ \mathrm m)}{0.015\ \mathrm m} = 84\ \mu\mathrm m .
> $$
>
> The centre of the pattern is not that of a slit: there the undiffracted beam ($U_0 \neq 0$) dominates.
>
> *Source: Fowles §5.2 (Babinet's principle); numbers chosen here*
