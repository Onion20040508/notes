Level A stated without proof that a plucked string moves as a sum of its normal modes, with amplitudes fixed by the initial shape ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^rem-a2-6-3|Remark: Resonance and general motion]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^rem-a2-2-2|Remark: Why this equation matters]]). For a chain of $N$ beads this is linear algebra: the $N$ mode vectors form a basis, and the expansion is a discrete sine sum ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]]). For the continuous string it is a statement about functions: which functions can be written as sums of sines and cosines, in what sense the sum converges, and how the coefficients are found. This section answers these questions for periodic functions. It proves orthogonality, the coefficient formulas, Bessel's inequality and the decay of the coefficients; it states Fourier's theorem with its conditions and sketches the proof; and it works out the Gibbs overshoot at a jump. The string itself is solved in [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String|§B4.2]].

## Fourier series and orthogonality

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.1: Fourier Series; Fourier Coefficients; Partial Sum
> Let $f$ be periodic with period $L$, $f(x + L) = f(x)$, and integrable over one period. Its **Fourier coefficients** are
>
> $$
> a_0 = \frac1L\int_0^L f(x)\,dx, \qquad a_n = \frac2L\int_0^L f(x)\cos k_nx\,dx, \qquad b_n = \frac2L\int_0^L f(x)\sin k_nx\,dx, \qquad k_n = \frac{2\pi n}{L},\ n \ge 1,
> $$
>
> and its **Fourier series** is the formal sum
>
> $$
> f(x) \sim a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(a_n\cos k_nx + b_n\sin k_nx\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The **$N$th partial sum** $S_N(x)$ is the sum up to $n = N$; it is a *trigonometric polynomial* of degree $N$. Any interval of length $L$ may replace $[0, L]$ in the integrals. For a signal periodic in time with period $T$, read $t$ for $x$ and $n\omega_1$ for $k_n$, with fundamental angular frequency $\omega_1 = 2\pi/T$; the terms with $n \ge 2$ are the harmonics of $\omega_1$ ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^def-a2-6-2|Def. §A2.6.2]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, eqs. (7)–(10)*

^def-b4-1-1

The symbol $\sim$ only assigns the series to $f$; whether and how it converges to $f$ is the content of [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]].

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.1: Orthogonality of Sines and Cosines
> For integers $n, m \ge 1$, with $k_n = 2\pi n/L$,
>
> $$
> \int_0^L \cos k_nx\,\cos k_mx\,dx = \frac L2\,\delta_{nm}, \qquad \int_0^L \sin k_nx\,\sin k_mx\,dx = \frac L2\,\delta_{nm}, \qquad \int_0^L \cos k_nx\,\sin k_mx\,dx = 0,
> $$
>
> and $\int_0^L \cos k_nx\,dx = \int_0^L \sin k_nx\,dx = 0$. Here $\delta_{nm}$ is $1$ if $n = m$ and $0$ otherwise. In words: the functions $1, \cos k_nx, \sin k_nx$ are mutually orthogonal for the inner product $\langle u, v\rangle = \int_0^L u(x)v(x)\,dx$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, eqs. (11)–(19) · PHY 287, Problem Set 10*

^thm-b4-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For an integer $p \ne 0$, $\int_0^L \cos(2\pi px/L)\,dx = \bigl[\tfrac{L}{2\pi p}\sin(2\pi px/L)\bigr]_0^L = 0$, and likewise for the sine: an integral over $p$ whole periods. The products reduce to such integrals by the product-to-sum identities
>
> $$
> \cos P\cos Q = \tfrac12\bigl[\cos(P - Q) + \cos(P + Q)\bigr], \quad \sin P\sin Q = \tfrac12\bigl[\cos(P - Q) - \cos(P + Q)\bigr], \quad \cos P\sin Q = \tfrac12\bigl[\sin(P + Q) - \sin(P - Q)\bigr],
> $$
>
> with $P = k_nx$, $Q = k_mx$. Since $n + m \ge 2$, every term with $P + Q$ integrates to zero; so does every term with $P - Q$ when $n \ne m$. When $n = m$, $\cos(P - Q) = 1$ integrates to $L$, which gives $L/2$ for the cosine and the sine products, while $\sin(P - Q) = 0$.

^der-b4-1-1

*Uses:* [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-4|451 Thm. §34.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.2: Fourier Coefficients by Projection
> If $f(x) = a_0 + \sum_{n \ge 1}(a_n\cos k_nx + b_n\sin k_nx)$ with a series that converges uniformly, then its coefficients are the Fourier coefficients of $f$ ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]). In particular, two uniformly convergent trigonometric series with the same sum have the same coefficients.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.4 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, eqs. (13)–(23) · PHY 287, week 14 Fourier slides*

^thm-b4-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Multiply the series by $\cos k_mx$ and integrate over a period. Uniform convergence allows integrating term by term, and by [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]] every term but one vanishes:
>
> $$
> \int_0^L f(x)\cos k_mx\,dx = \sum_{n \ge 1} a_n\,\frac L2\,\delta_{nm} = \frac L2\,a_m .
> $$
>
> The same with $\sin k_mx$ gives $b_m$, and integrating the series itself gives $a_0L$. The coefficients are therefore determined by $f$, which is the uniqueness statement.

^der-b4-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The same computation as for vectors
> The coefficient formulas are projections. In $\mathbb R^N$ with an orthonormal basis $e_j$, $v = \sum_j\langle v, e_j\rangle e_j$ ([[§20 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-30|LADR Thm. 6.30]]); here the "basis vectors" are $1, \cos k_nx, \sin k_nx$, orthogonal but with squared length $L$ or $L/2$, whence the factors $1/L$ and $2/L$. For the bead chain this is literally the expansion of the initial displacements in the mode vectors ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]]), which are orthogonal eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]); the series' dictionary turns the sum $\sum_j u_jv_j$ over beads into $\int u(x)v(x)\,dx$ as the spacing goes to zero, while the mode vectors become $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ ([[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]). What linear algebra cannot supply is that infinitely many orthogonal functions still span "everything": that is [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]].
>
> *Source: Series I §9.4 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, §6*

^rem-b4-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.3: Best Approximation and Bessel's Inequality
> Let $f$ be $L$-periodic and square-integrable over a period. Among all trigonometric polynomials $P$ of degree $\le N$, the partial sum $S_N$ of the Fourier series makes the mean-square error $\int_0^L (f - P)^2\,dx$ smallest, and
>
> $$
> \frac1L\int_0^L (f - S_N)^2\,dx = \frac1L\int_0^L f^2\,dx - \Bigl[a_0^2 + \frac12\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigl(a_n^2 + b_n^2\bigr)\Bigr] \ \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> Hence (**Bessel's inequality**) $a_0^2 + \tfrac12\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_n^2 + b_n^2) \le \tfrac1L\int_0^L f^2\,dx$; in particular $a_n, b_n \to 0$. Adding terms never makes the approximation worse, and the coefficients already found do not change when $N$ grows.
>
> *Source: added here (the sources state only the coefficient formulas); the abstract form is* [[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-5|556 Thm. §20.5]]

^thm-b4-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $P = \alpha_0 + \sum_{n \le N}(\alpha_n\cos k_nx + \beta_n\sin k_nx)$ and expand $\int (f - P)^2 = \int f^2 - 2\int fP + \int P^2$. By the coefficient formulas, $\int fP = L\bigl[a_0\alpha_0 + \tfrac12\sum(a_n\alpha_n + b_n\beta_n)\bigr]$, and by orthogonality ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]) $\int P^2 = L\bigl[\alpha_0^2 + \tfrac12\sum(\alpha_n^2 + \beta_n^2)\bigr]$. Completing the squares,
>
> $$
> \frac1L\int_0^L (f - P)^2 = \frac1L\int_0^L f^2 - \Bigl[a_0^2 + \tfrac12\sum(a_n^2 + b_n^2)\Bigr] + (\alpha_0 - a_0)^2 + \tfrac12\sum\bigl[(\alpha_n - a_n)^2 + (\beta_n - b_n)^2\bigr] .
> $$
>
> The last group is $\ge 0$ and vanishes exactly for $P = S_N$, which proves the minimising property and the error formula. The left side is $\ge 0$, so the partial sums of $\sum(a_n^2 + b_n^2)$ are bounded; a convergent series has terms tending to zero.

^der-b4-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]

## Complex form

> [!definition] Definition §B4.1.2: Complex Fourier Series
> For an $L$-periodic $f$ (real or complex) the **complex Fourier coefficients** and series are
>
> $$
> c_n = \frac1L\int_0^L f(x)\,e^{-ik_nx}\,dx, \qquad f(x) \sim \sum_{n = -\infty}^{\infty} c_n\,e^{+ik_nx}, \qquad k_n = \frac{2\pi n}{L},\ n \in \mathbb Z,
> $$
>
> the partial sum $S_N$ running over $\lvert n\rvert \le N$. In time, $p(t) \sim \sum_n c_n e^{+in\omega_1t}$ with $c_n = \frac1T\int_0^T p(t)e^{-in\omega_1t}\,dt$. The sign is chosen to match the time dependence $e^{+i\omega t}$ used throughout level B (physical quantity = real part; [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]]): the coefficient $c_n$ with $n > 0$ multiplies the positive-frequency exponential $e^{+in\omega_1t}$. Quantum mechanics uses $e^{-i\omega t}$, and then the roles of $c_n$ and $c_{-n}$ are exchanged.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, §5, eqs. (35)–(36)*

^def-b4-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.4: Complex and Real Coefficients
> The exponentials are orthonormal for the normalised inner product: $\frac1L\int_0^L e^{ik_mx}\,e^{-ik_nx}\,dx = \delta_{mn}$. The complex and real coefficients are related by
>
> $$
> c_0 = a_0, \qquad c_n = \tfrac12(a_n - ib_n), \qquad c_{-n} = \tfrac12(a_n + ib_n) \qquad (n \ge 1),
> $$
>
> so $S_N$ is the same function in both forms. The function $f$ is real exactly when $c_{-n} = c_n^*$ for all $n$, and then $a_n = 2\operatorname{Re}c_n$, $b_n = -2\operatorname{Im}c_n$, and $a_n\cos k_nx + b_n\sin k_nx = 2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(c_ne^{ik_nx}\bigr) = 2\lvert c_n\rvert\cos(k_nx + \arg c_n)$: each harmonic has amplitude $2\lvert c_n\rvert$ and phase $\arg c_n$. In the complex form the Bessel and Parseval sums read $\sum_n \lvert c_n\rvert^2$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, §5, eqs. (37)–(43)*

^thm-b4-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $m \ne n$, $\int_0^L e^{i(m - n)2\pi x/L}\,dx = \frac{L}{2\pi i(m - n)}\bigl[e^{2\pi i(m - n)} - 1\bigr] = 0$, and for $m = n$ the integrand is $1$. Insert $e^{-ik_nx} = \cos k_nx - i\sin k_nx$ into the definition of $c_n$ and compare with [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]]: $c_n = \tfrac12a_n - \tfrac i2b_n$, and $k_{-n} = -k_n$ gives $c_{-n} = \tfrac12a_n + \tfrac i2b_n$. Then $c_ne^{ik_nx} + c_{-n}e^{-ik_nx} = a_n\cos k_nx + b_n\sin k_nx$ by Euler's formula. If $f$ is real, conjugating the integral for $c_n$ gives $c_n^* = c_{-n}$; conversely, if $c_{-n} = c_n^*$ the terms pair into real numbers. The Parseval sums agree because $\lvert c_n\rvert^2 + \lvert c_{-n}\rvert^2 = \tfrac12(a_n^2 + b_n^2)$.

^der-b4-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^def-b4-1-1|Def. §B4.1.1]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^def-b4-1-2|Def. §B4.1.2]]

## Smoothness, decay and convergence

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.5: Smoothness Controls the Decay of the Coefficients
> Let $f$ be $L$-periodic and **piecewise smooth**: on each period $f$ and $f'$ are continuous except at finitely many points, where both have finite one-sided limits. Let $J_j = f(x_j^+) - f(x_j^-)$ be the jumps of $f$ at the points $x_j$ of a period. Then, for $n \ne 0$,
>
> $$
> c_n[f] = \frac{1}{ik_nL}\sum_j J_j\,e^{-ik_nx_j} + \frac{1}{ik_n}\,c_n[f'] .
> $$
>
> Consequently:
> - if $f$ has jumps, $\lvert c_n\rvert$ falls off like $1/n$ (unless the jump terms happen to cancel);
> - if $f$ is continuous but $f'$ jumps (kinks), $c_n = c_n[f']/(ik_n)$ falls off like $1/n^2$;
> - each further continuous derivative gains another factor $1/n$; for a smooth $f$ the coefficients fall faster than any power of $1/n$.
>
> *Source: added here; the cases are those of the examples below, of the plucked string ([[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String|§B4.2]]) and of PHY 287, Final practice*

^thm-b4-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Split the period at the points $x_j$ and integrate by parts on each piece $[x_j, x_{j+1}]$:
>
> $$
> \int_{x_j}^{x_{j+1}} f\,e^{-ik_nx}\,dx = \Bigl[\frac{f\,e^{-ik_nx}}{-ik_n}\Bigr]_{x_j^+}^{x_{j+1}^-} + \frac{1}{ik_n}\int_{x_j}^{x_{j+1}} f'\,e^{-ik_nx}\,dx .
> $$
>
> Summing over the pieces (and using periodicity, so that the last piece joins the first), each break point $x_j$ collects $\bigl[f(x_j^+) - f(x_j^-)\bigr]e^{-ik_nx_j}/(ik_n) = J_je^{-ik_nx_j}/(ik_n)$. Dividing by $L$ gives the formula. The jump sum is bounded, so its contribution is of order $1/n$. If $f$ is continuous, all $J_j = 0$, and $c_n[f']$ itself tends to zero by Bessel's inequality ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]]) and is of order $1/n$ when $f'$ is piecewise smooth (apply the formula to $f'$); then $c_n[f] = O(1/n^2)$. Repeat for higher derivatives.

^der-b4-1-5

*Uses:* [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.6: Fourier's Theorem
> Let $f$ be $L$-periodic.
> 1. **Mean square.** If $\int_0^L \lvert f\rvert^2\,dx < \infty$, then $\int_0^L \lvert f - S_N\rvert^2\,dx \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$, and **Parseval's identity** holds:
>    $$
>    \frac1L\int_0^L \lvert f\rvert^2\,dx = \sum_{n = -\infty}^{\infty}\lvert c_n\rvert^2 = a_0^2 + \frac12\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(a_n^2 + b_n^2\bigr) \quad (f \text{ real}) .
>    $$
> 2. **Pointwise.** If $f$ is piecewise smooth, then at every $x$
>    $$
>    \lim_{N\to\infty} S_N(x) = \tfrac12\bigl[f(x^+) + f(x^-)\bigr],
>    $$
>    which is $f(x)$ wherever $f$ is continuous and the midpoint of the jump where it is not.
> 3. **Uniform.** If $f$ is continuous and piecewise smooth, then $\sum_n \lvert c_n\rvert < \infty$ and $S_N \to f$ uniformly.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.4 (stated) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, §1–2 (stated for square-integrable functions) · conditions and proof idea added here*

^thm-b4-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The idea, with the analytic steps cited.*
> - *Physical motivation.* For $N$ beads the mode vectors span $\mathbb R^N$ ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]], [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]]), and the sampled modes $\sin(jn\pi/(N + 1))$ become $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ in the continuum limit ([[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]); both the series and Schwartz argue that the expansion should survive $N \to \infty$. That is a heuristic, not a proof: in infinite dimensions an orthogonal family can miss directions.
> - *Part 1.* By [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]] the error $\int\lvert f - S_N\rvert^2$ decreases with $N$ and tends to $L\bigl[\tfrac1L\int\lvert f\rvert^2 - \sum\lvert c_n\rvert^2\bigr] \ge 0$. It tends to zero, which is Parseval, exactly when the exponentials are *complete*: no nonzero square-integrable function is orthogonal to all of them ([[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-8|556 Thm. §20.8]]). Completeness of the Fourier basis is [[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-11|556 Thm. §20.11]]; its proof (deferred there as well) approximates continuous periodic functions uniformly by averages of the $S_N$ (Fejér's theorem) and uses that continuous functions are dense in $L^2$. Mathematical Methods, planned.
> - *Part 2.* Inserting the coefficients into $S_N$ and summing the geometric series $\sum_{\lvert n\rvert \le N}e^{in\theta} = \sin\bigl((N + \tfrac12)\theta\bigr)/\sin(\theta/2)$ gives
>   $$
>   S_N(x) = \frac1L\int_{-L/2}^{L/2} f(x + s)\,\frac{\sin\bigl((2N + 1)\pi s/L\bigr)}{\sin(\pi s/L)}\,ds ,
>   $$
>   a weighted average of $f$ near $x$ with a kernel of total weight $1$ (take $f = 1$), half of it on each side of $s = 0$, that oscillates ever faster away from $s = 0$. For piecewise smooth $f$ the quotients $\bigl[f(x + s) - f(x^\pm)\bigr]/\sin(\pi s/L)$ stay bounded for $s \to 0^\pm$, and an integrable function integrated against $\sin\bigl((2N + 1)\pi s/L\bigr)$ tends to zero (the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma; not proved here). What remains is $\tfrac12f(x^+) + \tfrac12f(x^-)$.
> - *Part 3.* If $f$ is continuous, [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]] gives $c_n[f] = c_n[f']/(ik_n)$, so by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $\sum_{n \ne 0}\lvert c_n\rvert \le \bigl(\sum\lvert c_n[f']\rvert^2\bigr)^{1/2}\bigl(\sum (L/2\pi n)^2\bigr)^{1/2}$, which is finite by Bessel's inequality for $f'$. A series of continuous functions bounded termwise by a convergent series converges uniformly, and by part 2 its sum is $f$.

^der-b4-1-6

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-3|Theorem §B4.1.3]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]], [[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-8|556 Thm. §20.8]], [[§20 Orthonormal Sets and Bases#^thm-20-11|556 Thm. §20.11]], [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]

> [!caution] Caution: "Any function can be written as a Fourier series"
> Schwartz's lecture says that every square-integrable periodic function *is* its Fourier series, and then that physicists may consider this proved. Both statements need care.
> - For a square-integrable $f$ the equality holds **in the mean** (part 1): the integrated squared error goes to zero. The series need not converge to $f(x)$ at a given point.
> - At a jump the series converges to the **midpoint**, not to either one-sided value (part 2), and the partial sums overshoot near the jump by a fixed fraction of it (the Gibbs phenomenon, [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-8|Theorem §B4.1.8]]); convergence there is never uniform.
> - Uniform convergence, which allows term-by-term integration and justifies [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]], needs $f$ continuous (on the circle: $f(0) = f(L)$) and piecewise smooth (part 3).
>
> For the initial shapes of strings these conditions hold, and every series in this chapter converges in the strongest sense that the shape allows.

## Sine and cosine series on an interval

A string occupies $0 \le x \le L$; nothing makes it periodic. Its expansions come from extending $f$ to a periodic function.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.7: Sine and Cosine Series on an Interval
> Let $f$ be piecewise smooth on $[0, L]$. Then on $0 < x < L$
>
> $$
> f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad B_n = \frac2L\int_0^L f(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx \qquad \text{(sine series)},
> $$
>
> $$
> f(x) = A_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}, \qquad A_0 = \frac1L\int_0^L f\,dx, \quad A_n = \frac2L\int_0^L f(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx \qquad \text{(cosine series)},
> $$
>
> at every point of continuity (the midpoint at jumps), with the orthogonality relation $\int_0^L\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{m\pi x}{L}\,dx = \frac L2\delta_{nm}$ (and the same for cosines). The sine series is $0$ at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ whatever $f$ is there; it converges uniformly on $[0, L]$ if $f$ is continuous with $f(0) = f(L) = 0$. The cosine series converges uniformly whenever $f$ is continuous.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.4, eqs. for orthogonality and $A_m$ · PHY 287, week 14 Fourier slides · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, §2*

^thm-b4-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Extend $f$ to $[-L, L]$ as an **odd** function, $f(-x) = -f(x)$, and then periodically with period $2L$. With period $2L$ the wave numbers are $2\pi n/2L = n\pi/L$. The product of the odd extension with $\cos(n\pi x/L)$ is odd and integrates to zero, so all $a_n$ vanish; with $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ it is even, so
>
> $$
> b_n = \frac{2}{2L}\int_{-L}^{L} f\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = \frac2L\int_0^L f\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = B_n .
> $$
>
> [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]] for the extension gives the convergence. The extension is continuous at $0$ and $L$ only if $f(0) = f(L) = 0$; otherwise it jumps there, and the series gives the midpoint $0$. The **even** extension gives the cosine series in the same way; it is continuous at $0$ and $L$ automatically. Orthogonality on $[0, L]$ is half of the orthogonality on $[-L, L]$ ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]), since the products are even.

^der-b4-1-7

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Which series for which boundary
> The sines vanish at both ends, so they are the natural basis for a string with fixed ends ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]]); the cosines have zero slope at both ends and suit free ends and pipes open at both ends ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]). In each case the basis is the set of normal modes of the system, and the expansion of the initial state is the decomposition into modes.

## The Gibbs phenomenon

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.1.8: Gibbs Phenomenon
> Let $f$ be $L$-periodic and piecewise smooth, with a jump $J = f(x_0^+) - f(x_0^-)$ at $x_0$ and no other break point nearby. Then, for fixed $u$, as $N \to \infty$,
>
> $$
> S_N\Bigl(x_0 + \frac{uL}{2\pi N}\Bigr) \to f(x_0^-) + J\Bigl[\frac12 + \frac{\operatorname{Si}(u)}{\pi}\Bigr], \qquad \operatorname{Si}(u) = \int_0^u \frac{\sin s}{s}\,ds .
> $$
>
> The limit profile passes through the midpoint at $u = 0$ and has its extremes at $u = \pm\pi$, a distance $L/2N$ on either side of the jump, where $\operatorname{Si}(\pi) = 1.8519$. There the partial sums overshoot the function by
>
> $$
> \Bigl(\frac{\operatorname{Si}(\pi)}{\pi} - \frac12\Bigr)\lvert J\rvert = 0.0895\,\lvert J\rvert
> $$
>
> on each side, about 9 % of the jump. As $N$ grows the overshoot moves closer to the jump but does not get smaller.
>
> *Source: added here; the overshoot is visible in Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, Figs. 2–3*

^thm-b4-1-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Reduce to one sawtooth.* The function $\sigma(y) = \tfrac12 - y/L$ on $0 < y < L$, extended with period $L$, jumps up by $1$ at $y = 0$ and has the series $\sigma(y) = \sum_{n \ge 1}\sin(2\pi ny/L)/(\pi n)$ ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^ex-b4-1-1|Example §B4.1.1]] with $x = y/L$). The function $g(x) = f(x) - J\sigma(x - x_0)$ has no jump at $x_0$, so near $x_0$ its partial sums converge uniformly to $g$ (the argument of part 3 of [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]], applied locally; stated). All the non-uniform behaviour near $x_0$ comes from $J\sigma$.
>
> *The sawtooth near its jump.* At $y = uL/(2\pi N)$, with $h = u/N$,
>
> $$
> \sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\sin(2\pi ny/L)}{\pi n} = \frac1\pi\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\sin(nh)}{nh}\,h \ \longrightarrow\ \frac1\pi\int_0^u\frac{\sin s}{s}\,ds = \frac{\operatorname{Si}(u)}{\pi},
> $$
>
> a Riemann sum for the integral with step $h \to 0$ over the fixed range $0$ to $u$. Near $x_0$, $g \approx g(x_0) = f(x_0^+) - J\sigma(0^+) = f(x_0^-) + J/2$, which gives the profile. $\operatorname{Si}'(u) = \sin u/u$ vanishes first at $u = \pi$, so the maximum of $\operatorname{Si}$ is $\operatorname{Si}(\pi) = 1.8519 > \pi/2 = \operatorname{Si}(\infty)$; numerically $1.8519/\pi - 0.5 = 0.0895$.

> [!remark] Remark: What the overshoot means
> The Gibbs overshoot is a property of truncation, not a failure of the series: at every fixed $x \ne x_0$ the partial sums converge to $f(x)$, but the point of largest error moves with $N$. It matters whenever a signal with a jump is cut off at a highest frequency, as in a band-limited audio channel or a low-pass filter ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function|§B4.4]]): a square wave sent through it rings at its edges by 9 % of the step. Smoother functions show no such effect, since their series converge uniformly.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.1.1: The Sawtooth
> Let $f(x) = x$ for $0 < x < 1$, extended with period $L = 1$. Then $a_0 = \int_0^1 x\,dx = \tfrac12$. Integrating by parts,
>
> $$
> a_n = 2\int_0^1 x\cos 2\pi nx\,dx = \Bigl[\frac{x\sin 2\pi nx}{\pi n}\Bigr]_0^1 - \frac1{\pi n}\int_0^1\sin 2\pi nx\,dx = 0, \qquad b_n = 2\int_0^1 x\sin 2\pi nx\,dx = \Bigl[-\frac{x\cos 2\pi nx}{\pi n}\Bigr]_0^1 + \frac{1}{\pi n}\int_0^1\cos 2\pi nx\,dx = -\frac1{\pi n},
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> f(x) = \frac12 - \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin 2\pi nx}{\pi n} = \frac12 - \frac1\pi\Bigl(\sin 2\pi x + \frac12\sin 4\pi x + \frac13\sin 6\pi x + \cdots\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> - *At the jump*, $x = 0$, every sine vanishes and the series gives $\tfrac12$, the midpoint of $f(0^-) = 1$ and $f(0^+) = 0$ (part 2 of [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]).
> - *Decay.* The coefficients fall as $1/n$, as [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]] predicts for one jump $J = -1$ per period: $c_n = J/(ik_nL) = i/(2\pi n)$, and indeed $c_n = \tfrac12(a_n - ib_n) = i/(2\pi n)$.
> - *Parseval.* $\int_0^1 x^2\,dx = \tfrac13 = a_0^2 + \tfrac12\sum b_n^2 = \tfrac14 + \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{1}{n^2}$, so
>
> $$
> \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} .
> $$
>
> The figure above shows the partial sums: three terms already give the shape, ten give it well away from the jumps, and near the jumps the Gibbs overshoot remains.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, §3, eqs. (24)–(30) and Figs. 1–3 (Parseval and the Gibbs analysis added here)*

^ex-b4-1-1

> [!example] Example §B4.1.2: The Square Wave
> Let $f = +1$ on $0 < x < L/2$ and $f = -1$ on $L/2 < x < L$, period $L$. Then $f$ is odd about $x = 0$ (on the circle), so $a_n = 0$ for all $n$, and
>
> $$
> b_n = \frac2L\Bigl[\int_0^{L/2}\sin k_nx\,dx - \int_{L/2}^{L}\sin k_nx\,dx\Bigr] = \frac{2}{\pi n}\bigl[1 - (-1)^n\bigr] = \begin{cases}4/\pi n, & n \text{ odd},\\ 0, & n \text{ even},\end{cases}
> $$
>
> $$
> f(x) = \frac4\pi\Bigl(\sin k_1x + \frac13\sin 3k_1x + \frac15\sin 5k_1x + \cdots\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> - *Only odd harmonics.* The wave satisfies $f(x + L/2) = -f(x)$, which every odd harmonic shares and every even harmonic violates.
> - *Gibbs.* The jumps have $\lvert J\rvert = 2$, so the partial sums peak at $1 + 0.0895 \times 2 = 1.18$ next to each jump ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-8|Theorem §B4.1.8]]).
> - *Power per harmonic.* Parseval gives $\tfrac1L\int f^2 = 1 = \tfrac12\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}16/(\pi^2n^2)$, so $\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}1/n^2 = \pi^2/8$. The fundamental carries the fraction $\tfrac12(4/\pi)^2 = 8/\pi^2 = 81\,\%$ of the mean square; the remaining $19\,\%$ is spread over the odd harmonics, falling as $1/n^2$.
>
> Played as a sound, this is the "tinny" square-wave note of [[§B4.3 Harmonics, Timbre and Musical Scales|§B4.3]], with the same pitch as a pure tone of frequency $\omega_1$ but a very different timbre.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7, §1 (square-wave note and its spectrum; coefficients computed here)*
