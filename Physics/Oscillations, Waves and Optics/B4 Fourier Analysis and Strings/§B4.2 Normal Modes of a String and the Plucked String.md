Level A found the normal modes of a string fixed at both ends by fitting reflected sinusoidal waves between the ends ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]]), and stated that a plucked string moves as a superposition of them ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^rem-a2-6-3|Remark: Resonance and general motion]]). Here both statements are derived from the wave equation of [[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation]]. Separation of variables produces the modes and shows there are no others. Fourier's theorem ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series|§B4.1]]) fits any initial shape and velocity, and the solution agrees with d'Alembert's travelling-wave form. Energy conservation makes it unique and splits the energy mode by mode. The plucked string is then solved exactly, and its spectrum is read off.

Notation: string of length $L$, tension $T$, mass per length $\mu$, wave speed $c = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ (the series' symbols; level A writes $F_T$ and $v = \sqrt{F_T/\mu}$, which are the same quantities); $k_n = n\pi/L$ and $\omega_n = ck_n$.

## The initial-value problem

> [!definition] Definition §B4.2.1: Initial-Value Problem of the Fixed String
> The motion $y(x, t)$ of an ideal string ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^mod-a2-3-1|Model §A2.3.1]]) held at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ is the solution of
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = c^2\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} \quad (0 < x < L), \qquad y(0, t) = y(L, t) = 0, \qquad y(x, 0) = f(x), \quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial t}(x, 0) = g(x),
> $$
>
> with a given initial shape $f$ and initial velocity $g$, both vanishing at the ends. A string **plucked** from rest has $g = 0$; a string **struck** in its rest position has $f = 0$.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.4, eq. for the initial data*

^def-b4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.1: The Normal Modes Are the Separable Solutions
> The nonzero solutions of the wave equation with fixed ends that have the product form $y = X(x)\,\Theta(t)$ are exactly
>
> $$
> y_n(x, t) = \sin(k_nx)\,\bigl[A\cos\omega_nt + B\sin\omega_nt\bigr], \qquad k_n = \frac{n\pi}{L}, \quad \omega_n = ck_n = \frac{n\pi c}{L}, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
> $$
>
> This derives [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]] from the wave equation alone: level A assumed sinusoidal travelling waves and their reflections; here every standing pattern is found, and none has a frequency other than $nc/2L$.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.3–9.4 · PHY 287, week 14 Fourier slides*

^thm-b4-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Substituting $y = X\Theta$ and dividing by $c^2X\Theta$ (where nonzero),
>
> $$
> \frac{\Theta''(t)}{c^2\,\Theta(t)} = \frac{X''(x)}{X(x)} .
> $$
>
> The left side depends only on $t$, the right side only on $x$, so both equal one constant, $-\kappa$. Then $X'' = -\kappa X$ with $X(0) = X(L) = 0$.
> - $\kappa < 0$: $X = \alpha\cosh(\sqrt{-\kappa}\,x) + \beta\sinh(\sqrt{-\kappa}\,x)$; $X(0) = 0$ gives $\alpha = 0$, and $X(L) = \beta\sinh(\sqrt{-\kappa}\,L) = 0$ gives $\beta = 0$.
> - $\kappa = 0$: $X$ is linear and vanishes at both ends, so $X = 0$.
> - $\kappa > 0$: $X = \beta\sin(\sqrt\kappa\,x)$ after $X(0) = 0$, and $X(L) = 0$ with $\beta \ne 0$ needs $\sqrt\kappa\,L = n\pi$, $n = 1, 2, \ldots$
>
> So $\kappa = k_n^2$, and $\Theta'' = -c^2k_n^2\Theta$ is simple harmonic motion at $\omega_n = ck_n$ ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]). Conversely, each $y_n$ solves the equation: $\partial_t^2$ gives the factor $-\omega_n^2$, $c^2\partial_x^2$ gives $-c^2k_n^2$, and these are equal.

^der-b4-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]], [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^def-b4-2-1|Def. §B4.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.2: Energy of the String Is Conserved
> For a solution with fixed ends, the energy
>
> $$
> E = \int_0^L\Bigl[\frac12\,\mu\Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\Bigr)^2 + \frac12\,T\Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Bigr)^2\Bigr]dx ,
> $$
>
> the integral of the kinetic and stretching energy densities of [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]], is constant in time. Consequently the solution of [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^def-b4-2-1|Def. §B4.2.1]] (twice continuously differentiable) is **unique**.
>
> *Source: added here, from the energy densities of Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10*

^thm-b4-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate under the integral and use the wave equation in the form $\mu\,\partial_t^2y = T\,\partial_x^2y$:
>
> $$
> \frac{dE}{dt} = \int_0^L\bigl(\mu\,y_t\,y_{tt} + T\,y_x\,y_{xt}\bigr)dx = T\int_0^L\bigl(y_t\,y_{xx} + y_x\,y_{xt}\bigr)dx = T\int_0^L\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigl(y_x\,y_t\bigr)dx = T\bigl[y_x\,y_t\bigr]_0^L = 0,
> $$
>
> because $y_t = 0$ at a fixed end. Physically, $-T\,y_xy_t$ is the power flowing along the string ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^der-a2-4-3|derivation of Theorem §A2.4.3]]), and a fixed end does no work. *Uniqueness:* the difference $w$ of two solutions with the same data solves the problem with $f = g = 0$, so its energy is $0$ at $t = 0$ and hence always. Both squares then vanish: $w_t = w_x = 0$, so $w$ is constant, and $w = 0$ at the ends.

^der-b4-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]], [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^def-b4-2-1|Def. §B4.2.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-4|451 Thm. §34.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.3: Solution by Normal Modes
> Let $f$ and $g$ be continuous and piecewise smooth on $[0, L]$, vanishing at both ends. Then the motion of the string is
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sin(k_nx)\,\bigl[A_n\cos\omega_nt + B_n\sin\omega_nt\bigr], \qquad A_n = \frac2L\int_0^L f(x)\sin(k_nx)\,dx, \qquad B_n = \frac{2}{L\,\omega_n}\int_0^L g(x)\sin(k_nx)\,dx .
> $$
>
> 1. The same function is d'Alembert's form
>    $$
>    y(x, t) = \frac12\bigl[F(x - ct) + F(x + ct)\bigr] + \frac{1}{2c}\int_{x - ct}^{x + ct} G(s)\,ds ,
>    $$
>    where $F$ and $G$ are the **odd** extensions of $f$ and $g$ to $[-L, L]$, continued with period $2L$. When $f$ and $g$ are smooth enough that $F$ is twice and $G$ once continuously differentiable, $y$ is a solution in the ordinary sense and, by [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]], the only one; for a shape with kinks (a pluck) it is the physically meaningful solution, a superposition of kinked travelling waves.
> 2. The motion is periodic with the fundamental period $\tau_1 = 2L/c = 2\pi/\omega_1$, and half a period later the shape is reflected about the midpoint and inverted: $y(x, t + \tau_1/2) = -y(L - x, t)$.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.4, eqs. for the general solution and the coefficients · PHY 287, week 14 Fourier slides · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, §4*

^thm-b4-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Each term solves the problem*, by [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]], and the wave equation is linear, so finite sums do too ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-6|Theorem §A2.2.6]]). At $t = 0$ the series gives $\sum A_n\sin k_nx$ and $\sum B_n\omega_n\sin k_nx$; these are the sine series of $f$ and $g$ exactly when $A_n$ and $B_n$ are as stated ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-7|Theorem §B4.1.7]]), and they converge uniformly because $f$, $g$ are continuous, piecewise smooth and zero at the ends.
>
> *Equality with d'Alembert's form*, which also settles convergence for all $t$. By the product-to-sum identity, $\sin(k x)\cos(ck t) = \tfrac12\bigl[\sin k(x - ct) + \sin k(x + ct)\bigr]$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{\sin(kx)\sin(ckt)}{ck} = \frac{1}{2c}\int_{x - ct}^{x + ct}\sin(ks)\,ds .
> $$
>
> The sine series of $f$ converges on the whole line to its odd $2L$-periodic extension $F$ (the sines are odd and $2L$-periodic), and likewise for $g$ and $G$. Summing term by term gives d'Alembert's form. That form solves the wave equation whenever $F$ is twice and $G$ once differentiable ([[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution]]). It vanishes at $x = 0$ because $F$ and $G$ are odd, and at $x = L$ because $F(L + ct) = F(ct - L) = -F(L - ct)$, and similarly for $G$.
>
> *Periodicity.* $\omega_n\tau_1 = 2n\pi$. After half a period, $\cos(\omega_nt + n\pi) = (-1)^n\cos\omega_nt$ (and the same for the sine), while $\sin k_n(L - x) = -(-1)^n\sin k_nx$; comparing term by term gives $y(x, t + \tau_1/2) = -y(L - x, t)$.

^der-b4-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-6|Theorem §A2.2.6]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-7|Theorem §B4.1.7]], [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two bases for one set of motions
> The mode sum and d'Alembert's form are the same motion written in two bases ([[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution]]). The mode basis makes the frequencies and the spectrum visible; the travelling-wave basis makes the shape visible: the initial shape splits into two half-height copies that run apart at $\pm c$ and are reflected, inverted, at the fixed ends ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-3|Theorem §A2.5.3]]). Expanding the initial state in the modes is the continuum version of decomposing the initial displacements of coupled oscillators into normal coordinates ([[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange]]): each coefficient pair $(A_n, B_n)$ is the initial state of one independent oscillator of frequency $\omega_n$.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.4–9.5*

## Energy in the modes

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.4: Energy Is Shared Out Mode by Mode
> For the motion of [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]],
>
> $$
> E = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}E_n, \qquad E_n = \frac{\mu L}{4}\,\omega_n^2\bigl(A_n^2 + B_n^2\bigr),
> $$
>
> and each $E_n$ is constant in time. There are no cross terms: the modes do not exchange energy, and the energy in a mode is that of a single oscillator of mass $\mu L/2$ and amplitude $\sqrt{A_n^2 + B_n^2}$.
>
> *Source: added here (the sources give the energy densities, Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10, and the orthogonality, Series I §9.4)*

^thm-b4-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $q_n(t) = A_n\cos\omega_nt + B_n\sin\omega_nt$, so $y_t = \sum\dot q_n\sin k_nx$ and $y_x = \sum k_nq_n\cos k_nx$. These are a sine and a cosine series on $[0, L]$; by Parseval's identity for them ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]] for the odd and even extensions), equivalently by orthogonality with $\int_0^L\sin^2 = \int_0^L\cos^2 = L/2$,
>
> $$
> \int_0^L y_t^2\,dx = \frac L2\sum_n\dot q_n^2, \qquad \int_0^L y_x^2\,dx = \frac L2\sum_nk_n^2q_n^2 .
> $$
>
> With $Tk_n^2 = \mu c^2k_n^2 = \mu\omega_n^2$,
>
> $$
> E = \frac{\mu L}{4}\sum_n\bigl(\dot q_n^2 + \omega_n^2q_n^2\bigr) = \frac{\mu L}{4}\sum_n\omega_n^2\bigl(A_n^2 + B_n^2\bigr),
> $$
>
> since $\dot q_n^2 + \omega_n^2q_n^2 = \omega_n^2\bigl[(-A_n\sin\omega_nt + B_n\cos\omega_nt)^2 + (A_n\cos\omega_nt + B_n\sin\omega_nt)^2\bigr] = \omega_n^2(A_n^2 + B_n^2)$. Each bracket $\tfrac{\mu L}{4}(\dot q_n^2 + \omega_n^2q_n^2)$ is the energy of an oscillator of mass $\mu L/2$ ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion]]).

^der-b4-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]], [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]], [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]]

## The plucked string

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.2.5: The Plucked String
> A string pulled aside at $x = d$ to a height $h$ (two straight segments) and released from rest moves as
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\cos\frac{n\pi ct}{L}, \qquad A_n = \frac{2h}{\pi^2n^2}\,\frac{L^2}{d(L - d)}\,\sin\frac{n\pi d}{L} .
> $$
>
> - The amplitudes fall as $1/n^2$: the initial shape is continuous with one kink ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]).
> - Every mode with a node at the plucking point, $nd/L$ an integer, is absent.
> - The energy fractions are
>   $$
>   \frac{E_n}{E} = \frac{2\sin^2(n\pi d/L)}{\pi^2n^2\,(d/L)(1 - d/L)} ,
>   $$
>   which for a pluck in the middle ($d = L/2$) is $8/\pi^2n^2$ for odd $n$: $81\,\%$ in the fundamental.
> - The shape stays piecewise straight: two kinks run back and forth at speed $c$ (d'Alembert's form of [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Series I §9.4 ("the Fourier coefficients of the pluck shape are the timbre") · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, §4 · PHY 287, week 14 Fourier slides (coefficients for this shape computed here)*

^thm-b4-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The shape is $f(x) = hx/d$ for $x \le d$ and $h(L - x)/(L - d)$ for $x \ge d$, and $g = 0$ gives $B_n = 0$. Integrate by parts twice, using $f(0) = f(L) = 0$ and $\sin k_nL = 0$:
>
> $$
> \int_0^L f\sin k_nx\,dx = \frac{1}{k_n}\int_0^L f'\cos k_nx\,dx = \frac{1}{k_n}\Bigl[\frac hd\int_0^d\cos k_nx\,dx - \frac{h}{L - d}\int_d^L\cos k_nx\,dx\Bigr] = \frac{h\sin k_nd}{k_n^2}\Bigl(\frac1d + \frac{1}{L - d}\Bigr) = \frac{hL\,\sin k_nd}{k_n^2\,d(L - d)} .
> $$
>
> Multiplying by $2/L$ with $k_n = n\pi/L$ gives $A_n$. *Energies:* by [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-4|Theorem §B4.2.4]], $E_n = \tfrac{\mu L}{4}\omega_n^2A_n^2 = \dfrac{T h^2L^3\sin^2(n\pi d/L)}{\pi^2n^2d^2(L - d)^2}$. The total is the stretching energy of the initial shape, $E = \tfrac12T\int_0^L f'^2\,dx = \tfrac12Th^2\bigl[\tfrac1d + \tfrac1{L - d}\bigr] = \dfrac{Th^2L}{2d(L - d)}$; the ratio is the stated fraction. (Summing the $E_n$ instead reproduces $E$, via $\sum_n\sin^2(n\pi a)/n^2 = \pi^2a(1 - a)/2$, which is Parseval's identity for this shape.) For $d = L/2$, $\sin^2(n\pi/2) = 1$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$, and $(d/L)(1 - d/L) = \tfrac14$.

> [!caution] Caution: "Pulling up one end"
> Schwartz's lecture and the PHY 287 slides model a pluck by the shape $y(x, 0) = Hx/L$, the string pulled up at one end. That shape does not vanish at $x = L$, so it is not an admissible initial shape for a string with two fixed ends. Its sine series, $\sum (-1)^{n+1}\tfrac{2H}{n\pi}\sin(n\pi x/L)$ (PHY 287), converges to $0$ at $x = L$, with a Gibbs overshoot next to it ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-8|Theorem §B4.1.8]]), and its coefficients fall only as $1/n$. Read it as the limit $d \to L$ of [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]]: for $n \ll L/(L - d)$, $\sin(n\pi d/L) \approx (-1)^{n+1}n\pi(L - d)/L$, so $A_n \approx (-1)^{n+1}2h/(n\pi)$. A pluck very close to an end has a $1/n$ spectrum over the low harmonics, which is the regime Schwartz uses in his music lecture, and the $1/n^2$ fall-off sets in only above $n \sim L/(L - d)$. (Schwartz also writes the modes as $\sin(2n\pi x/L)$ and $\cos(2n\pi x/L)$, which are those of a periodic string, not of one with fixed ends.)
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, §4 · PHY 287, week 14 Fourier slides*

^cau-b4-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Beyond the ideal string
> A real string is not infinitely thin, is plucked or bowed with a finger or bow of finite width, and sounds through the body of the instrument, which responds more strongly at some frequencies than at others. All of this reshapes the spectrum: the harmonics stay at (nearly) integer multiples of the fundamental, but their relative heights change, and each peak acquires a width set by how fast that mode decays ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^ex-b4-4-1|Example §B4.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §1*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.2.1: A Parabolic Initial Shape
> A string of length $L$ starts from rest with $y(x, 0) = A\,x(L - x)$. Find its motion.
>
> PHY 287 suggests starting from $\sum\bigl[A_n\sin(n\pi x/L) + B_n\cos(n\pi x/L)\bigr]\cos(\omega_nt + \varphi_n)$; the fixed end at $x = 0$ kills every cosine, and starting from rest sets $\varphi_n = 0$, leaving [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]] with $B_n = 0$. Integrating by parts twice (the boundary terms vanish because $x(L - x)$ is zero at both ends),
>
> $$
> \int_0^L x(L - x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = \frac{2L^3}{n^3\pi^3}\bigl[1 - (-1)^n\bigr], \qquad A_n = \frac{2A}{L}\cdot\frac{4L^3}{n^3\pi^3} = \frac{8AL^2}{\pi^3n^3}\quad (n \text{ odd}),
> $$
>
> and $A_n = 0$ for even $n$, so
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = \frac{8AL^2}{\pi^3}\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac{1}{n^3}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\cos\frac{n\pi ct}{L} .
> $$
>
> The even modes are absent because they are antisymmetric about the midpoint while the shape is symmetric, so their overlap integrals vanish. The coefficients fall as $1/n^3$: the odd extension of the parabola is continuous with a continuous slope, and only its curvature jumps ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-5|Theorem §B4.1.5]]). The energy fractions go as $n^2A_n^2 \propto 1/n^4$; with $\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}n^{-4} = \pi^4/96$ the fundamental holds $96/\pi^4 = 98.6\,\%$ of the energy. The motion is almost a pure fundamental, much purer than the triangular pluck ($81\,\%$) of the same symmetry: a smooth shape makes a nearly pure tone.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Final practice, problem 7*

^ex-b4-2-1

> [!example] Example §B4.2.2: Where to Pluck a Guitar String
> The guitar string of [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^ex-a2-6-2|Example §A2.6.2]] ($L = 60\ \mathrm{cm}$, $f_1 = 323\ \mathrm{Hz}$) is plucked one third of the way along, $d = L/3$. By [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-5|Theorem §B4.2.5]], $\sin(n\pi/3) = 0$ for $n = 3, 6, 9, \ldots$: the harmonics at $969\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $1940\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $2910\ \mathrm{Hz}$, … are missing. For the other modes $\lvert\sin(n\pi/3)\rvert = \sqrt3/2$, so $\lvert A_n\rvert/A_1 = 1/n^2$ and $E_n/E_1 = 1/n^2$; with $(d/L)(1 - d/L) = 2/9$ the energy fractions are
>
> $$
> \frac{E_1}{E} = 68\,\%, \qquad \frac{E_2}{E} = 17\,\%, \qquad \frac{E_4}{E} = 4.3\,\%, \qquad \frac{E_5}{E} = 2.7\,\%, \ \ldots
> $$
>
> Compare the other ways of using the same point. *Touching* the string lightly at $L/3$ forces a node there and leaves *only* $n = 3, 6, 9, \ldots$ ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^ex-a2-6-2|Example §A2.6.2]]); *plucking* there removes exactly those modes. Plucking in the middle gives $81\,\%$ in the fundamental and no even harmonics; plucking near the bridge, $d = L/10$, gives only $E_1/E = 21.5\,\%$, with $E_2/E = 19\,\%$, $E_3/E = 16\,\%$ and a long tail. That is why guitarists play near the bridge for a bright, thin sound and near the middle of the string for a round one.
>
> *Source: string data from PHY 300, Midterm 1 practice; the plucking-point analysis is added here*

^ex-b4-2-2

> [!example] Example §B4.2.3: The Struck String
> A string at rest in its equilibrium position is struck by a hammer of width $w$ centred at $x = d$, which gives that stretch the velocity $v_0$: $f = 0$, $g = v_0$ on $\lvert x - d\rvert < w/2$ and $0$ elsewhere. By [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]], $A_n = 0$ and
>
> $$
> B_n = \frac{2v_0}{L\omega_n}\int_{d - w/2}^{d + w/2}\sin k_nx\,dx = \frac{4v_0}{L\,\omega_nk_n}\,\sin k_nd\,\sin\frac{k_nw}{2} \ \approx\ \frac{2v_0w}{L\,\omega_n}\,\sin\frac{n\pi d}{L} \qquad (k_nw \ll 1) .
> $$
>
> The displacement amplitudes fall only as $1/n$, and the mode energies $E_n = \tfrac{\mu L}{4}\omega_n^2B_n^2 \approx \dfrac{\mu v_0^2w^2}{L}\sin^2\dfrac{n\pi d}{L}$ do not fall at all until $n \sim L/w$, where the factor $\sin(k_nw/2)$ cuts them off. A narrow, hard hammer therefore excites many harmonics with comparable energy, a bright sound; a broad, soft one excites fewer. (Check: about $L/w$ modes each with average energy $\tfrac12\mu v_0^2w^2/L$ add up to $\tfrac12\mu v_0^2w$, the kinetic energy given by the hammer.) As for the pluck, modes with a node at $d$ are not excited.
>
> *Source: added here (the initial-velocity formula for $B_n$ is from Series I §9.4)*
