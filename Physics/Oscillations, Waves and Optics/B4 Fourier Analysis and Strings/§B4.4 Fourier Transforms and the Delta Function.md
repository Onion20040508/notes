Fourier series describe periodic signals, whose spectra are lines at the harmonics ([[§B4.3 Harmonics, Timbre and Musical Scales|§B4.3]]). A sound that starts and stops, a pulse on a string, or the ringing of a damped oscillator after a blow ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]) is not periodic, and its spectrum is continuous. The Fourier transform is the limit of the series as the period goes to infinity. This section fixes its convention, derives the rules and the Dirac $\delta$ function, and proves the duration–bandwidth trade-off that level A and quantum mechanics quote ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-5|QM Theorem §A3.3.5]]). It ends with the use that matters most for oscillators: a linear system acts on each frequency separately, so it is a filter, and its response to any force is built from its response to a single kick.

## The transform pair

> [!definition] Definition §B4.4.1: Fourier Transform
> The **Fourier transform** of a function $f(t)$ and its inverse are, in these notes,
>
> $$
> \tilde f(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt, \qquad f(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde f(\omega)\,e^{+i\omega t}\,d\omega .
> $$
>
> The factor $1/2\pi$ sits in the forward transform, and $f$ is synthesised from $e^{+i\omega t}$: the component at $\omega > 0$ has the time dependence $e^{+i\omega t}$ used throughout level B ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]]), and $\tilde f$ is the continuum version of the coefficients $c_n$ of [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^def-b4-1-2|Def. §B4.1.2]]. A function of position is transformed the same way, with $x$ for $t$ and the wave number $k$ for $\omega$: $\tilde f(k) = \frac1{2\pi}\int f(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx$, $f(x) = \int\tilde f(k)e^{+ikx}\,dk$. In two dimensions, $\tilde f(k_x, k_y) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^2}\iint f(x, y)\,e^{-i(k_xx + k_yy)}\,dx\,dy$. The **spectrum** usually plotted is $\lvert\tilde f(\omega)\rvert^2$; $\tilde f$ itself is complex, with magnitude and phase.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §2, eqs. (10)–(11)*

^def-b4-4-1

> [!caution] Caution: Conventions
> There is no universal convention. Other texts put $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ in both directions, or the $1/2\pi$ in the inverse, or the opposite sign in the exponent; quantum mechanics, with time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$, attaches $e^{-i\omega t}$ to positive frequencies. All conventions describe the same physics, but factors of $2\pi$ and signs must be converted before formulas are combined. In particular, a wave travelling toward $+x$ written as $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat A\,e^{i(\omega t - kx)}\bigr]$ has the spatial factor $e^{-ikx}$, which the spatial transform above assigns to the wave number $-k$; a transform in $x$ and $t$ together is therefore best written with $e^{i(\omega t - kx)}$ in the synthesis.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §2*

^cau-b4-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.4.1: Fourier Inversion
> If $f$ is absolutely integrable, $\int\lvert f\rvert\,dt < \infty$, then $\tilde f$ is a bounded continuous function, and if $f$ is moreover piecewise smooth,
>
> $$
> \lim_{W\to\infty}\int_{-W}^{W}\tilde f(\omega)\,e^{i\omega t}\,d\omega = \tfrac12\bigl[f(t^+) + f(t^-)\bigr]
> $$
>
> at every $t$: the inverse transform returns $f$, with the midpoint at jumps, as for Fourier series. For square-integrable $f$ the pair is inverse in the mean-square sense (Plancherel; see [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-4|Theorem §B4.4.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §2 (the limit $L \to \infty$ of the series); conditions added here*

^thm-b4-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Idea, as the limit of Fourier series.* Let $f$ vanish outside $\lvert t\rvert < T_0$ and repeat it with a period $T > 2T_0$. Its complex coefficients ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^def-b4-1-2|Def. §B4.1.2]]) are, with $\omega_n = 2\pi n/T$ and spacing $\Delta\omega = 2\pi/T$,
>
> $$
> c_n = \frac1T\int_{-T/2}^{T/2} f(t)\,e^{-i\omega_nt}\,dt = \Delta\omega\;\tilde f(\omega_n), \qquad f(t) = \sum_n c_ne^{i\omega_nt} = \sum_n\tilde f(\omega_n)\,e^{i\omega_nt}\,\Delta\omega .
> $$
>
> As $T \to \infty$ the lines of the spectrum crowd together and the sum becomes the Riemann sum of $\int\tilde f(\omega)e^{i\omega t}\,d\omega$. Pointwise convergence then follows from [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]. The rigorous proof for functions that do not vanish outside a bounded interval (a Dirichlet-kernel argument as for series, or Gaussian regularisation) is not given here: Mathematical Methods, planned.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.4.2: Rules for the Transform; the Gaussian
> Let $f \mapsto \tilde f$ denote the transform of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|Def. §B4.4.1]].
> - *Linearity:* $af + bg \mapsto a\tilde f + b\tilde g$.
> - *Reality:* $f$ real $\Longleftrightarrow$ $\tilde f(-\omega) = \tilde f(\omega)^*$; then $\lvert\tilde f\rvert$ is even, and the negative frequencies carry no information beyond the positive ones.
> - *Time shift:* $f(t - t_0) \mapsto e^{-i\omega t_0}\tilde f(\omega)$; a delay changes only the phase.
> - *Frequency shift (modulation):* $e^{i\omega_ct}f(t) \mapsto \tilde f(\omega - \omega_c)$.
> - *Scaling:* $f(at) \mapsto \frac{1}{\lvert a\rvert}\tilde f(\omega/a)$; compressing in time stretches in frequency.
> - *Derivative:* $f'(t) \mapsto i\omega\,\tilde f(\omega)$, if $f \to 0$ at $\pm\infty$.
> - *Gaussian:* $e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2} \mapsto \dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\sigma^2\omega^2/2}$, a Gaussian of width $1/\sigma$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §4 (real functions), Lecture 11, eqs. (2), (3), (7) (Gaussian, shifts)*

^thm-b4-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Linearity is that of the integral. *Reality:* conjugating $\tilde f(\omega) = \frac1{2\pi}\int fe^{-i\omega t}dt$ for real $f$ gives $\frac1{2\pi}\int fe^{+i\omega t}dt = \tilde f(-\omega)$; conversely, if $\tilde f(-\omega) = \tilde f(\omega)^*$, the inverse integral is its own conjugate. *Shift:* substitute $s = t - t_0$: $\frac1{2\pi}\int f(s)e^{-i\omega(s + t_0)}ds = e^{-i\omega t_0}\tilde f(\omega)$. *Modulation:* $e^{i\omega_ct}e^{-i\omega t} = e^{-i(\omega - \omega_c)t}$. *Scaling:* substitute $s = at$ (for $a < 0$ the limits swap, hence $\lvert a\rvert$). *Derivative:* integrate by parts; the boundary term vanishes and $-\frac{d}{dt}e^{-i\omega t} = i\omega e^{-i\omega t}$.
>
> *Gaussian.* Let $g(t) = e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}$ and $I(\omega) = \int g(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt$. Since $-it\,g = i\sigma^2g'$, differentiating under the integral and integrating by parts gives
>
> $$
> I'(\omega) = \int(-it)\,g\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt = i\sigma^2\int g'\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt = i\sigma^2\,(i\omega)\,I(\omega) = -\sigma^2\omega\,I(\omega),
> $$
>
> so $I(\omega) = I(0)\,e^{-\sigma^2\omega^2/2}$, with $I(0) = \int e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}dt = \sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma$. Divide by $2\pi$.

## The delta function

> [!definition] Definition §B4.4.2: Dirac Delta Function; Step Function
> The **Dirac delta function** $\delta(t)$ is defined by what it does under an integral: for every continuous function $\varphi$,
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t - t_0)\,\varphi(t)\,dt = \varphi(t_0) .
> $$
>
> No ordinary function does this; $\delta$ is a **distribution**, a rule that turns functions into numbers, and every statement about it is a statement about such integrals. It is pictured as an infinitely tall spike of unit area at $t = 0$ and realised as the limit $\varepsilon \to 0$ of **nascent delta functions** of unit area and width $\varepsilon$, such as
>
> $$
> \delta_\varepsilon(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\varepsilon}\,e^{-t^2/2\varepsilon^2} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \delta_\varepsilon(t) = \frac1\pi\,\frac{\varepsilon}{t^2 + \varepsilon^2} .
> $$
>
> The **unit step** is $\theta(t) = 1$ for $t > 0$ and $0$ for $t < 0$. The $\delta$ function is the continuum version of the Kronecker $\delta_{nm}$ of [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-1|Theorem §B4.1.1]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §6, eqs. (21)–(27), (33)*

^def-b4-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.4.3: Properties of the Delta Function
> 1. *Locality:* $\int_a^b\delta(t)\,dt = 1$ if $a < 0 < b$ and $0$ if $0$ is outside $[a, b]$; in this sense $\delta(t) = 0$ for $t \ne 0$.
> 2. *Symmetry and scaling:* $\delta(-t) = \delta(t)$, $\delta(at) = \delta(t)/\lvert a\rvert$, and $t\,\delta(t) = 0$.
> 3. *Derivative of the step:* $\theta'(t) = \delta(t)$.
> 4. *Fourier representation:*
>    $$
>    \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\omega t}\,d\omega = 2\pi\,\delta(t), \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\omega t}\,dt = \delta(\omega) .
>    $$
> 5. *Transforms:* $\delta(t - t_0) \mapsto e^{-i\omega t_0}/2\pi$; the constant $1 \mapsto \delta(\omega)$; $e^{i\omega_0t} \mapsto \delta(\omega - \omega_0)$; $\cos\omega_0t \mapsto \tfrac12\bigl[\delta(\omega - \omega_0) + \delta(\omega + \omega_0)\bigr]$.
>
> A pure tone of infinite duration has all its spectrum at one frequency; an infinitely sharp click has a flat spectrum, all frequencies equally (white noise).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §6*

^thm-b4-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each statement is an identity between integrals against a continuous, rapidly decaying $\varphi$, checked with the Gaussian $\delta_\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon \to 0$.
> 1. $\int_a^b\delta_\varepsilon\,dt = \int_{a/\varepsilon}^{b/\varepsilon}\frac{e^{-u^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}du \to 1$ or $0$. The sifting property itself: $\int\delta_\varepsilon(t)\varphi(t)\,dt = \int\frac{e^{-u^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\varphi(\varepsilon u)\,du \to \varphi(0)$.
> 2. Substitute $s = at$ in $\int\delta(at)\varphi(t)\,dt$: it becomes $\frac{1}{\lvert a\rvert}\int\delta(s)\varphi(s/a)\,ds = \varphi(0)/\lvert a\rvert$; $a = -1$ gives the symmetry. And $\int t\,\delta(t)\varphi\,dt = 0\cdot\varphi(0)$.
> 3. Integrate by parts: $\int\theta'\varphi\,dt = -\int\theta\varphi'\,dt = -\int_0^\infty\varphi'\,dt = \varphi(0)$.
> 4. Insert a convergence factor: by the Gaussian rule of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]] with the roles of $t$ and $\omega$ exchanged and $\sigma = 1/\varepsilon$,
>    $$
>    \int e^{i\omega t}\,e^{-\varepsilon^2\omega^2/2}\,d\omega = \frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{\varepsilon}\,e^{-t^2/2\varepsilon^2} = 2\pi\,\delta_\varepsilon(t) \ \longrightarrow\ 2\pi\,\delta(t) .
>    $$
> 5. $\frac1{2\pi}\int\delta(t - t_0)e^{-i\omega t}dt = e^{-i\omega t_0}/2\pi$ by sifting; $\frac1{2\pi}\int e^{i\omega_0t}e^{-i\omega t}dt = \delta(\omega - \omega_0)$ by 4; $\cos\omega_0t = \tfrac12(e^{i\omega_0t} + e^{-i\omega_0t})$.
>
> Consistency with inversion: $\int\tilde f(\omega)e^{i\omega t}d\omega = \int f(s)\,\bigl[\tfrac1{2\pi}\int e^{i\omega(t - s)}d\omega\bigr]ds = \int f(s)\,\delta(t - s)\,ds = f(t)$.

^der-b4-4-3

*Uses:* [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|Def. §B4.4.2]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-3|451 Thm. §34.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.4.4: Parseval–Plancherel Identity
> For square-integrable $f$ and $g$,
>
> $$
> \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert f(t)\rvert^2\,dt = 2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lvert\tilde f(\omega)\rvert^2\,d\omega, \qquad \int f\,g^*\,dt = 2\pi\int\tilde f\,\tilde g^*\,d\omega .
> $$
>
> The total "energy" $\int\lvert f\rvert^2dt$ of a signal is distributed over frequency with density $2\pi\lvert\tilde f(\omega)\rvert^2$; this is why $\lvert\tilde f\rvert^2$ is the spectrum of power (or energy) that a spectrum analyser displays. It is the continuum version of Parseval's identity for series ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §4 ("the energy in a frequency mode only depends on the amplitude"); identity derived here*

^thm-b4-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert the inverse transforms and use the Fourier representation of $\delta$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-3|Theorem §B4.4.3]], part 4):
>
> $$
> \int f\,g^*\,dt = \iint\tilde f(\omega)\,\tilde g(\omega')^*\Bigl[\int e^{i(\omega - \omega')t}\,dt\Bigr]d\omega\,d\omega' = 2\pi\iint\tilde f(\omega)\,\tilde g(\omega')^*\,\delta(\omega - \omega')\,d\omega\,d\omega' = 2\pi\int\tilde f\,\tilde g^*\,d\omega .
> $$
>
> Take $g = f$ for the first form. (The exchange of the integrals is formal here; the rigorous version is Plancherel's theorem, Mathematical Methods, planned.)

## Duration and bandwidth

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.4.5: Duration–Bandwidth Relations
> Measure the spread of a signal by the root-mean-square widths $\Delta t$ of $\lvert f(t)\rvert^2$ and $\Delta\omega$ of $\lvert\tilde f(\omega)\rvert^2$ (standard deviations of these as distributions).
> 1. **Gaussian pulse.** For $f(t) = e^{-(t - t_0)^2/2\sigma^2}\,e^{i\omega_ct}$,
>    $$
>    \lvert\tilde f(\omega)\rvert = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{-\sigma^2(\omega - \omega_c)^2/2}, \qquad \Delta t = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt2}, \quad \Delta\omega = \frac{1}{\sqrt2\,\sigma}, \qquad \Delta t\,\Delta\omega = \frac12 .
>    $$
>    The carrier frequency $\omega_c$ sets where the spectrum sits; the duration alone sets how wide it is.
> 2. **Tone burst.** For $f(t) = e^{i\omega_ct}$ during $\lvert t\rvert < \tau/2$ and $0$ otherwise,
>    $$
>    \tilde f(\omega) = \frac{\tau}{2\pi}\,\frac{\sin\bigl((\omega - \omega_c)\tau/2\bigr)}{(\omega - \omega_c)\tau/2},
>    $$
>    whose central peak reaches from $\omega_c - 2\pi/\tau$ to $\omega_c + 2\pi/\tau$: a half-width of $\Delta\omega = 2\pi/\tau$, or $\Delta f = 1/\tau$.
>
> In every case a signal of duration $\Delta t$ occupies a band $\Delta\omega$ with $\Delta\omega\,\Delta t$ of order $1$; the exact number depends on the shape and on how the widths are defined. (Measured by the Gaussian parameters of the amplitudes, $\sigma_t$ and $\sigma_\omega = 1/\sigma_t$, the product is $1$, as in [[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^thm-b6-1-1|Theorem §B6.1.1]]; the rms widths of the intensities are smaller by $\sqrt2$ each.)
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11, eq. (7) and §2 (Gaussian packets; "a band of width $\sigma_\omega = 1/\sigma_t$ to construct pulses of width $\sigma_t$") · PHY 300, Worksheet 11 (the same integral for a slit); widths computed here*

^thm-b4-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) By the Gaussian rule, the time shift and the modulation rule of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]], $\tilde f(\omega) = e^{-i(\omega - \omega_c)t_0}\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\sigma^2(\omega - \omega_c)^2/2}$. Now $\lvert f\rvert^2 = e^{-(t - t_0)^2/\sigma^2}$ is a Gaussian $e^{-(t - t_0)^2/2s^2}$ with $s = \sigma/\sqrt2$, whose standard deviation is $s$. Likewise $\lvert\tilde f\rvert^2 \propto e^{-\sigma^2(\omega - \omega_c)^2}$ has standard deviation $1/(\sqrt2\,\sigma)$.
>
> (2) $\tilde f(\omega) = \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\tau/2}^{\tau/2}e^{-i(\omega - \omega_c)t}\,dt = \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{2\sin\bigl((\omega - \omega_c)\tau/2\bigr)}{\omega - \omega_c}$, which equals the stated form. Its first zeros are where $(\omega - \omega_c)\tau/2 = \pm\pi$.

^der-b4-4-5

*Uses:* [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The general inequality, and the quantum case
> - For every square-integrable signal the rms widths obey $\Delta t\,\Delta\omega \ge \tfrac12$, with equality only for Gaussian pulses (up to a shift and a carrier). The proof uses Plancherel and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and is not given here (Mathematical Methods, planned). The tone burst shows why the inequality is phrased with rms widths: its $\lvert\tilde f\rvert^2$ falls only as $1/\omega^2$, so its rms $\Delta\omega$ is infinite, while the width of its central peak is $2\pi/\tau$.
> - This theorem is the Fourier-analysis content of the "classical uncertainty relations" $\Delta k\,\Delta x \gtrsim 1$, $\Delta\omega\,\Delta t \gtrsim 1$ quoted in quantum mechanics ([[§A3.3 Wave Packets#^thm-a3-3-5|QM Theorem §A3.3.5]]). With $p = \hbar k$ and the de Broglie wave function as $f(x)$ it becomes the Heisenberg relation $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2$, with equality for a Gaussian packet ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-1|QM Theorem §A3.5.1]]); with $E = \hbar\omega$ it becomes the energy–time relation ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-3|QM Theorem §A3.5.3]]). Nothing quantum enters the mathematics.
> - In communication, pulses of width $\Delta t$ separated by about $\Delta t$ need a band $\Delta\omega \sim 1/\Delta t$ around the carrier: the bandwidth limits the rate at which information can be sent. Wave packets built this way, and how they move: [[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11, §2*

## Linear systems as filters

> [!definition] Definition §B4.4.3: Linear Filter; Transfer Function
> A **linear time-invariant system** turns an input signal $F(t)$ into an output $x(t)$ linearly and in the same way at all times. It responds to each exponential input $e^{i\omega t}$ with the output $H(\omega)\,e^{i\omega t}$; the complex function $H(\omega)$ is its **transfer function**. Such a system is a **filter**: a **low-pass** filter passes frequencies below some $\omega_f$ ($H \approx 1$) and blocks those above ($H \approx 0$), a **high-pass** filter does the opposite, and a **band-pass** filter passes only a band. For the damped driven oscillator, with input the force and output the displacement, $H(\omega) = \chi(\omega)/m = 1/\bigl[m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + i\gamma\omega)\bigr]$, with $\chi$ the complex response function of [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^def-b1-3-1|Def. §B1.3.1]]. For displacement it is a low-pass filter with a resonant peak near $\omega_0$, of width about $\gamma = b/m$ and about $Q$ times the low-frequency gain $1/m\omega_0^2$; with the velocity as output, $i\omega H(\omega)$, it is a band-pass filter whose power passband has full width exactly $\gamma$ around $\omega_0$ ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §5 (filters); transfer-function form added here*

^def-b4-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.4.6: Response of a Damped Oscillator to Any Force
> Let $m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = F(t)$ with $\gamma = b/m > 0$ and a force with a Fourier transform. The solution that is at rest before the force acts is given equivalently by
>
> $$
> \tilde x(\omega) = \frac{\chi(\omega)}{m}\,\tilde F(\omega) \qquad\text{and}\qquad x(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G(t - t')\,F(t')\,dt',
> $$
>
> where the **impulse response** (Green's function) of the underdamped oscillator, the motion after a unit kick $F = \delta(t)$, is
>
> $$
> G(t) = \theta(t)\,\frac{e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin\omega_dt}{m\,\omega_d}, \qquad \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4} .
> $$
>
> Each frequency component of the force is amplified and phase-shifted by $\chi(\omega)/m$ on its own; in time, the response is the sum of the ringings left by all the past kicks $F(t')\,dt'$. $G$ vanishes for $t < 0$: there is no response before the cause.
>
> *Source: added here (Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8 transforms the free decay; the driven response by components is that of Lecture 2)*

^thm-b4-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Frequency domain.* Write $F(t) = \int\tilde F(\omega)e^{i\omega t}d\omega$, a superposition of exponential forces. The steady response to $e^{i\omega t}$ is $\frac{\chi(\omega)}{m}e^{i\omega t}$: substituting $x = \hat xe^{i\omega t}$ turns the equation into $m(-\omega^2 + i\gamma\omega + \omega_0^2)\hat x = 1$ ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]). The equation is linear, so the response to the superposition is $\int\frac{\chi(\omega)}{m}\tilde F(\omega)e^{i\omega t}d\omega$. Any other solution differs from it by a free motion ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]]), which grows without bound into the past for $\gamma > 0$; so this is the solution at rest before the force acts.
>
> *A single kick.* For $F = P\,\delta(t)$, integrate the equation from $-\varepsilon$ to $\varepsilon$: $m[\dot x]_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon} + b[x]_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon} + k\int_{-\varepsilon}^{\varepsilon}x\,dt = P$. The displacement is continuous, so as $\varepsilon \to 0$ only the first term survives: the kick changes the velocity by $P/m$ and leaves the position at $0$. Afterwards the oscillator moves freely; the underdamped motion with $x(0) = 0$ and $\dot x(0^+) = P/m$ is $x = \frac{P}{m\omega_d}e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin\omega_dt$ ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]). With $P = 1$ this is $G$.
>
> *Any force* is a superposition of kicks, $F(t) = \int F(t')\,\delta(t - t')\,dt'$ ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-2|Def. §B4.4.2]]). By linearity and time invariance the kick $F(t')\,dt'$ at time $t'$ contributes $G(t - t')F(t')\,dt'$, and the contributions add. The two descriptions agree because both give the unique solution at rest before the force acts; in particular $\tilde G(\omega) = \chi(\omega)/2\pi m$, the transform of the response to $\delta(t)$, whose transform is $1/2\pi$.

^der-b4-4-6

> [!remark] Remark: Filtering images
> A grey-scale picture is a function $f(x, y)$, and its two-dimensional transform $\tilde f(k_x, k_y)$ has a magnitude and a phase. For ordinary photographs the magnitude is concentrated at small $k$, the large-scale variations, while the phase looks random. Yet the phase carries the structure: recombining the magnitude of one picture with the phase of another gives an image that looks like the second. A **high-pass** filter, which removes small $k$, keeps the edges and fine detail; a **low-pass** filter keeps the smooth variations and blurs the rest (and smooths noise). Adding the high-pass version of one face to the low-pass version of another gives a hybrid that shows the first face close up and the second from a distance, because the eye resolves fine detail only at short range.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §4–5, Figs. 3–8*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.4.1: The Spectrum of a Ringing Oscillator
> An oscillator set ringing at $t = 0$ and left alone has the displacement $f(t) = \theta(t)\,e^{-\gamma t/2}\cos\omega_dt$, with energy decaying as $e^{-\gamma t}$, i.e. with lifetime $\tau = 1/\gamma$ ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]). Writing the cosine as two exponentials,
>
> $$
> \tilde f(\omega) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-\gamma t/2}\bigl(e^{i\omega_dt} + e^{-i\omega_dt}\bigr)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt = \frac{1}{4\pi}\Bigl[\frac{1}{\gamma/2 + i(\omega - \omega_d)} + \frac{1}{\gamma/2 + i(\omega + \omega_d)}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> For light damping, $\gamma \ll \omega_d$, the second term is negligible near $\omega = \omega_d$, and the spectrum is a **Lorentzian**
>
> $$
> \lvert\tilde f(\omega)\rvert^2 \approx \frac{1}{16\pi^2}\,\frac{1}{(\omega - \omega_d)^2 + \gamma^2/4},
> $$
>
> which falls to half its peak at $\omega - \omega_d = \pm\gamma/2$: its full width at half maximum is $\gamma$ ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^def-b1-4-1|Def. §B1.4.1]]). So
>
> $$
> \Delta\omega_{\text{FWHM}}\cdot\tau = 1 .
> $$
>
> This is the bandwidth $\Delta\omega = \gamma$ of the driven oscillator's power curve ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-3|Theorem §A1.6.3]], [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]]). The level-A remark that a sharp resonance rings long ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^rem-a1-6-3|Remark: A sharp resonance rings long]]) is now a theorem of Fourier analysis: the free ringing and the resonance curve are a transform pair (near resonance; exactly, the transform of the ringing $G$ left by a kick is $\chi/2\pi m$, [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-6|Theorem §B4.4.6]]). The peaks in the spectrum of a violin note have widths of this kind, and each width measures how fast that harmonic decays.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §1 and §3, eqs. (12)–(15), Fig. 2 (written here with $\gamma$ the energy decay rate; the lecture's exponent is $e^{-\gamma t}$)*

> [!example] Example §B4.4.2: How Long Must a Note Last?
> A violinist plays A4 ($440\ \mathrm{Hz}$) as a short note of $\tau = 50\ \mathrm{ms}$, roughly a tone burst. By [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-5|Theorem §B4.4.5]] (2) the central peak of its spectrum extends $\Delta f = 1/\tau = 20\ \mathrm{Hz}$ to either side of $440\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The equal-tempered semitone above A4 is $440\,(2^{1/12} - 1) = 26\ \mathrm{Hz}$ away ([[§B4.3 Harmonics, Timbre and Musical Scales#^def-b4-3-3|Def. §B4.3.3]]), so the pitch of so short a note is defined only to within almost a semitone, and a mistuning of a few hertz cannot be heard in it. A note of $0.5\ \mathrm{s}$ has $\Delta f = 2\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and its tuning can be judged, for example by the beats of [[§B4.3 Harmonics, Timbre and Musical Scales#^ex-b4-3-1|Example §B4.3.1]], which need a note long enough to contain several beat periods. Conversely, a click a millisecond long spreads over a kilohertz and has no pitch at all.
>
> *Source: added here, applying Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11, §2*
