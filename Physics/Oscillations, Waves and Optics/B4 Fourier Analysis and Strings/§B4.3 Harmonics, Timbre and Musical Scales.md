Level A described timbre as the mix of overtones that distinguishes instruments ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]], [[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^rem-a3-3-6|Remark: What makes an instrument sound like itself]]). It also explained consonance by beats between harmonics and quoted the equal-tempered scale ([[§A3.4 Beats#^rem-a3-4-2|Remark: Beats and consonance]]). With Fourier series in hand these become quantitative. A periodic note *is* its spectrum, and the power splits harmonic by harmonic. Which harmonics two notes share follows from the ratio of their frequencies. And no tuning can make all the consonant ratios exact at once: the three standard compromises, and the arithmetic that forces them, are the subject of this section.

## The spectrum of a note

> [!definition] Definition §B4.3.1: Spectrum of a Periodic Sound; Pitch, Timbre and Intensity
> A steady musical note is a periodic pressure signal $\Delta p(t)$ of period $T = 2\pi/\omega_1$. Its **spectrum** is the set of its complex Fourier coefficients $c_n$ ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^def-b4-1-2|Def. §B4.1.2]]), or the amplitudes $P_n = 2\lvert c_n\rvert$ of its harmonics $n\omega_1$ ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-4|Theorem §B4.1.4]]). In these terms:
> - the **pitch** is set by the fundamental frequency $\omega_1/2\pi$, which is usually the lowest peak and the strongest one (if it is not the strongest, the pitch heard can be ambiguous);
> - the **timbre** is the pattern of relative heights $P_n/P_1$, and, for real notes, the widths of the peaks;
> - the **intensity** is the overall scale.
>
> This makes [[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]] quantitative: two notes of the same pitch and loudness differ in timbre exactly when their relative spectra differ.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7, §2 · Series I §9.4 ("the Fourier coefficients of the pluck shape are the timbre")*

^def-b4-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.3.1: The Intensity Is the Sum of the Harmonic Intensities
> A plane sound wave travelling in one direction, with an arbitrary periodic pressure signal $\Delta p(t) = \sum_{n \ne 0}c_ne^{in\omega_1t}$ at a fixed point, carries the intensity
>
> $$
> I = \frac{\overline{\Delta p^{\,2}}}{\rho v} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} I_n, \qquad I_n = \frac{P_n^2}{2\rho v},
> $$
>
> where the bar is the time average, $P_n = 2\lvert c_n\rvert$, and $I_n$ is the intensity that the $n$th harmonic would carry alone ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]). The relative phases of the harmonics change the waveform but not the intensity, and not the power in any harmonic.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 8, §4 ("the energy in a frequency mode only depends on the amplitude"; the spectrum displayed is $\lvert c_n\rvert^2$)*

^thm-b4-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For a wave $s = F(x - vt)$ moving toward $+x$, the velocity of the medium is $u = \partial s/\partial t = -vF'$ and the pressure fluctuation is $\Delta p = -B\,\partial s/\partial x = -BF'$ ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]]). With $B = \rho v^2$ ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]]), $\Delta p = \rho v\,u$ for any waveform. The power per unit area through a fixed plane is $\Delta p\,u = \Delta p^{\,2}/\rho v$ (the ambient pressure does no net work over a period, as in the derivation of [[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]). Its time average over a period is, by Parseval's identity ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]) with $c_0 = 0$,
>
> $$
> \overline{\Delta p^{\,2}} = \sum_{n \ne 0}\lvert c_n\rvert^2 = 2\sum_{n \ge 1}\lvert c_n\rvert^2 = \sum_{n \ge 1}\tfrac12P_n^2 ,
> $$
>
> using $\lvert c_{-n}\rvert = \lvert c_n\rvert$ for a real signal. The phases $\arg c_n$ do not appear.

^der-b4-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Reading spectra
> - A pure tone sounds pleasant but plain; a square wave of the same pitch sounds tinny and harsh, because a fifth of its power sits in high harmonics ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^ex-b4-1-2|Example §B4.1.2]]). At the far end, a signal with all frequencies present at once is white noise, as unmusical as sound gets.
> - Real instruments have many strong harmonics. A flute playing G4 ($392\ \mathrm{Hz}$) shows peaks at all integer multiples of $392\ \mathrm{Hz}$; their relative heights, and how narrow each peak is, are the flute's timbre.
> - The spectrum is harmonic, with lines exactly at $n\omega_1$, for any periodic sound, whatever produced it. Instruments produce periodic sound because their resonators have harmonic normal modes: strings ([[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]]) and air columns ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]], odd harmonics only). A membrane's modes are not harmonic ([[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^rem-b7-2-2|Remark: What one hears and sees]]): a drum thuds rather than sings a pitch.
> - Which harmonics are strong depends on how the resonator is excited: a pluck at a point removes the modes with a node there and falls as $1/n^2$; a hammer blow falls only as $1/n$ in amplitude ([[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^ex-b4-2-3|Example §B4.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7, §1–2 (Fig. 2, flute spectrum)*

^rem-b4-3-1

## Consonance from coinciding harmonics

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.3.2: Coinciding Harmonics of Two Notes
> Let two notes with harmonic spectra have fundamentals $\nu_1 < \nu_2$ in the ratio $\nu_2/\nu_1 = p/q$, with $p$ and $q$ positive integers without a common factor. Then harmonic $a$ of the lower note coincides with harmonic $b$ of the upper note exactly when
>
> $$
> (a, b) = j\,(p, q), \qquad j = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
> $$
>
> The lowest shared frequency is $p\nu_1 = q\nu_2$: the $p$th harmonic of the lower note is the $q$th of the upper. If the ratio is slightly off, $\nu_2 = (p/q)\,\nu_1(1 + \varepsilon)$ with $\lvert\varepsilon\rvert \ll 1$, these pairs no longer coincide but beat ([[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]) at the frequencies
>
> $$
> f_{\text{beat}} = j\,p\,\nu_1\,\lvert\varepsilon\rvert = j\,\lvert q\nu_2 - p\nu_1\rvert, \qquad j = 1, 2, \ldots
> $$
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7, §2*

^thm-b4-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Harmonic $a$ of the lower note is at $a\nu_1$ and harmonic $b$ of the upper at $b\nu_2 = bp\nu_1/q$. They coincide when $aq = bp$. Since $p$ and $q$ have no common factor, $p$ divides $a$ (unique prime factorisation), say $a = jp$, and then $b = jq$; conversely every such pair coincides. With the mistuning, harmonic $jq$ of the upper note is at $jq\nu_2 = jp\nu_1(1 + \varepsilon)$, which differs from harmonic $jp$ of the lower note by $jp\nu_1\lvert\varepsilon\rvert$.

^der-b4-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]], [[§B4.3 Harmonics, Timbre and Musical Scales#^def-b4-3-1|Def. §B4.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why small ratios sound best
> The harmonics of real notes weaken with $n$ and become messy at high $n$. A ratio with small $p$ and $q$ makes strong, low harmonics coincide, and leaves few near-misses among the low harmonics to beat. For a ratio like $17/11$ the first coincidence is the 17th harmonic of the lower note, where there is little amplitude left, while near-coincidences among lower harmonics produce audible beats long before. In practice intervals with $p$ or $q$ larger than about $5$ are no longer heard as consonant. A slightly mistuned consonance is heard through its beats, at $p\nu_1\lvert\varepsilon\rvert$, slow enough to be counted when the mistuning is small: this is how instruments are tuned by ear ([[§A3.4 Beats#^rem-a3-4-1|Remark: When beats are heard, and tuning]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7, §2*

^rem-b4-3-2

## Intervals and scales

> [!definition] Definition §B4.3.2: Interval; Consonant Intervals
> The **interval** between two notes is the ratio of their fundamental frequencies; intervals compose by multiplication, so they add on a logarithmic scale. The **octave** is the ratio $2$. The consonant intervals of Western music, and their small-integer ratios, are:
>
> | interval | octave | fifth | fourth | major third | minor third | major sixth | minor sixth |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | ratio | $2$ | $3/2$ | $4/3$ | $5/4$ | $6/5$ | $5/3$ | $8/5$ |
> | above C4 | C5 | G4 | F4 | E4 | E♭4 | A4 | A♭4 |
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7, Table 1 (octave labels of the sixths corrected)*

^def-b4-3-2

> [!definition] Definition §B4.3.3: Just, Pythagorean and Equal-Tempered Tuning
> A **tuning** assigns frequencies to the notes of the scale relative to a starting note, the **key** (here C, frequency $\nu_0$).
> - **Just intonation** gives each note the simplest ratio to the key note, so that it is consonant with it.
> - **Pythagorean tuning** builds every note from perfect fifths and octaves: ratios $3^a2^b$ with integers $a, b$ (for example D $= (3/2)^2/2 = 9/8$, A $= (3/2)^3/2 = 27/16$).
> - **Equal temperament** divides the octave into 12 equal semitones, each the ratio $2^{1/12} = 1.0595$; a note $s$ semitones above the key has the ratio $2^{s/12}$. It refers to no key.
>
> | note | C | D | E | F | G | A | B | C |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | just | $1$ | $9/8 = 1.125$ | $5/4 = 1.250$ | $4/3 = 1.333$ | $3/2 = 1.500$ | $5/3 = 1.667$ | $15/8 = 1.875$ | $2$ |
> | Pythagorean | $1$ | $9/8 = 1.125$ | $81/64 = 1.266$ | $4/3 = 1.333$ | $3/2 = 1.500$ | $27/16 = 1.688$ | $243/128 = 1.898$ | $2$ |
> | equal-tempered | $1$ | $2^{2/12} = 1.122$ | $2^{4/12} = 1.260$ | $2^{5/12} = 1.335$ | $2^{7/12} = 1.498$ | $2^{9/12} = 1.682$ | $2^{11/12} = 1.888$ | $2$ |
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7, §3, Tables 2–6*

^def-b4-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B4.3.3: The Circle of Fifths Does Not Close
> 1. No number of perfect fifths equals a whole number of octaves: $(3/2)^a = 2^b$ has no solution in positive integers.
> 2. Twelve fifths come closest among small numbers: they exceed seven octaves by the **Pythagorean comma**
>    $$
>    \frac{(3/2)^{12}}{2^7} = \frac{3^{12}}{2^{19}} = \frac{531441}{524288} = 1.0136 ,
>    $$
>    about $1.4\,\%$, a quarter of a semitone.
> 3. Equal temperament closes the circle by making each fifth $2^{7/12} = 1.49831$, $0.11\,\%$ flat of $3/2$ (the fourth $2^{5/12}$ is $0.11\,\%$ sharp of $4/3$), at the price of a major third $2^{4/12} = 1.25992$ that is $0.79\,\%$ sharp of $5/4$ and a major sixth $0.91\,\%$ sharp of $5/3$.
> 4. The Pythagorean major third $81/64$ exceeds the just one by the factor $81/80 = 1.0125$; the same factor separates the Pythagorean sixth $27/16$ from the just $5/3$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7, §3.2–3.4 and Fig. 5 (percentages computed here)*

^thm-b4-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $(3/2)^a = 2^b$ means $3^a = 2^{a + b}$, an odd number equal to an even one for $a \ge 1$, which is impossible (equivalently, unique prime factorisation). (2) $3^{12} = 531441$ and $2^{19} = 524288$; their ratio is $1.01364$, and $2^{1/12} = 1.0595$, so the excess is $\ln 1.01364/\ln 1.0595 = 0.23$ of a semitone. (3) $2^{7/12} = 1.498307$, and $1.498307/1.5 = 0.99887$; $2^{5/12}/(4/3) = 1.334840/1.333333 = 1.00113$; $2^{4/12}/(5/4) = 1.259921/1.25 = 1.00794$; $2^{9/12}/(5/3) = 1.681793/1.666667 = 1.00908$. (4) $(81/64)/(5/4) = 81/80$ and $(27/16)/(5/3) = 81/80$.

^der-b4-3-3

*Uses:* [[§B4.3 Harmonics, Timbre and Musical Scales#^def-b4-3-3|Def. §B4.3.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why a keyboard cannot be just in every key
> Tune a piano justly in C. The fifth above D should then be $\tfrac32 \times \tfrac98 = 27/16 = 1.688$ times C, but the A of the C scale is $5/3 = 1.667$: the D-major chord is out of tune by $81/80$, and every key brings its own new notes. Instruments with continuously variable pitch (violin, voice, and woodwinds adjusted with the lips) can play just intervals in any key. Instruments with fixed notes (piano, fretted guitar) must compromise. Pythagorean tuning keeps every fifth and fourth exact but makes the thirds rough and leaves one fifth off by the comma. Equal temperament spreads the comma evenly: every interval is the same in every key, the fifths and fourths are almost exact, and the thirds and sixths are noticeably off. It is the standard tuning of Western music.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7, §3.1–3.3*

^rem-b4-3-3

> [!caution] Caution: Counting harmonics and octaves
> Schwartz's music lecture calls $2\nu_0$ the "first harmonic"; these notes call $n\nu_0$ the $n$th harmonic ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^cau-a3-3-1|Caution: Counting harmonics]]). In the note names, the digit counts octaves starting at C: C4 is middle C ($261.6\ \mathrm{Hz}$ in equal temperament with A4 $= 440\ \mathrm{Hz}$; Schwartz rounds to $261\ \mathrm{Hz}$), and A4 is the A *above* C4.

^cau-b4-3-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B4.3.1: Tempered Fifths Barely Beat, Tempered Thirds Rattle
> Take C4 $= 261\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and play its fifth and its major third in equal temperament. By [[§B4.3 Harmonics, Timbre and Musical Scales#^thm-b4-3-2|Theorem §B4.3.2]] the lowest pair of harmonics that should coincide is the 3rd of C with the 2nd of G for the fifth ($p/q = 3/2$), and the 5th of C with the 4th of E for the third ($5/4$).
> - *Fifth.* G4 $= 261 \times 2^{7/12} = 391.06\ \mathrm{Hz}$. Harmonics: $3 \times 261 = 783.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$ against $2 \times 391.06 = 782.1\ \mathrm{Hz}$, a beat of $0.9\ \mathrm{Hz}$: one slow swell per second, hardly noticed.
> - *Major third.* E4 $= 261 \times 2^{4/12} = 328.8\ \mathrm{Hz}$. Harmonics: $5 \times 261 = 1305\ \mathrm{Hz}$ against $4 \times 328.8 = 1315.4\ \mathrm{Hz}$, a beat of $10.4\ \mathrm{Hz}$, an audible roughness. The next coinciding pair ($j = 2$) beats at $21\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
> - *Pythagorean third.* E4 $= 261 \times 81/64 = 330.3\ \mathrm{Hz}$ gives $4 \times 330.3 - 1305 = 16.3\ \mathrm{Hz}$, rougher still; the just third ($326.25\ \mathrm{Hz}$) does not beat at all.
>
> This is the content of [[§B4.3 Harmonics, Timbre and Musical Scales#^thm-b4-3-3|Theorem §B4.3.3]] (3) in audible terms: equal temperament sacrifices the thirds to keep the fifths.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7, §2–3 (frequencies; the beat rates are computed here)*

^ex-b4-3-1

> [!example] Example §B4.3.2: Where the Frets Go
> A string's fundamental is inversely proportional to its vibrating length ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]]). On the $60\ \mathrm{cm}$ guitar string of [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^ex-b4-2-2|Example §B4.2.2]], the fret that raises the pitch by $s$ equal-tempered semitones leaves the length $L\,2^{-s/12}$, so it sits at
>
> $$
> x_s = L\bigl(1 - 2^{-s/12}\bigr)
> $$
>
> from the nut. The 12th fret (octave) is at $30.00\ \mathrm{cm}$, exactly half way. The 7th fret (fifth) is at $19.95\ \mathrm{cm}$, against $20.00\ \mathrm{cm}$ for a just fifth (length $2L/3$). The 5th fret (fourth) is at $15.05\ \mathrm{cm}$, against $15.00\ \mathrm{cm}$. The 4th fret (major third) is at $12.38\ \mathrm{cm}$, against $12.00\ \mathrm{cm}$ for a just third (length $4L/5$): almost $4\ \mathrm{mm}$ away. The fret spacing shrinks by the factor $2^{-1/12}$ from one fret to the next. A Pythagorean guitar would place its frets at lengths $2^b/3^a$ of the string instead.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7, §3.2 and Fig. 3 (fret positions computed here)*
