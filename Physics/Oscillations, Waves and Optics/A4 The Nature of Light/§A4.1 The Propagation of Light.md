Light is an electromagnetic wave ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves|EM §A12.2]]): in vacuum it travels at $c$, whatever its frequency. This section asks how fast it goes in matter, which one number per material, the index of refraction, answers, and when the wave can be replaced by straight lines, the rays of geometric optics. Both ideas carry the rest of the chapter: the laws of reflection and refraction ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction|§A4.2]]) are statements about rays, and the index sets how much they bend.

**Setup.** Light is a classical electromagnetic wave of one frequency, in vacuum or in a uniform, transparent, isotropic medium at rest, described by its index $n$ at the light's wavelength. The measured constants are laws with their own *Valid when* lines. Rays replace the wave when every aperture and obstacle is many wavelengths across ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^mod-a4-1-4|Model §A4.1.4]]); smaller ones need wave optics (Chapters A6–A7).

## The speed of light

> [!law] Law §A4.1.1: The Speed of Light in Vacuum
> Light travels in vacuum at
>
> $$
> c = 2.99792458 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> The approximate value is used when three digits suffice. Measurements from Roemer's (1675) to Michelson's, who by 1926 found $(2.99796 \pm 0.00004) \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$, converged on this value ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^rem-a4-1-1|Remark: The speed of light in vacuum]]). Since 1983 it is exact by definition: the metre is the distance light travels in vacuum in $1/299\,792\,458\ \mathrm s$ ([[§A1.1 Units, Standards and Conversion#^rem-a1-1-4|CM §A1.1, Remark: The three mechanical standards]]).
>
> *Valid when:* light in vacuum, of every wavelength (no dispersion in vacuum, [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]]); in matter light is slower ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]). The value agrees with Maxwell's prediction $1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$ ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|EM Theorem §A12.2.4]]); that every inertial observer measures the same value is a principle of Relativity ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-2|REL Principle §A1.3.2]]), not part of this law.
> *Source: UP3 §1.1, eq. (1.1) · UP1 §1.2*

^law-a4-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: The speed of light in vacuum
> The measured value of [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^law-a4-1-1|Law §A4.1.1]] is the value predicted by Maxwell's equations, $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$ ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|EM Theorem §A12.2.4]]). It is one of the fundamental constants: observers moving fast relative to each other all measure the same value, the starting point of [[Relativity]] ([[§A1.3 Einstein's Postulates and Simultaneity#^pr-a1-3-2|REL Principle §A1.3.2]]).
>
> The measurements that led to it:
> - **Roemer (1675)**: the interval between eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io (period $42.5\ \mathrm h$) is a few seconds longer while Earth recedes from Jupiter than while it approaches, because the light carrying each eclipse has farther (or less far) to go. His estimate, $2.0 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$, was 33 % low.
> - **Fizeau (1849)**: a rapidly rotating toothed wheel chopped a beam sent to a mirror $8\ \mathrm{km}$ away. At the right rotation rate, the returning pulse met a tooth instead of a gap: during the round trip $2L/c$ the wheel turned by $(n + \tfrac12)$ tooth spacings. Result $3.15 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$, 5 % high.
> - **Foucault (1862)** replaced the wheel by a rotating mirror: $2.98 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$, within 0.6 %. **Michelson** refined the method until 1926: $(2.99796 \pm 0.00004) \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

^rem-a4-1-1

## The index of refraction

> [!definition] Definition §A4.1.1: Index of Refraction
> The **index of refraction** of a transparent material is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed $v$ in the material:
>
> $$
> n = \frac{c}{v}, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad v = \frac{c}{n} .
> $$
>
> Measured values, and the bound $n \geq 1$, are in [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^law-a4-1-2|Law §A4.1.2]].
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.1, eq. (1.2)*

^def-a4-1-1

> [!law] Law §A4.1.2: Indices of Refraction of Common Media
> Light in matter is never faster than $c$, so $n \geq 1$, with $n = 1$ exactly only in vacuum. The index depends slightly on the wavelength ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]]), so tables quote it at one wavelength, usually $589\ \mathrm{nm}$:
>
> | Medium | $n$ | Medium | $n$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | air ($0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, 1 atm) | 1.000293 | crown glass | 1.52 |
> | water ($20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$) | 1.333 | flint glass | 1.66 |
> | ice ($0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$) | 1.309 | polystyrene | 1.49 |
> | ethanol | 1.361 | zircon | 1.923 |
> | fused quartz | 1.458 | diamond | 2.419 |
>
> For gases $n$ is so close to 1 (the molecules are far apart, and between them light travels at $c$) that one takes $n = 1$ unless great precision is needed.
>
> *Valid when:* visible light ($589\ \mathrm{nm}$ in the table) in transparent media; gases at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ and $1\ \mathrm{atm}$, liquids and solids at $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ unless stated. The bound $n \geq 1$ holds below a material's resonances: for X-rays every material has an index slightly below 1 ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^rem-b13-1-2|§B13.1, Remark: An index below one]]).
> *Source: UP3 §1.1, Table 1.1*

^law-a4-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Why light is slower in matter
> The wave's electric field drives the charges of the material, mostly the light electrons bound in the atoms, and the driven charges radiate. The light that travels on is the superposition of the incoming wave and this re-radiated light, and the re-radiated part lags in phase. Adding them layer by layer gives a wave whose crests advance more slowly than $c$: in the medium, light *appears* to move at $c/n$, while every piece of field still propagates at $c$ between the atoms. Working this out with bound electrons treated as driven oscillators ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance|§A1.6]]) also predicts how $n$ depends on frequency ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^rem-a4-3-3|Remark: Where dispersion comes from]]); the calculation is [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-7|Theorem §B13.1.7]], and the frequency dependence [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-4|Theorem §B13.1.4]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16*

^rem-a4-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.1.3: Frequency and Wavelength in a Medium
> When light passes from one medium into another, its frequency $f$ does not change; its wavelength does. In a medium of index $n$ the wavelength is
>
> $$
> \lambda_n = \frac{\lambda_0}{n},
> $$
>
> where $\lambda_0 = c/f$ is the wavelength of the same light in vacuum. For two media, $n_1\lambda_1 = n_2\lambda_2$.
>
> *Model:* light of one frequency crossing between uniform transparent media at rest; *applies:* exact; $\lambda_0$ is the vacuum wavelength and $n$ is taken at that wavelength.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14, eq. (28)*

^thm-a4-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At the boundary the wave in the second medium is driven by the wave arriving in the first, so the fields on the two sides oscillate together, at the same rate. Equivalently, crests cannot pile up at the boundary or vanish there: as many crests leave per second as arrive. So $f$ is the same on both sides. In each medium a sinusoidal wave advances one wavelength per period, $v = f\lambda$ ([[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|Theorem §A2.1.1]]), so with $v = c/n$,
>
> $$
> \lambda_n = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{c}{nf} = \frac{\lambda_0}{n} .
> $$
>
> Multiplying by $n$ gives $n\lambda_n = \lambda_0$, the same for every medium, hence $n_1\lambda_1 = n_2\lambda_2$.

^der-a4-1-3

> [!example] Example §A4.1.1: Light in Zircon
> Yellow light of vacuum wavelength $\lambda_0 = 589\ \mathrm{nm}$ enters zircon, $n = 1.923$, a gemstone used to imitate diamond. Its speed, wavelength and frequency inside are
>
> $$
> v = \frac{c}{n} = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}}{1.923} = 1.56 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad \lambda_n = \frac{589\ \mathrm{nm}}{1.923} = 306\ \mathrm{nm}, \qquad f = \frac{c}{\lambda_0} = 5.09 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz} .
> $$
>
> The speed is a little over half of $c$, still enormous by everyday standards; of the solids in the table of [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^law-a4-1-2|Law §A4.1.2]] only diamond slows light more. The wavelength inside, $306\ \mathrm{nm}$, would be ultraviolet in vacuum, but the light is no different: the frequency is unchanged, and on leaving the stone it is yellow light of $589\ \mathrm{nm}$ again.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 1.1*

## The ray model

> [!definition] Definition §A4.1.2: Ray; Geometric and Wave Optics
> A **ray** is a straight line that starts at a point, drawn along the direction in which light travels. **Geometric optics** is the part of optics in which light is described by rays and their paths found by geometry and trigonometry; **wave optics** is the part that needs the wave character of light.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.1 and chapter glossary*

^def-a4-1-2

> [!model] Model §A4.1.4: The Ray Model of Light
> Light travels from a source to an observer along rays:
> - in a uniform medium, or in vacuum, a ray is a straight line;
> - a ray changes direction only where the medium changes: it is reflected at a surface ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-1|Law §A4.2.1]]) or refracted on entering another medium ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|Law §A4.2.4]]), and then continues straight.
>
> So light can reach an observer in three ways: directly through empty space (sunlight reaching the upper atmosphere), through one or more media (air and glass), or after reflection (a mirror).
>
> - *Assumes:* every object the light meets (apertures, obstacles, lenses, mirrors) is many times larger than the wavelength. Visible light has $\lambda < 1\ \mu\mathrm m$, so anything visible to the unaided eye, such as a coin, qualifies.
> - *Fails:* for openings and obstacles comparable to the wavelength, where light spreads behind edges (diffraction, [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^def-a4-4-2|Def. §A4.4.2]]) and overlapping beams interfere (Chapters A6–A7). Polarization ([[§A4.5 Polarization|§A4.5]]) is also outside the model.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.1*

^mod-a4-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: Rays and waves
> A ray is not a thin beam of light that one could isolate: narrowing a beam by passing it through a slit eventually makes it spread (Chapter A7). A ray is the direction of travel of the wave, perpendicular to its wave fronts ([[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]]). Picturing it as a laser beam is fine as long as the model's assumption holds.
