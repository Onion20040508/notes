In the ray model ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^mod-a4-1-2|Model §A4.1.2]]) a ray travels straight until it meets a surface. Two empirical laws say what happens there: the law of reflection for the light that bounces back, and Snell's law for the light that crosses into the second medium. This section states both as laws, as they were found; [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle|§A4.4]] derives them from the wave picture, as theorems (why the same statements appear in both layers: [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^rem-a4-4-3|§A4.4, Remark: What the wave picture adds]]).

## Angles at a surface

> [!definition] Definition §A4.2.1: Angles of Incidence, Reflection and Refraction
> Where a ray meets a surface, draw the **normal**, the line perpendicular to the surface at that point. The **angle of incidence** $\theta_1$ (or $\theta_i$), the **angle of reflection** $\theta_r$ and the **angle of refraction** $\theta_2$ are the angles that the incident, reflected and refracted (transmitted) rays make with the normal. The incident ray and the normal span the **plane of incidence**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.2–1.3*

^def-a4-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Measure from the normal
> All angles in this chapter are measured from the normal, not from the surface. A beam "at $20^\circ$ to the surface of a pond" has $\theta_1 = 70^\circ$.

^cau-a4-2-1

## Reflection

> [!law] Law §A4.2.1: Law of Reflection
> The reflected ray lies in the plane of incidence, on the other side of the normal, and the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence:
>
> $$
> \theta_r = \theta_i .
> $$
>
> *Valid when:* the surface is smooth on the scale of the wavelength of the light, so that a normal is defined over a region many wavelengths across. It holds for every wavelength and for any reflecting surface: mirror, glass, water. It follows from Huygens's principle ([[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^thm-a4-4-2|Theorem §A4.4.2]]) and, at level B, from Maxwell's equations ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]).
> *Source: UP3 §1.2, eq. (1.3)*

^law-a4-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: Mirrors and white paper
> A mirror is smooth compared with the wavelength: parallel incident rays leave parallel, in one direction (**specular** reflection), and only an observer in that direction sees the reflected light. A rough surface, such as paper, cloth, leaves or walls, still obeys the law at each point, but its normal changes from point to point, so parallel rays leave in many directions (**diffuse** reflection). That is why we see paper from every angle, and why the Moon's reflection on a slightly rippled lake is smeared into a band. Seen in a mirror, an object appears behind the mirror at the same distance as it is in front; no light reaches that place (the image is *virtual*), yet a camera records it just as the eye sees it. Image formation by mirrors: [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors|§A5.1]].

^rem-a4-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.2: Reflection in Vector Form
> Let $\hat d$ be the unit vector along an incident ray and $\hat n$ the unit normal of the reflecting surface. The reflected ray points along
>
> $$
> \hat d_r = \hat d - 2(\hat d\cdot\hat n)\,\hat n :
> $$
>
> the component of the direction along the normal is reversed, the component along the surface is kept.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.2, eq. (1.3) (restated)*

^thm-a4-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Split $\hat d = (\hat d\cdot\hat n)\hat n + \hat d_\parallel$ into its parts along the normal and along the surface. By the law of reflection, the reflected ray lies in the plane spanned by $\hat d$ and $\hat n$, on the other side of the normal, at the same angle to it. A unit vector in that plane making the angle $\theta_i$ with the normal line, on the far side of it, and pointing away from the surface, is $-(\hat d\cdot\hat n)\hat n + \hat d_\parallel$: its normal component has the same size as before ($\cos\theta_i$) but the opposite sign, and its surface component is unchanged ($\sin\theta_i$, same direction). This equals $\hat d - 2(\hat d\cdot\hat n)\hat n$.

^der-a4-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-1|Law §A4.2.1]], [[§A1.5 Products of Vectors#^def-a1-5-1|CM Def. §A1.5.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.3: Corner Reflector
> A ray that reflects once from each of two mutually perpendicular mirrors, travelling in a plane perpendicular to both, leaves antiparallel to the direction it came from, whatever its angle of incidence. With three mutually perpendicular mirrors (the corner of a cube) the same holds for a ray in any direction that strikes all three.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.2*

^thm-a4-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put the mirrors in the coordinate planes, so that their normals are $\hat\imath$, $\hat\jmath$ (and $\hat k$). By [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]], a reflection from the mirror with normal $\hat\jmath$ reverses only the $y$-component of the ray's direction, and so on. A direction $(-a, -b)$ becomes $(-a, +b)$ after one mirror and $(+a, +b)$ after the other: the reverse of the original. The order of the reflections does not matter, and in three dimensions the third mirror reverses the third component, giving $-\hat d$. (University Physics calls the three-mirror geometry "much more complex"; in components it is no harder than two.)

^der-a4-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^thm-a4-2-2|Theorem §A4.2.2]]

![[ph-wo-a4-2-2.svg]]
*A corner reflector. The incoming ray (blue) has direction $(-a, -b)$; mirror 1 reverses its vertical component, giving $(-a, +b)$, and mirror 2 reverses its horizontal component, so the outgoing ray (red) has direction $(+a, +b)$, parallel to the incoming ray and opposite to it. Dashed grey: the normals.*

> [!remark] Remark: Retroreflectors
> Corner reflectors are retroreflectors: they send light back toward its source, so drivers see a bicycle reflector or road sign lit by their own headlights. The Apollo astronauts left a three-mirror reflector on the Moon; timing laser pulses bounced off it shows the Moon receding by a few centimetres per year. Radar reflectors on small boats work the same way for radio waves, and a stealth aircraft avoids $90^\circ$ corners for the same reason in reverse. Prisms using total internal reflection make perfect corner reflectors: [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^ex-a4-3-2|Example §A4.3.2]].

## Refraction

> [!definition] Definition §A4.2.2: Refraction
> **Refraction** is the change of direction of a ray when it passes from one medium into another with a different index of refraction ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]). The ray in the second medium is the **refracted ray**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.3*

^def-a4-2-2

> [!law] Law §A4.2.4: Law of Refraction (Snell's Law)
> The refracted ray lies in the plane of incidence, on the other side of the normal, and
>
> $$
> n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2 ,
> $$
>
> where $n_1$, $n_2$ are the indices of the first and second medium and $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$ the angles of incidence and refraction.
>
> *Valid when:* both media are transparent, uniform and isotropic (not birefringent crystals such as calcite, [[§A4.5 Polarization#^rem-a4-5-7|Remark: Polarization in materials]]), the interface is smooth on the scale of the wavelength, and $n_1$, $n_2$ are taken at the wavelength of the light ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]]). It holds for either polarization. Derived from Huygens's principle in [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]; from Maxwell's equations in [[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]].
> *Source: UP3 §1.3, eq. (1.4)*

^law-a4-2-4

![[ph-wo-a4-2-1.svg]]
*Reflection and refraction at an air–glass surface, drawn to scale. The incident ray (blue) arrives at $\theta_1 = 50^\circ$; part of the light is reflected (pale blue) at $\theta_r = 50^\circ$, the rest is refracted (red) toward the normal, at $\theta_2 = 30.7^\circ$ from $\sin\theta_2 = \sin 50^\circ/1.50$. All three rays and the dashed normal lie in one plane.*

> [!remark] Remark: What the law says, and what it does not
> - Snell (1621) found it from measured angles, without knowing that light changes speed. His discovery was that a single number $n$ can be assigned to each medium and then works for every pair: once $n_{\text{water}} = 1.33$ is fixed from air–water measurements, it also gives the bending from water into glass. That $n = c/v$ was understood later, and is what [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]] uses.
> - Snell's law gives the *direction* of the transmitted light, not how much light is transmitted. Some light is always reflected as well (about 4 % for air to glass at normal incidence); how much depends on the angle and the polarization (Fresnel equations, [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]]; see also [[§A4.5 Polarization#^law-a4-5-4|Brewster's law]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 15 (reflected fraction at normal incidence, $\bigl((n_1 - n_2)/(n_1 + n_2)\bigr)^2 = 0.04$ for $n_2/n_1 = 1.5$)*

^rem-a4-2-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.2.5: Direction of Bending; Reversibility
> 1. A ray entering a medium of higher index ($n_2 > n_1$) bends toward the normal, $\theta_2 < \theta_1$; entering a medium of lower index, it bends away from the normal, $\theta_2 > \theta_1$. A ray along the normal ($\theta_1 = 0$) goes straight through.
> 2. For a given angle of incidence, the farther the ratio $n_2/n_1$ is from 1 (the larger the change of speed), the larger the change of direction.
> 3. Light paths are reversible: a ray sent backward along the refracted ray emerges backward along the incident ray.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.3*

^thm-a4-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From Snell's law, $\sin\theta_2 = (n_1/n_2)\sin\theta_1$. On $0 \leq \theta \leq 90^\circ$ the sine is strictly increasing (its derivative, the cosine, is positive). So $n_1/n_2 < 1$ gives $\sin\theta_2 < \sin\theta_1$, hence $\theta_2 < \theta_1$, and the reverse for $n_1/n_2 > 1$; $\theta_1 = 0$ gives $\theta_2 = 0$. For fixed $\theta_1$, $\sin\theta_2$ decreases as $n_2/n_1$ grows: for $n_2/n_1 > 1$, a larger ratio makes $\theta_2$ smaller and the bend $\theta_1 - \theta_2$ larger; for $n_2/n_1 < 1$, a smaller ratio makes $\theta_2$ larger and the bend $\theta_2 - \theta_1$ larger (as long as a refracted ray exists). This proves 2. For 3: Snell's law is symmetric in the two media; a ray arriving from medium 2 at angle $\theta_2$ is refracted to the angle $\theta_1$ that satisfies the same equation, and by the law it leaves on the other side of the normal in the plane of incidence, which is back along the original incident ray.

^der-a4-2-5

*Uses:* [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|Law §A4.2.4]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-7|451 Cor. §29.7]]

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.2.1: Water versus Diamond
> A ray in air ($n_1 = 1.00$) strikes a liquid at $\theta_1 = 30.0^\circ$ and is refracted to $\theta_2 = 22.0^\circ$. Snell's law gives the index of the liquid:
>
> $$
> n_2 = n_1\,\frac{\sin\theta_1}{\sin\theta_2} = \frac{\sin 30.0^\circ}{\sin 22.0^\circ} = \frac{0.500}{0.375} = 1.33 ,
> $$
>
> the index of water. Now let the same ray enter diamond, $n_2 = 2.419$:
>
> $$
> \sin\theta_2 = \frac{n_1}{n_2}\sin\theta_1 = \frac{0.500}{2.419} = 0.207, \qquad \theta_2 = 11.9^\circ .
> $$
>
> The ray is turned by $30.0^\circ - 11.9^\circ = 18.1^\circ$ in diamond against $8.0^\circ$ in water: the larger change of speed causes the larger change of direction ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^thm-a4-2-5|Theorem §A4.2.5]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 Examples 1.2–1.3*

^ex-a4-2-1

> [!example] Example §A4.2.2: Through a Window Pane
> A ray in air strikes a pane of crown glass ($n = 1.52$, thickness $t = 1.00\ \mathrm{cm}$, parallel faces) at $\theta_1 = 40.0^\circ$. At the first face
>
> $$
> \sin\theta_2 = \frac{\sin 40.0^\circ}{1.52} = 0.423, \qquad \theta_2 = 25.0^\circ .
> $$
>
> The faces are parallel, so the ray meets the second face at $25.0^\circ$ from its normal, and Snell's law there, $1.52\sin 25.0^\circ = 1.00\sin\theta_3$, is the first equation reversed: $\theta_3 = 40.0^\circ$. The ray leaves parallel to its original direction, whatever the glass ($n$ cancels; with a third medium behind the glass, $n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_3\sin\theta_3$ as if the glass were absent). It is only shifted sideways. The path inside has length $t/\cos\theta_2$ and makes the angle $\theta_1 - \theta_2$ with the original line, so the shift is
>
> $$
> d = \frac{t\,\sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2)}{\cos\theta_2} = \frac{(1.00\ \mathrm{cm})\sin 15.0^\circ}{\cos 25.0^\circ} = 0.285\ \mathrm{cm} .
> $$
>
> This is why a window does not distort the view: directions are preserved, and a few millimetres of shift are invisible at a distance.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.3 (cf. end-of-chapter Problems 79–80)*

^ex-a4-2-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The vector form of the law of reflection is the reflection across a plane, $\hat d \mapsto \hat d - 2(\hat d\cdot\hat n)\hat n$, built from the orthogonal decomposition of $\hat d$ along $\hat n$ ([[§20 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-13|LADR Thm. 6.13]]).
> - Both laws are derived from Huygens's principle in [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle|§A4.4]], and from Maxwell's boundary conditions, together with the amounts reflected and transmitted, in [[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]], [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]] and [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]].
> - Classical Mechanics level B: Snell's law in continuous form, $n\sin\theta =$ const in a layered medium, from Fermat's principle and the calculus of variations — [[§B5.1 The Euler–Lagrange Equation#^ex-b5-1-2|CM Example §B5.1.2]]; its mechanical twin, a beam of projectiles whose conserved horizontal momentum plays the role of $n\sin\theta$, with $\sqrt{2m(E - U)}$ as refractive index — [[§B8.3 The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation#^ex-b8-3-3|CM Example §B8.3.3]].
