So far light has been treated as rays obeying two empirical laws ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction|§A4.2]]). Huygens's principle is a rule for how a wave front moves forward, and it explains both laws from the single fact that light travels at $c/n$ in a medium. It also shows where the ray picture breaks down: behind small openings the wave front bends (diffraction), which is the door to wave optics (Chapters A6–A7).

## Wave fronts and wavelets

> [!definition] Definition §A4.4.1: Wave Front
> A **wave front** is a surface (in a planar drawing, a line) on which the wave has the same phase at a given instant, for instance a line along a crest. In an isotropic medium the wave travels perpendicular to its wave fronts, and that direction is the ray ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^def-a4-1-2|Def. §A4.1.2]]). For an electromagnetic wave, the fronts are where the electric field has, say, its maximum.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.6*

^def-a4-4-1

> [!principle] Principle §A4.4.1: Huygens's Principle
> Every point on a wave front is a source of wavelets that spread out in the forward direction at the speed of the wave at that point. A time $t$ later the wavelets have radius $s = vt$, and the new wave front is the surface tangent to all of them.
>
> *Domain:* waves of every kind (water, sound, light) in media whose wave speed is known at each point; it locates wave fronts and directions of travel, not amplitudes. The wavelets are taken to go forward only. Not valid as stated for anisotropic media (birefringent crystals), where the wavelets are not spheres.
> *Source: UP3 §1.6*

^pr-a4-4-1

![[ph-wo-a4-4-1.svg]]
*Huygens's construction for a plane wave front (blue). Each marked point emits a forward wavelet (grey semicircle) of radius $s = vt$; the line tangent to them (red) is the front a time $t$ later, parallel to the old one. The rays are perpendicular to the fronts. Near the ends of a front of finite length the wavelets reach around the edges: the seed of diffraction.*

> [!remark] Remark: What kind of statement this is
> - Christiaan Huygens (1629–1695) stated it as a construction; at this level it is assumed and judged by its consequences: it reproduces straight-line propagation, the laws of reflection and refraction (below), and diffraction. The PHY 287 version says the same in causal terms: what happens at $r + \Delta r$ at time $t + \Delta t$ is produced by the disturbance at $r$ at time $t$.
> - The forward-only rule is an extra assumption: wavelets spreading backward would produce a wave travelling the wrong way, which is not seen. Fresnel added that the wavelets carry phases and interfere, which gives intensities and is what Chapters A6–A7 use. Its justification from the wave equation (Kirchhoff, Fresnel) is [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^thm-b11-1-3|Theorem §B11.1.3]], read as a sum of wavelets in [[§B11.1 The Fresnel–Kirchhoff Diffraction Integral#^rem-b11-1-1|§B11.1, Remark: Huygens's principle, now a theorem]]; the rigorous scalar and vector theory is Chapter C1 (planned).
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 14 slides*

## Reflection and refraction from wavelets

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.2: Law of Reflection from Huygens's Principle
> A plane wave reflected by a plane mirror leaves at an angle of reflection equal to the angle of incidence. This is the law of reflection, [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-1|Law §A4.2.1]].
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.6*

^thm-a4-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let the incident front $AB$ touch the mirror at $A$, with $B$ still a distance $BB' = v\,\Delta t$ (measured along the ray) from the mirror point $B'$. During $\Delta t$, while $B$ travels to $B'$, the wavelet emitted at $A$ when the front touched it grows to radius $AA' = v\,\Delta t$, in the same medium, at the same speed. The reflected front is the tangent from $B'$ to this wavelet, touching it at $A'$. Compare the right triangles $ABB'$ (right angle at $B$, since the ray $BB'$ is perpendicular to the front $AB$) and $B'A'A$ (right angle at $A'$, since a tangent is perpendicular to the radius). They share the hypotenuse $AB'$, and $BB' = AA'$. So they are congruent, and the angles they make with the mirror at $A$ and at $B'$ are equal: the incident front and the reflected front make equal angles with the mirror. The angle between a front and the mirror equals the angle between the ray (perpendicular to the front) and the normal (perpendicular to the mirror). Hence $\theta_r = \theta_i$; the reflected ray lies in the plane of incidence because the whole construction does.

^der-a4-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^pr-a4-4-1|Principle §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.4.3: Snell's Law from Huygens's Principle
> A plane wave passing from a medium where its speed is $v_1 = c/n_1$ into one where it is $v_2 = c/n_2$ is refracted so that
>
> $$
> \frac{\sin\theta_1}{v_1} = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{v_2}, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2 .
> $$
>
> This is Snell's law, [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|Law §A4.2.4]], now with the index explained as the ratio $c/v$.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 1.6*

^thm-a4-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The incident front $AB$ reaches the surface at $A$, while $B$ is still in medium 1. In the time $\Delta t$ that $B$ needs to reach the surface at $B'$, it travels $BB' = v_1\,\Delta t$. Meanwhile the wavelet from $A$ spreads into medium 2 at the slower speed and reaches radius $AA' = v_2\,\Delta t$. The refracted front is the tangent $A'B'$ from $B'$ to this wavelet.
>
> The segment $AB'$ on the surface is the hypotenuse of both right triangles $ABB'$ (right angle at $B$) and $AA'B'$ (right angle at $A'$). The angle $\angle BAB'$ between the incident front and the surface equals $\theta_1$, and $\angle AB'A'$ between the refracted front and the surface equals $\theta_2$ (each front is perpendicular to its ray, the surface to the normal). So
>
> $$
> AB' = \frac{BB'}{\sin\theta_1} = \frac{AA'}{\sin\theta_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\sin\theta_1}{v_1\,\Delta t} = \frac{\sin\theta_2}{v_2\,\Delta t} .
> $$
>
> Cancel $\Delta t$ and substitute $v_i = c/n_i$ ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]): $n_1\sin\theta_1/c = n_2\sin\theta_2/c$, which is Snell's law.

^der-a4-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^pr-a4-4-1|Principle §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^def-a4-4-1|Def. §A4.4.1]], [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]

![[ph-wo-a4-4-2.svg]]
*Huygens's construction of refraction, to scale for $\theta_1 = 50^\circ$ and $n_2/n_1 = 1.5$. While the far end $B$ of the incident front (blue) covers $BB' = v_1\Delta t$ (dashed blue), the wavelet from $A$ (grey circle) grows only to $AA' = v_2\Delta t$ (dashed red). The refracted front $A'B'$ (red) is tangent to the wavelet, so it is tilted less steeply against the surface, $\theta_2 = 30.7^\circ$: the ray turns toward the normal.*

> [!remark] Remark: The same result by matching crests
> Draw the wave fronts as crests, spaced $\lambda_1$ in medium 1 and $\lambda_2$ in medium 2. Along the surface, the crests on the two sides must meet at the same points, a distance $R$ apart, or the wave would be torn at the boundary. Each crest spacing is the projection of $R$ onto the ray's direction: $\lambda_1 = R\sin\theta_1$, $\lambda_2 = R\sin\theta_2$. With $n_1\lambda_1 = n_2\lambda_2$ ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]]) this is Snell's law again; for the reflected wave, in the same medium, $\lambda$ is the same, so $\theta_r = \theta_i$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 15*

^rem-a4-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: What the wave picture adds
> - *Laws there, theorems here.* In [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction|§A4.2]] the law of reflection and Snell's law are laws ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-1|Law §A4.2.1]], [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|Law §A4.2.4]]): summaries of measurement, trusted within their *Valid when* range. Here the same statements are theorems ([[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^thm-a4-4-2|Theorem §A4.4.2]], [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^thm-a4-4-3|Theorem §A4.4.3]]), derived from Huygens's principle and $v = c/n$; the empirical input has moved into the principle and the index. The laws keep their boxes and their home in §A4.2, and the theorems link back. At level B both become theorems of Maxwell's equations ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]): a statement's layer depends on what is taken as the starting point.
> - In the form $\sin\theta_1/v_1 = \sin\theta_2/v_2$ the derivation uses nothing specific to light, so refraction is a property of all waves: sound bends toward the region where it travels more slowly. In the ocean and in the atmosphere the speed of sound has a minimum at some depth or height, and sound is bent back toward that layer from both sides and channelled along it, like light in a fibre.
> - It explains *why* a ray bends toward the normal on entering a slower medium: the part of the front that enters first is held back. For $v_2 > v_1$ the wavelet from $A$ outruns $B$, the tangent from $B'$ exists only while $v_2\,\Delta t < AB'$, i.e. $\sin\theta_1 < v_1/v_2$, and beyond that there is no refracted front: total internal reflection ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^thm-a4-3-1|Theorem §A4.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 15*

^rem-a4-4-3

## Diffraction

> [!definition] Definition §A4.4.2: Diffraction
> **Diffraction** is the bending of a wave around the edges of an opening or an obstacle into the region that would be in shadow if the wave travelled in straight lines. It occurs for all types of waves and is strongest when the opening is comparable to the wavelength.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.6*

^def-a4-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Diffraction as evidence of waves
> By Huygens's principle, a plane front cut off by an opening keeps going straight in the middle, but near the edges the wavelets have no neighbours to cancel their sideways spread, and the front curves around. The narrower the opening compared with the wavelength, the larger the fraction of the front that bends. Observing diffraction is therefore evidence of a wave: the spreading of a laser beam behind narrow slits shows that light is a wave. The converse fails: no visible diffraction may only mean that the wavelength is far smaller than the objects involved.

^rem-a4-4-4

> [!example] Example §A4.4.1: Sound and Light through a Doorway
> Light from a doorway makes a sharp-edged patch on the floor of a dark room, while sound from the doorway is heard everywhere in the room. For sound of $1000\ \mathrm{Hz}$ in air ($v = 330\ \mathrm{m/s}$), by [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|Theorem §A2.1.1]],
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{330\ \mathrm{m/s}}{1000\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}} = 0.33\ \mathrm m ,
> $$
>
> about a third of the width of the door, so the sound diffracts strongly. Visible light has $\lambda \approx 5 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm m$, about $10^{-6}$ of the door's width: it diffracts imperceptibly and behaves as rays ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^mod-a4-1-2|Model §A4.1.2]]). To see light bend, the opening must be made comparable to its wavelength: a slit a fraction of a millimetre wide or less (Chapter A7).
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.6*
