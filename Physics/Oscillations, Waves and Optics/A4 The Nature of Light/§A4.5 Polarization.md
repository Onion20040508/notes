Rays and wave fronts say where light goes; they ignore that light is a *transverse* wave, whose electric field points in some direction perpendicular to the direction of travel ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-2|EM Theorem §A12.2.2]]). That direction, the polarization, is invisible to the eye but decides how much light passes a polarizing filter (Malus's law) and how much is reflected from glass or water (Brewster's law). Polarizing sunglasses cut glare because of both.

## Polarized and unpolarized light

> [!definition] Definition §A4.5.1: Polarization
> A transverse wave is **polarized** if its oscillations have a definite direction perpendicular to the direction of propagation. For an electromagnetic wave the **direction of polarization** is the direction of the electric field $\vec E$ (the magnetic field is perpendicular to it, [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-5|EM Theorem §A12.2.5]]). Light from the Sun and from most lamps is **unpolarized**: it is a mixture of many waves whose directions of polarization are random, all directions perpendicular to the ray equally represented. **Vertically** and **horizontally polarized** waves oscillate in a vertical or a horizontal plane.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.7*

^def-a4-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Only transverse waves can be polarized
> Shake a rope up and down and the wave is vertically polarized; a vertical slit around the rope lets it through, but blocks a wave from side-to-side shaking. A longitudinal wave ([[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^def-a2-1-2|Def. §A2.1.2]]) has only one direction of oscillation, along the propagation, so there is nothing to select: sound in air cannot be polarized. Light can, which is one piece of evidence that it is transverse.

^rem-a4-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Linear polarization is one case
> "Polarized" in this chapter means **linearly** polarized: $\vec E$ oscillates back and forth along one line. In general the two perpendicular components of $\vec E$ can oscillate out of phase. With equal amplitudes and a quarter-cycle phase difference the tip of $\vec E$ turns in a circle (**circular polarization**); in general it traces an ellipse. Such states, the plates that convert between them, and the matrix (Jones) calculus for polarizers and plates are in Chapter B8: the general state and its handedness ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-1|Def. §B8.1.1]], [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-2|Def. §B8.1.2]]), the polarization ellipse ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-3|Theorem §B8.1.3]]), and Jones vectors ([[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus#^def-b8-2-1|Def. §B8.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14 · PHY 300, Worksheet 8*

^rem-a4-5-2

## Polarizers and Malus's law

> [!model] Model §A4.5.1: Ideal Polarizer
> An **ideal polarizer** (polarizing filter) has a direction in its plane, its **transmission axis**. Of an incident wave travelling perpendicular to it, it transmits the component of $\vec E$ along the axis completely and absorbs the component perpendicular to the axis completely. What leaves is polarized along the axis.
>
> - *Assumes:* normal incidence; complete transmission along the axis and complete absorption across it, at all wavelengths of interest.
> - *Fails:* real sheets pass less than all of the parallel component and leak a little of the perpendicular one. In the lab report below, a single sheet passed 85 % of the ideal $I_0/2$, and two crossed sheets still let through about 5 % of the light.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.7 · PHY 300, Polarization lab report*

^mod-a4-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: How a polarizing filter works
> Polaroid sheet, invented by Edwin Land, contains long molecules (polymer chains) aligned in one direction. Electrons can move easily along a molecule but hardly across it. The component of $\vec E$ along the molecules drives the electrons, which absorb its energy; the component across the molecules passes almost untouched. So the **transmission axis is perpendicular to the molecules**, not along them, unlike the slit for the rope. Materials that absorb one polarization preferentially are called **dichroic**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.7 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14*

^rem-a4-5-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.2: Malus's Law
> Linearly polarized light of intensity $I_0$ falls on an ideal polarizer whose axis makes the angle $\theta$ with the direction of polarization. The transmitted intensity is
>
> $$
> I = I_0\cos^2\theta ,
> $$
>
> and the transmitted light is polarized along the axis. The rest, $I_0\sin^2\theta$, is absorbed.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.7, eq. (1.6)*

^thm-a4-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let $\hat e_\parallel$ be the unit vector along the axis and $\hat e_\perp$ the one across it, in the plane of the polarizer. The incident field, of amplitude $E_0$, oscillates along $\cos\theta\,\hat e_\parallel + \sin\theta\,\hat e_\perp$:
>
> $$
> \vec E = E_0\cos\theta\,\hat e_\parallel\cos(kx - \omega t) + E_0\sin\theta\,\hat e_\perp\cos(kx - \omega t) .
> $$
>
> By [[§A4.5 Polarization#^mod-a4-5-1|Model §A4.5.1]] only the first term passes: the transmitted wave has amplitude $E_0\cos\theta$, along $\hat e_\parallel$. The intensity of a plane wave is proportional to the square of its amplitude, $I = \tfrac12 c\varepsilon_0E_0^2$ ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]]), so $I/I_0 = (E_0\cos\theta)^2/E_0^2 = \cos^2\theta$. Since $\hat e_\parallel \perp \hat e_\perp$, the squared amplitudes add, $E_0^2 = E_0^2\cos^2\theta + E_0^2\sin^2\theta$, and the absorbed share is $I_0\sin^2\theta$.

^der-a4-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A4.5 Polarization#^mod-a4-5-1|Model §A4.5.1]], [[§A4.5 Polarization#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]], [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]], [[§20 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-13|LADR Thm. 6.13]], [[§20 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-12|LADR Thm. 6.12]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.3: Unpolarized Light through a Polarizer
> Unpolarized light of intensity $I_0$ passing an ideal polarizer emerges polarized along the axis with intensity
>
> $$
> I = \tfrac12 I_0 ,
> $$
>
> whatever the orientation of the polarizer.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.7 (chapter summary) · PHY 300, Polarization lab report*

^thm-a4-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Unpolarized light is a mixture of waves whose polarization directions make all angles $\varphi$ with the axis equally often, without fixed phase relations among them, so their intensities add. By Malus's law each passes with the factor $\cos^2\varphi$, so the transmitted fraction is the average of $\cos^2\varphi$ over all directions. A turn of the polarizer by $90^\circ$ changes $\cos^2\varphi$ into $\sin^2\varphi$ and leaves the uniform mixture the same, so the two averages are equal, and they add to $\langle\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi\rangle = 1$. Hence $\langle\cos^2\varphi\rangle = \tfrac12$. No orientation is preferred, so the result does not depend on the orientation.

^der-a4-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.5 Polarization#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]], [[§A4.5 Polarization#^def-a4-5-1|Def. §A4.5.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Using Malus's law
> - $\theta$ is the angle between the **direction of polarization** of the incoming light and the **axis** of the filter, not an angle to the beam.
> - Malus's law is for polarized light. For unpolarized light the first polarizer passes $\tfrac12 I_0$ (Theorem §A4.5.3), not $I_0\cos^2\theta$.
> - The intensity goes as $\cos^2\theta$, the field amplitude as $\cos\theta$. A filter that halves the intensity reduces $E_0$ (and $B_0$) by the factor $1/\sqrt2 = 0.707$.
> - For a chain of polarizers, apply the law at each sheet, with $\theta$ measured from the axis of the *previous* sheet, since that is the new polarization direction.
> - The missing intensity is absorbed in the sheet and warms it.

^cau-a4-5-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A4.5.1: Cutting the Intensity by 90 %
> At what angle between the polarization direction and the filter axis is polarized light reduced to $10.0\ \%$? From $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$ with $I = 0.100\,I_0$,
>
> $$
> \cos\theta = \sqrt{0.100} = 0.3162, \qquad \theta = 71.6^\circ .
> $$
>
> A large angle is needed: at $45^\circ$ half the light still passes. The law is symmetric about $45^\circ$ in the sense that at $90^\circ - 71.6^\circ = 18.4^\circ$ the transmitted fraction is $\cos^2 18.4^\circ = 0.900$, the complement: in general $\cos^2\theta + \cos^2(90^\circ - \theta) = 1$. Turning the filter the other way, by $-71.6^\circ$, gives the same $10\ \%$, since $\cos^2$ is even.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 1.7*

^ex-a4-5-1

> [!example] Example §A4.5.2: A Third Polarizer between Crossed Ones
> Unpolarized light of intensity $I_0$ meets polarizer 1 (axis at $0^\circ$) and polarizer 3 (axis at $90^\circ$). Crossed, they pass nothing: after polarizer 1 the light has $I_1 = \tfrac12 I_0$ and is polarized at $0^\circ$, and polarizer 3 is at $90^\circ$ to that, $\cos^2 90^\circ = 0$. Now insert polarizer 2 between them, at angle $\theta$. By [[§A4.5 Polarization#^thm-a4-5-2|Theorem §A4.5.2]] at each sheet (polarizer 3 is at $90^\circ - \theta$ from polarizer 2):
>
> $$
> I = I_1\cos^2\theta\,\cos^2(90^\circ - \theta) = I_1\cos^2\theta\,\sin^2\theta = \frac{I_1}{4}\sin^2 2\theta = \frac{I_0}{8}\sin^2 2\theta .
> $$
>
> Adding an absorbing sheet lets light through: up to $I_0/8$, at $\theta = 45^\circ$ (and $135^\circ$), and none at $\theta = 0^\circ, 90^\circ, 180^\circ$, where polarizer 2 is aligned with one of the others. The middle sheet rotates the polarization in two steps of $45^\circ$, each costing only a factor $\tfrac12$, while one step of $90^\circ$ costs everything.
>
> *In the lab.* In the user's PHY 300 polarization experiment, with $I_1 = 135.0\ \mathrm{lux}$ measured after the first sheet, the ideal maximum is $I_1/4 = 33.8\ \mathrm{lux}$. The measured intensity was $0.6$, $2.3$ and $0.5\ \mathrm{lux}$ at $0^\circ$, $90^\circ$, $180^\circ$, and peaked at $24$–$28.5\ \mathrm{lux}$ near $50$–$60^\circ$ and $110$–$120^\circ$: the predicted shape, with peaks $15$–$30\ \%$ below the ideal value because the sheets are not ideal (Model §A4.5.1).
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Polarization lab report · UP3 §1.7*

## Polarization by reflection

> [!remark] Remark: Glare is partly polarized
> Look at light reflected from water or glass through polarizing sunglasses and turn them: the light gets brighter and dimmer but never black. Reflected light is **partially polarized**, with more of its field parallel to the surface (horizontal, for a lake) than across it. The other component, with $\vec E$ in the plane of incidence, is preferentially refracted into the surface. Sunglasses with vertical transmission axes therefore cut reflected glare more than other light.

^rem-a4-5-4

> [!caution] Caution: Naming the two polarizations
> At a reflecting surface, the two natural directions of $\vec E$ are perpendicular to the plane of incidence, i.e. parallel to the surface, and in the plane of incidence. University Physics calls them "horizontal" and "vertical" (for a horizontal surface). PHY 300 calls them **s** or **TE** (transverse electric) and **p** or **TM** (transverse magnetic). Schwartz calls the in-plane one "vertical". So: UP horizontal = s = TE; UP vertical = p = TM.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 9 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 15*

^cau-a4-5-2

> [!law] Law §A4.5.4: Brewster's Law
> Unpolarized light travelling in a medium of index $n_1$ and reflected from the surface of a medium of index $n_2$ is **completely polarized**, with $\vec E$ perpendicular to the plane of incidence (parallel to the surface), when the angle of incidence is **Brewster's angle** $\theta_b$:
>
> $$
> \tan\theta_b = \frac{n_2}{n_1} .
> $$
>
> At other angles the reflected light is only partially polarized.
>
> *Valid when:* a smooth interface between transparent, isotropic, non-magnetic media (magnetic permeability $\mu \approx \mu_0$ on both sides, true of glass and water). University Physics states it without proof; it follows from the Fresnel equations ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]), as the angle at which the reflection coefficient of the in-plane (p) polarization vanishes ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-4|Theorem §B9.2.4]]).
> *Source: UP3 §1.7, eq. (1.7)*

^law-a4-5-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.5.5: Reflected and Refracted Rays Are Perpendicular at Brewster's Angle
> When light strikes a surface at Brewster's angle, the refracted ray is at $\theta_2 = 90^\circ - \theta_b$ from the normal, so the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular to each other.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 15, eq. (43)*

^thm-a4-5-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Brewster's law, $\sin\theta_b/\cos\theta_b = n_2/n_1$, says $n_1\sin\theta_b = n_2\cos\theta_b$. Snell's law at the same angle of incidence says $n_1\sin\theta_b = n_2\sin\theta_2$. So $\sin\theta_2 = \cos\theta_b = \sin(90^\circ - \theta_b)$, and since both angles lie between $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$, where the sine is one-to-one, $\theta_2 = 90^\circ - \theta_b$. The reflected ray makes $\theta_b$ with the normal on the incident side of the surface, the refracted ray $\theta_2$ on the other side, so the angle between them is $180^\circ - \theta_b - \theta_2 = 90^\circ$.

^der-a4-5-5

*Uses:* [[§A4.5 Polarization#^law-a4-5-4|Law §A4.5.4]], [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|Law §A4.2.4]], [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-1|Law §A4.2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the in-plane polarization is not reflected
> The reflected light is radiated by the electrons at the surface, which the refracted wave sets oscillating along its own field $\vec E$, perpendicular to the refracted ray. An oscillating charge radiates most strongly at right angles to its motion and not at all along it. For p-polarized light the electrons oscillate in the plane of incidence, perpendicular to the refracted ray; at Brewster's angle the reflected direction is also perpendicular to the refracted ray (Theorem §A4.5.5), so it lies exactly along their motion, and they send nothing that way. For s-polarized light they oscillate perpendicular to the plane of incidence and radiate into the reflected direction as usual. This is a picture, not a proof; the proof is the Fresnel calculation ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-4|Theorem §B9.2.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lectures 15–16*

^rem-a4-5-5

> [!example] Example §A4.5.3: Brewster's Angle for Water and Glass
> For light in air ($n_1 = 1.00$) reflected from water ($n_2 = 1.333$) and from crown glass ($n_2' = 1.520$):
>
> $$
> \theta_b = \tan^{-1}1.333 = 53.1^\circ, \qquad \theta_b' = \tan^{-1}1.52 = 56.7^\circ .
> $$
>
> Light reflected at these angles is horizontally polarized and is blocked completely by a polarizing filter with a vertical axis. The two angles are close, so sunglasses work about equally well on water and on glass. The *refracted* light at $\theta_b$ is only slightly polarized: just a small fraction of the light is reflected, so most of the horizontal component is refracted too.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 1.8*

^ex-a4-5-3

> [!example] Example §A4.5.4: A Plate at Brewster's Angle Reflects Nothing
> p-polarized light (field in the plane of incidence) meets a glass plate with parallel faces, in air, at Brewster's angle $\theta_b = \tan^{-1}n$. At the first face nothing is reflected. Inside, the light travels at $\theta_2 = 90^\circ - \theta_b$ (Theorem §A4.5.5) and meets the second face at that angle. For light going from glass to air, Brewster's law gives $\tan\theta_b'' = 1/n$; and $\tan(90^\circ - \theta_b) = \cot\theta_b = 1/n$ as well. So $\theta_2 = \theta_b''$: the light hits the second face exactly at its Brewster angle, and again nothing is reflected. For crown glass, $\theta_b = 56.7^\circ$ outside and $33.3^\circ$ inside; the two Brewster angles of any interface add up to $90^\circ$. All of the p-polarized light passes the plate.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Final practice exam*

## Scattering and polarizing materials

> [!remark] Remark: Polarization by scattering
> Sunlight scattered by air molecules is partly polarized, which polarizing sunglasses show as a sky that brightens and darkens as they turn. The incoming wave shakes the electrons of a molecule perpendicular to the sunlight's direction; they radiate like small antennas, with a field along their motion. Looking at right angles to the sunlight, we receive only light whose field is perpendicular to the original ray: a field along the original ray would require the sunlight to have been longitudinal. In other directions the polarization is partial, and multiple scattering mixes it further. Photographers darken a blue sky with a polarizing filter to make clouds stand out.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.7 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14*

^rem-a4-5-6

> [!remark] Remark: Polarization in materials
> - **Liquid crystals** rotate the polarization of light passing through them by $90^\circ$, and a voltage switches this off. A cell between polarizers is then a fast, small shutter: the basis of LCD screens, with red, green and blue cells in each pixel.
> - **Optically active** substances (sugar solutions, insulin, collagen) rotate the plane of polarization by an amount that depends on concentration, path length and wavelength; measuring the rotation measures sugar concentrations ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-5|Theorem §B8.3.5]]). Glass and plastic become optically active under stress, so a plastic model between crossed polarizers shows its stress pattern in colour ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^rem-b8-3-2|§B8.3, Remark: Variable retarders: stress, Kerr and Pockels cells]]).
> - **Birefringent** crystals have different indices for the two polarizations, so an unpolarized beam splits into two beams polarized at right angles; calcite has $n = 1.66$ and $1.49$, and one sees a double image through it ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^thm-b8-3-2|Theorem §B8.3.2]]). A birefringent plate of the right thickness turns linear into circular polarization (a quarter-wave plate): [[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity#^mod-b8-3-3|Model §B8.3.3]].
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.7 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 14*
