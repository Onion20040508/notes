Snell's law ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|Law §A4.2.4]]) has two consequences that the bare equation hides. Going from a denser to a less dense medium, it has no solution beyond a certain angle: then no light gets out, and the surface becomes a perfect mirror. And since the index depends slightly on wavelength, the colours in white light are refracted by slightly different amounts and separate, which is how prisms and rainbows make spectra.

## Total internal reflection

> [!definition] Definition §A4.3.1: Critical Angle; Total Internal Reflection
> For light going from medium 1 into medium 2 with $n_2 < n_1$, the **critical angle** $\theta_c$ is the angle of incidence for which the angle of refraction is $90^\circ$: the refracted ray grazes the surface. For $\theta_1 > \theta_c$ there is no refracted ray and all the light is reflected back into medium 1; this is **total internal reflection**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.4*

^def-a4-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.3.1: Critical Angle
> For light in medium 1 meeting a medium 2 of lower index ($n_1 > n_2$),
>
> $$
> \theta_c = \sin^{-1}\frac{n_2}{n_1} ,
> $$
>
> and total internal reflection occurs for every angle of incidence $\theta_1 > \theta_c$. If $n_1 < n_2$, there is no critical angle: some light is refracted at every angle of incidence.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.4, eq. (1.5)*

^thm-a4-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Snell's law gives $\sin\theta_2 = (n_1/n_2)\sin\theta_1$. Setting $\theta_2 = 90^\circ$, $\sin 90^\circ = 1$, gives $n_1\sin\theta_c = n_2$, which has a solution $\theta_c < 90^\circ$ exactly when $n_2 < n_1$. For $\theta_1 > \theta_c$ the right-hand side exceeds $(n_1/n_2)\sin\theta_c = 1$, and no angle has a sine greater than 1: there is no refracted ray. The light that would have crossed is reflected, and the reflected ray obeys the law of reflection as at any angle. If $n_1 < n_2$, then $(n_1/n_2)\sin\theta_1 < 1$ for every $\theta_1$, and a refracted ray always exists.

^der-a4-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Which way the light goes
> - Total internal reflection needs light travelling *from* the higher index *toward* the lower one: from water into air, never from air into water. "Internal" means inside the denser medium.
> - In $\theta_c = \sin^{-1}(n_2/n_1)$, $n_1$ is the medium the light is in. Writing the ratio upside down gives a sine greater than 1, a sign of the mistake.
> - Below $\theta_c$ the reflection is partial, not zero; how much is reflected at each angle is given by the Fresnel equations ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]]).

^cau-a4-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Fibre optics
> A thin glass or plastic fibre traps light that enters near its axis: the light strikes the wall at large angles of incidence, beyond $\theta_c$, and is totally reflected again and again, even around gentle bends. Bare fibres in contact would leak light into each other (same index), so each fibre has a core surrounded by a **cladding** of slightly lower index. In a telecommunication fibre the difference is a fraction of a percent, e.g. $n_{\text{core}} = 1.4475$, $n_{\text{clad}} = 1.444$ (0.24 % apart), so $\theta_c = \sin^{-1}(1.444/1.4475) = 86^\circ$: rays within about $4^\circ$ of the axis are guided. Bundles of fibres carry images (endoscopes, with one bundle to illuminate and one to look); single fibres carry telephone and internet signals over many kilometres with low loss, high bandwidth and little crosstalk between fibres.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.4 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 15*

^rem-a4-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Diamonds and the swimmer's mirror
> - Diamond has the largest index in the table of [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], so its critical angle with air is only $24.4^\circ$. Light that enters a cut diamond is reflected many times off facets placed to make escape unlikely, and leaves in a few bright directions: the sparkle. The flashes of colour come from dispersion (below). Zircon ($n = 1.923$) and cubic zirconia ($n \approx 2.17$) sparkle less.
> - Seen from below, an undisturbed water surface is a mirror for all rays that meet it at more than $48.6^\circ$ from the vertical: an underwater camera sees the swimmer's reflection above, and sees the world above the water only through a cone around the vertical.

^rem-a4-3-2

> [!example] Example §A4.3.1: Critical Angles of Common Pairs
> For a polystyrene rod ($n_1 = 1.49$) in air ($n_2 = 1.00$),
>
> $$
> \theta_c = \sin^{-1}\frac{1.00}{1.49} = \sin^{-1}0.671 = 42.2^\circ .
> $$
>
> Any ray inside the plastic that meets the surface at more than $42.2^\circ$ is totally reflected: the inside surface is a perfect mirror without silvering. The same calculation gives $48.6^\circ$ for water to air, $24.4^\circ$ for diamond to air and $66.3^\circ$ for flint glass ($1.66$) to crown glass ($1.52$). The closer the two indices, the larger the critical angle, and the more nearly grazing a ray must be to be trapped.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 1.4*

^ex-a4-3-1

> [!example] Example §A4.3.2: A Prism as a Perfect Mirror
> Light enters a $45^\circ$–$45^\circ$–$90^\circ$ glass prism perpendicularly through one of the short faces, so it goes straight in and meets the long face (the hypotenuse) at $45^\circ$ from its normal. Outside is air, $n_2 = 1$.
>
> *Condition for total reflection.* We need $45^\circ \geq \theta_c$, i.e. $\sin 45^\circ \geq 1/n_1$:
>
> $$
> n_1 \geq \frac{1}{\sin 45^\circ} = \sqrt2 = 1.414 .
> $$
>
> Crown glass ($1.52$) qualifies: the light is turned through $90^\circ$ with no loss at the hypotenuse, and leaves through the other short face. Binoculars and periscopes use such prisms instead of silvered mirrors, which absorb part of the light (a good mirror reflects a little over 90 %).
>
> *A prism that fails.* For $n_1 = 1.33$ (water-like), $\theta_c = 48.8^\circ > 45^\circ$, and the light partly escapes through the hypotenuse at
>
> $$
> \sin\theta_t = 1.33\sin 45^\circ = 0.940, \qquad \theta_t = 70.1^\circ
> $$
>
> from its normal; only a fraction is reflected (how much needs the Fresnel equations, [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]], [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Final practice exam (problem 1; its figure is not available, so the usual $45^\circ$ geometry is assumed)*

## Dispersion

> [!definition] Definition §A4.3.2: Dispersion
> **Dispersion** is any dependence of the propagation of a wave on its wavelength. For light it shows as the spreading of white light into its spectrum of wavelengths, its colours. **White light** is a fairly uniform mixture of all visible wavelengths, about $400$ to $750\ \mathrm{nm}$ ([[§A12.5 The Electromagnetic Spectrum#^def-a12-5-1|EM Def. §A12.5.1]]); the eye sees roughly six colour bands, red ($\approx 660\ \mathrm{nm}$), orange, yellow, green, blue and violet ($\approx 410\ \mathrm{nm}$), but the spectrum is continuous.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.5*

^def-a4-3-2

> [!law] Law §A4.3.2: Dispersion of Transparent Materials
> The index of refraction of a material depends on the wavelength of the light, $n = n(\lambda)$. For ordinary transparent materials in the visible, $n$ increases steadily as the wavelength decreases, and is largest for violet:
>
> | Medium | red, $660\ \mathrm{nm}$ | yellow, $580\ \mathrm{nm}$ | blue, $470\ \mathrm{nm}$ | violet, $410\ \mathrm{nm}$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | water | 1.331 | 1.333 | 1.338 | 1.342 |
> | crown glass | 1.512 | 1.518 | 1.524 | 1.530 |
> | flint glass | 1.662 | 1.667 | 1.684 | 1.698 |
> | polystyrene | 1.488 | 1.492 | 1.499 | 1.506 |
> | diamond | 2.410 | 2.417 | 2.444 | 2.458 |
>
> *Valid when:* transparent materials, in the visible range, away from the frequencies at which the material absorbs strongly. In vacuum there is no dispersion: all wavelengths travel at $c$.
> *Source: UP3 §1.5, Table 1.2*

^law-a4-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Where dispersion comes from
> In the picture of [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^rem-a4-1-2|Remark: Why light is slower in matter]], the electrons bound in the material respond like driven oscillators with a natural frequency $\omega_0$ above the frequencies of visible light. Their response, hence the index, depends on how close the light's frequency is to $\omega_0$; for $\omega \ll \omega_0$ this gives Cauchy's formula
>
> $$
> n(\lambda) \approx A + \frac{B}{\lambda^2},
> $$
>
> with constants $A$, $B$ for each material: $n$ rises toward short wavelengths, as in the table. (Derivation: [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-8|Theorem §B13.1.8]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^law-b13-1-9|Law §B13.1.9]]; a fit to the table: [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^ex-b13-1-1|Example §B13.1.1]].) A prism does not *create* the colours: Newton showed that light of one colour sent into a second prism stays that colour, and that the separated colours, recombined, give white light again.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lectures 16–17*

^rem-a4-3-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A4.3.3: Colours Are Refracted by Different Amounts
> White light refracted from air into a dispersive medium spreads into its colours: at a common angle of incidence $\theta_1 \neq 0$, violet light is refracted closest to the normal and red light farthest from it. Reflection, in contrast, sends all wavelengths in the same direction.
>
> *Source: UP3 §1.5*

^thm-a4-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For each wavelength, Snell's law gives $\sin\theta_2(\lambda) = \sin\theta_1/n(\lambda)$ (with $n_{\text{air}} = 1$). By [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]] $n$ is largest for violet and smallest for red, so $\sin\theta_2$, hence $\theta_2$, is smallest for violet: violet is bent most ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^thm-a4-2-5|Theorem §A4.2.5]], part 2). The law of reflection, $\theta_r = \theta_i$, contains no property of the medium, hence no wavelength.

> [!example] Example §A4.3.3: White Light Entering Flint Glass
> A beam of white light goes from air into flint glass at $\theta_1 = 43.2^\circ$. With the indices of [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]],
>
> $$
> \theta_{\text{red}} = \sin^{-1}\frac{\sin 43.2^\circ}{1.662} = \sin^{-1}0.4119 = 24.3^\circ, \qquad \theta_{\text{violet}} = \sin^{-1}\frac{\sin 43.2^\circ}{1.698} = \sin^{-1}0.4032 = 23.8^\circ .
> $$
>
> The red and violet edges of the refracted beam diverge by $0.55^\circ$, about $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ of separation after $10\ \mathrm{cm}$ of glass ($0.55^\circ = 9.6 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad}$). Small, but over a long enough path the colours separate visibly. (University Physics prints $27.0^\circ$ and $26.4^\circ$, which do not satisfy Snell's law with these indices; the spread, about $0.5^\circ$, is nearly the same.)
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 1.5*

^ex-a4-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Rainbows
> Sunlight entering a raindrop is refracted at the front surface, reflected from the back, and refracted again on leaving. Both refractions disperse it; the reflection does not ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^thm-a4-3-3|Theorem §A4.3.3]]). Each colour therefore leaves the drops at its own angle to the incoming sunlight, so we see a rainbow only with the Sun behind us, and each colour comes from the drops that lie at the right angle from the line through the Sun and our eye: a circular arc. A second reflection inside the drops gives the fainter secondary rainbow above the primary one, with the colours in reverse order (red lowest).

^rem-a4-3-4

> [!remark] Remark: Dispersion as nuisance and as tool
> In a fibre, white light used to send a message spreads out in time, since the wavelengths travel at different speeds, until successive pulses overlap; lasers, nearly monochromatic, suffer little dispersion. Conversely, the dispersion of electromagnetic waves reaching us from space shows how much matter they crossed, and has revealed much about the interstellar medium. Any wave can be dispersive: water waves, sound in a long tube, a pulse on a spring, in which the high-frequency parts outrun the low ones. A diffraction grating also spreads white light into a spectrum, but by interference rather than through $n(\lambda)$, and in the opposite order, red deviated most ([[§A7.3 Diffraction Gratings#^rem-a7-3-1|§A7.3]]).
