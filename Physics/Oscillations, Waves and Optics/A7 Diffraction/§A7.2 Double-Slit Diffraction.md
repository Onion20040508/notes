Young's double slit ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference|§A6.1]]) treated each slit as a point source, which predicts fringes of equal brightness out to large angles. Real slits have a width, and each one by itself produces the single-slit pattern of [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction|§A7.1]]. With both effects at work, the fringes stay where interference puts them, but their brightness follows the single-slit pattern, and some fringes disappear altogether.

## Interference times diffraction

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.2.1: Two-Slit Diffraction Pattern
> Two slits of width $D$ whose centres are a distance $d$ apart, lit as in [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^mod-a7-1-1|Model §A7.1.1]], produce the intensity
>
> $$
> I(\theta) = I_0\,\cos^2\Bigl(\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\Bigr)\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2, \qquad \beta = \frac{\pi D\sin\theta}{\lambda} :
> $$
>
> the interference pattern of two point sources a distance $d$ apart, multiplied by the diffraction pattern of one slit of width $D$. The bright fringes lie where $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, as for point sources; their intensities are reduced by the single-slit factor.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.3 · PHY 287, week 14 slides*

^thm-a7-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> University Physics states the result without proof; here is the phasor argument (phasors as in the derivation of [[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]]). Toward angle $\theta$, the wavelets from slit 1 add to a phasor of amplitude $E_{\text{c}}\,\sin\beta/\beta$ ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-3|Theorem §A7.1.3]]), and those from slit 2 to a phasor of the same length. Each point of slit 2 lies a distance $d$ below the corresponding point of slit 1, so its wavelet is behind by the path $d\sin\theta$; hence the whole phasor of slit 2 is turned by $\phi = 2\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda$ relative to that of slit 1. Two equal phasors at an angle $\phi$ add to $2E_{\text{c}}\,(\sin\beta/\beta)\cos(\phi/2)$ in magnitude, exactly as two point sources do ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]). Squaring, and calling $I_0$ the intensity at $\theta = 0$, gives the product formula. Its first factor is the double-slit pattern ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-5|Theorem §A6.1.5]]), its second the single-slit pattern.

^der-a7-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: When can diffraction be ignored?
> If the slits are narrower than the wavelength, the single-slit factor has no zero ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^rem-a7-1-3|Remark: Wide slits, narrow peaks]]) and varies slowly; all fringes are then nearly equally bright, and Chapter A6 was right to treat the slits as points. Once $D$ is a few wavelengths, the envelope dominates the look of the pattern.

^rem-a7-2-1

> [!remark] Remark: More light, less light
> Compared with one slit, opening a second slit makes some places on the screen *darker*: in the minima of the $\cos^2$ factor the two waves cancel. There is no paradox, since amplitudes, not intensities, add. Elsewhere the intensity rises to four times the single-slit value, and on average over a fringe it is twice the single-slit value, as two slits pass twice the light.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 11*

^rem-a7-2-2

## Missing orders

> [!definition] Definition §A7.2.1: Missing Order
> A **missing order** is an interference maximum, $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, that is not seen because it falls at a minimum of the single-slit diffraction pattern.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.3*

^def-a7-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.2.2: Missing Orders and Fringes in the Central Peak
> Write the interference maxima as $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ and the diffraction minima as $D\sin\theta = n\lambda$ ($n \neq 0$). The interference order $m$ is missing exactly when $m/n = d/D$ for some such $n$. In particular, if $d/D = k$ is an integer, the orders $m = \pm k, \pm 2k, \dots$ are missing, and the central diffraction peak contains the $2k - 1$ bright fringes $m = 0, \pm1, \dots, \pm(k - 1)$. The relative intensity of the fringe of order $m$ is
>
> $$
> \frac{I_m}{I_0} = \left(\frac{\sin(m\pi D/d)}{m\pi D/d}\right)^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.3, Examples 4.3–4.4*

^thm-a7-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At an interference maximum $\sin\theta = m\lambda/d$, so the $\cos^2$ factor is $1$ and $\beta = \pi D\sin\theta/\lambda = m\pi D/d$; [[§A7.2 Double-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-2-1|Theorem §A7.2.1]] gives the stated $I_m$. It vanishes when $\beta$ is a nonzero multiple of $\pi$, $mD/d = n$, i.e. $m/n = d/D$. For $d/D = k$ this means $m = nk$. The central diffraction peak is $\lvert D\sin\theta\rvert < \lambda$, i.e. $\lvert m\rvert < d/D = k$, which leaves $m = 0, \pm1, \dots, \pm(k - 1)$.

^der-a7-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A7.2 Double-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-2-1|Theorem §A7.2.1]], [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-2|Theorem §A7.1.2]], [[§A7.2 Double-Slit Diffraction#^def-a7-2-1|Def. §A7.2.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Two integers, two letters
> $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ (bright, from interference, spacing $d$) and $D\sin\theta = n\lambda$ (dark, from diffraction, width $D$) look alike. Keep the letters apart, and remember $d > D$ always: the slits cannot overlap, so the diffraction minima are farther out than the first interference fringes. The formula for $I_m$ evaluates the envelope at the ideal fringe angle; since the envelope slopes, the actual peak sits very slightly closer to the centre.

^cau-a7-2-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A7.2.1: Slits Three Times Their Width Apart
> Two slits of width $D$ are $d = 3D$ apart, lit by light of wavelength $\lambda$.
> - Ignoring diffraction, the next four maxima after the central one are at $\sin\theta = \lambda/d,\ 2\lambda/d,\ 3\lambda/d,\ 4\lambda/d$ ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]]).
> - With diffraction, $\beta = m\pi D/d = m\pi/3$ there ([[§A7.2 Double-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]]):
> $$
> \frac{I_m}{I_0} = \Bigl(\frac{\sin(m\pi/3)}{m\pi/3}\Bigr)^2 = 0.684,\ 0.171,\ 0,\ 0.043 \qquad \text{for } m = 1, 2, 3, 4 .
> $$
> - Missing: $m = \pm3, \pm6, \pm9, \dots$, where $\beta$ is a multiple of $\pi$. The central diffraction peak holds the five fringes $m = 0, \pm1, \pm2$.
>
> These are the numbers of University Physics's standard case $D = 2\lambda$, $d = 6\lambda$ (its Example 4.3 gets $0.684$ for $m = 1$). PHY 287 writes $a$ for the slit width.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Final practice*

> [!example] Example §A7.2.2: Counting Fringes in the Central Peak
> Slits $D = 0.020\ \mathrm{mm}$ wide and $d = 0.20\ \mathrm{mm}$ apart are lit with $\lambda = 500\ \mathrm{nm}$. The first diffraction minimum is at $\theta \approx \sin\theta = \lambda/D = 2.5 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{rad}$, where the interference order is $m = d\sin\theta/\lambda = d/D = 10$. So $m = \pm10$ are missing orders, and the central peak contains the $19$ bright fringes $m = -9, \dots, +9$ ([[§A7.2 Double-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-2-2|Theorem §A7.2.2]] with $k = 10$). By the same rule $m = \pm20$ are missing as well.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 4.4*
