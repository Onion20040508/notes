Every optical instrument lets light in through a finite opening: a pupil, a lens, a telescope mirror. Like a slit ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction|§A7.1]]), the opening spreads the light, so the image of a point is not a point but a small blurred disk surrounded by faint rings. Two points closer together than the size of these blurs cannot be told apart. This section fixes a criterion for "just distinguishable", gives the angular limit it sets, and applies the same idea to telescopes, microscopes and gratings.

## The Rayleigh criterion

> [!definition] Definition §A7.4.1: Resolution; Rayleigh Criterion
> **Resolution** is the ability of an optical system to show two nearby objects as distinct. By the **Rayleigh criterion**, the images of two point sources are *just resolvable* when the centre of the diffraction pattern of one lies on the first minimum of the diffraction pattern of the other.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.5*

^def-a7-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: A convention, not a law
> Nothing physical changes abruptly at the Rayleigh separation: as two sources approach, the dip between their blurred images just gets shallower (to $73\%$ of the peaks at the Rayleigh separation for a circular aperture, figure below) until it disappears. The criterion is a reasonable, widely used choice of where to draw the line. The same rule is applied to spectral lines, with "the maximum of one wavelength on the minimum of the other" ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-3|Theorem §A7.4.3]]).

^rem-a7-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.4.1: Diffraction Limit of a Circular Aperture
> The diffraction pattern of a circular aperture of diameter $D \gg \lambda$ has a bright central disk whose first minimum is at the angle
>
> $$
> \theta = 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D} \qquad (\theta \text{ in radians}) .
> $$
>
> By the Rayleigh criterion, two point objects observed through the aperture (or a lens or mirror of diameter $D$) are just resolvable when their angular separation is this **diffraction limit**.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.5, eq. (4.5)*

^thm-a7-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The idea; the full proof is beyond level A.* For a slit of width $D$ the first minimum is at $\sin\theta = \lambda/D$, where the wavelets of the upper half cancel those of the lower half in pairs ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-2|Theorem §A7.1.2]]). A circular opening of diameter $D$ can likewise be cut into parallel strips, each of which sends its wavelets toward $\theta$ with a single path length; the same pairing would give $\lambda/D$ if all strips were equally long. But the strips near the edge of a circle are short, so the edges count less than in a slit, and the opening behaves like a somewhat narrower slit: the first zero moves out, to $1.22\lambda/D$. Getting the factor requires adding the wavelets over the disk, an integral that leads to a Bessel function; $1.22 = 3.832/\pi$, where $3.832$ is its first zero (Fraunhofer diffraction, Chapter B11, planned). For small angles $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, so $\theta = 1.22\lambda/D$ in radians. The resolution statement is then [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^def-a7-4-1|Def. §A7.4.1]] applied to this pattern: the second source's central maximum sits on the first source's first zero.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19*

> [!remark] Remark: What limits resolution in practice
> - The limit applies to any system with a lens or mirror of diameter $D$: telescopes ([[§A5.5 Magnifiers, Microscopes and Telescopes#^thm-a5-5-3|Theorem §A5.5.3]]) are limited by the diameter of the primary mirror, the eye ([[§A5.4 The Eye and the Camera#^mod-a5-4-1|Model §A5.4.1]]) by its pupil. Magnification alone ([[§A5.5 Magnifiers, Microscopes and Telescopes#^def-a5-5-1|Def. §A5.5.1]]) cannot beat the limit: it enlarges the blur along with the image. Ground-based telescopes do worse because of the atmosphere, and real instruments also suffer from aberrations and imperfect surfaces. The acuity of the eye is likewise limited by diffraction at the pupil, its circular aperture.
> - The same spreading affects a beam itself. A beam of diameter $D$ cannot stay parallel; it diverges at about $1.22\lambda/D$, which matters for long-distance laser and microwave links. Laser light sent to the Moon is first expanded through a telescope to make $D$ large.
> - All imaging is limited by the wavelength of the probe. Clever fluorescence techniques can beat the Rayleigh limit by controlling which molecules emit (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2014).
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.5 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19*

^rem-a7-4-2

## Microscopes

> [!definition] Definition §A7.4.2: Numerical Aperture
> For a lens that accepts light from a point $P$ in a cone of half-angle $\alpha$, through a medium of index $n$ between $P$ and the lens, the **numerical aperture** is $\mathrm{NA} = n\sin\alpha$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.5*

^def-a7-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.4.2: Resolving Power of a Microscope
> A microscope objective ([[§A5.5 Magnifiers, Microscopes and Telescopes#^thm-a5-5-2|Theorem §A5.5.2]]) of diameter $D$ at distance $d$ from the specimen can just resolve two points a distance
>
> $$
> x = 1.22\,\frac{\lambda_n d}{D} = 0.61\,\frac{\lambda_0}{\mathrm{NA}}
> $$
>
> apart, where $\lambda_n = \lambda_0/n$ is the wavelength in the medium between specimen and lens and $\lambda_0$ the vacuum wavelength. The smaller $x$, the better the resolution: it improves with larger numerical aperture and shorter wavelength.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.5*

^thm-a7-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Two points a distance $x \ll d$ apart subtend the angle $x/d$ at the lens. They are just resolved when this equals the diffraction limit ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-1|Theorem §A7.4.1]]) for the light in the medium, of wavelength $\lambda_n$ ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]]): $x/d = 1.22\lambda_n/D$. With small angles the edge of the lens is seen from $P$ at $\sin\alpha \approx (D/2)/d$, so $d/D = 1/(2\sin\alpha)$ and $x = 0.61\lambda_n/\sin\alpha = 0.61\lambda_0/(n\sin\alpha) = 0.61\lambda_0/\mathrm{NA}$.

^der-a7-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-1|Theorem §A7.4.1]], [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^def-a7-4-2|Def. §A7.4.2]], [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^thm-a4-1-1|Theorem §A4.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Which wavelength?
> University Physics writes the result as $x = 0.61\lambda n/\mathrm{NA}$. That is correct only if $\lambda$ there means the wavelength in the medium, $\lambda_n$, since $\lambda_n n = \lambda_0$. With the vacuum wavelength the formula is $x = 0.61\lambda_0/\mathrm{NA}$: an immersion oil of higher $n$ raises NA and *improves* the resolution. A large NA also gathers more light, so the image is brighter, and the focal spot is smaller and more intense.

^cau-a7-4-1

## Resolving wavelengths with a grating

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.4.3: Resolving Power of a Grating
> A grating with $N$ illuminated lines separates, in order $m$, two wavelengths $\lambda$ and $\lambda + \Delta\lambda$ by the Rayleigh criterion when
>
> $$
> \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} \le mN .
> $$
>
> The ratio $\lambda/\Delta\lambda$ for the smallest separable $\Delta\lambda$ is the **resolving power**; for a grating it is $mN$, in first order simply $N$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19*

^thm-a7-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The order-$m$ principal maximum of $\lambda + \Delta\lambda$ is at $d\sin\theta = m(\lambda + \Delta\lambda)$ ([[§A7.3 Diffraction Gratings#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]). The zero of the $\lambda$ pattern next to its own order-$m$ maximum is at $d\sin\theta = m\lambda + \lambda/N$ ([[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]]). Rayleigh's criterion ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^def-a7-4-1|Def. §A7.4.1]]) puts the first on the second: $m\,\Delta\lambda = \lambda/N$, i.e. $\lambda/\Delta\lambda = mN$. Wavelengths farther apart than this are resolved. The result does not depend on $d$: what counts is the number of lines that take part.

^der-a7-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A7.3 Diffraction Gratings#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]], [[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]], [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^def-a7-4-1|Def. §A7.4.1]]

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A7.4.1: The Hubble Space Telescope
> Hubble's primary mirror has $D = 2.40\ \mathrm m$; take $\lambda = 550\ \mathrm{nm}$. By [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-1|Theorem §A7.4.1]] the smallest resolvable angle is
>
> $$
> \theta = 1.22\,\frac{550 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm m}{2.40\ \mathrm m} = 2.80 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{rad},
> $$
>
> less than $1/50\,000$ of a degree. At the distance of the Andromeda Galaxy, $r = 2.0 \times 10^6$ light-years, two stars this angle apart are separated by $s = r\theta = (2.0 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{ly})(2.80 \times 10^{-7}) = 0.56\ \mathrm{ly}$. Stars in a galaxy are typically about $1$ to $5\ \mathrm{ly}$ apart, so Hubble can resolve most individual stars of Andromeda. A radio telescope needs a far larger dish for the same resolution, because its wavelength is far longer.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 4.6*

^ex-a7-4-1

> [!example] Example §A7.4.2: How Many Lines to Split Hydrogen's Violet Lines?
> The two violet lines of hydrogen are at $410.1\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $434.0\ \mathrm{nm}$, the closest pair of its visible lines. In first order, [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-3|Theorem §A7.4.3]] requires
>
> $$
> N \ge \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = \frac{410.1\ \mathrm{nm}}{23.9\ \mathrm{nm}} = 17.2,
> $$
>
> so a grating of $18$ lines would barely separate them. Ordinary gratings have hundreds to thousands of illuminated lines and separate wavelengths about $1\%$ apart or much closer; high-quality ones reach $N \sim 10^4$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19*

^ex-a7-4-2
