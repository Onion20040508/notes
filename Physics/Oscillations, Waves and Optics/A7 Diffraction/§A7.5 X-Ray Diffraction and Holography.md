Interference needs structures comparable to the wavelength. For visible light these are slits and gratings made by hand; for X-rays, with wavelengths near $0.1\ \mathrm{nm}$, nature supplies them: the regularly spaced planes of atoms in a crystal act as a three-dimensional grating, and the angles at which X-rays reflect strongly reveal the spacing of the atoms. The section ends with holography, which records the interference pattern of light from an object and uses it as a grating to rebuild the object's three-dimensional image.

## X-ray diffraction

> [!definition] Definition §A7.5.1: X-Ray Diffraction; Bragg Planes
> **X-ray diffraction** is the interference of X-rays scattered by the atoms of a crystal or molecule, used to infer the arrangement of the atoms. A crystal contains many families of parallel, equally spaced planes of atoms, its **Bragg planes**, each family with its own orientation and spacing $d$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.6*

^def-a7-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why X-rays
> X-ray wavelengths are about $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-12}\ \mathrm m$ ([[§A12.5 The Electromagnetic Spectrum#^def-a12-5-1|EM Def. §A12.5.1]]). Against macroscopic objects, such as teeth, X-rays behave as rays and cast sharp shadows. Against atoms, about $0.1\ \mathrm{nm}$ in size and spacing, they diffract. Ultraviolet light, with wavelengths a thousand times longer than the atomic spacing, cannot resolve a crystal lattice.

^rem-a7-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.5.1: Bragg's Law
> X-rays of wavelength $\lambda$ that strike a family of crystal planes of spacing $d$ at the glancing angle $\theta$, measured from the planes, are reflected strongly when
>
> $$
> m\lambda = 2d\sin\theta, \qquad m = 1, 2, 3, \dots
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.6, eq. (4.6)*

^thm-a7-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each plane of atoms scatters a little of the incident wave; the scattered waves add up to a strong beam only in the direction of mirror reflection, making the same angle $\theta$ with the plane as the incident beam ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-1|Law §A4.2.1]]). Compare the rays reflected by two neighbouring planes (figure). Drop perpendiculars from the reflection point on the upper plane onto the incoming and the outgoing lower ray: up to them the two paths are equally long, and the lower ray's extra path consists of two segments, each the side opposite the angle $\theta$ in a right triangle with hypotenuse $d$, of length $d\sin\theta$ each. The path difference is $2d\sin\theta$. If it is a whole number of wavelengths, the reflections from the two planes are in phase ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]); then so are those from all deeper planes, each a further $2d\sin\theta$ behind, and they add to a strong reflected beam.

^der-a7-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-1|Law §A4.2.1]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]], [[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^def-a7-5-1|Def. §A7.5.1]]

![[ph-wo-a7-5-1.svg]]
*Bragg reflection from two neighbouring planes of atoms (grey dots), a distance $d$ apart. The blue X-rays meet the planes at the glancing angle $\theta$ and leave at the same angle. The ray reflected by the lower plane travels the two red segments, $d\sin\theta$ each, farther than the ray reflected by the upper plane.*

> [!caution] Caution: The Bragg angle is measured from the planes
> In geometric optics angles are measured from the normal ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^def-a4-2-1|Def. §A4.2.1]]); in Bragg's law $\theta$ is measured from the crystal planes. Also, $d$ is the spacing of the planes, not of slits, and $m$ starts at $1$ (the $m = 0$ "order" is just the undeflected beam). Since $\sin\theta \le 1$, only orders with $m\lambda \le 2d$ exist.

^cau-a7-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: From X-rays to DNA
> In 1912 Max von Laue proposed scattering X-rays from crystals, whose atomic spacings were known: a diffraction pattern would prove that X-rays are electromagnetic waves and give their wavelength. It did (Nobel Prize 1914). William Henry and William Lawrence Bragg then built the X-ray spectrometer and the analysis of crystal structures on this law (Nobel Prize 1915). X-ray crystallography reveals the atomic arrangement of materials and molecules: Rosalind Franklin's diffraction data were the basis of Watson and Crick's double-helix model of DNA (1953). Because a real crystal has many families of Bragg planes at once, its diffraction pattern is a complex array of spots.

^rem-a7-5-2

> [!example] Example §A7.5.1: X-Rays on Table Salt
> A family of planes in an NaCl crystal is $d = 0.252\ \mathrm{nm}$ apart, and the first-order reflection ($m = 1$) is at $\theta = 18.1^\circ$. By [[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^thm-a7-5-1|Theorem §A7.5.1]],
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{2d\sin\theta}{m} = 2(0.252 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm m)\sin 18.1^\circ = 1.57 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm m = 0.157\ \mathrm{nm},
> $$
>
> an X-ray wavelength, comparable to the spacing it probes. The same wavelength is also reflected at $\sin\theta = m\lambda/2d = 0.621$ and $0.932$ for $m = 2, 3$ ($\theta = 38.4^\circ$ and $68.8^\circ$); $m = 4$ would need $\sin\theta = 1.24$, so these three are all.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 4.7*

^ex-a7-5-1

## Holography

> [!definition] Definition §A7.5.2: Hologram; Holography
> A **hologram** is a three-dimensional image recorded on film as an interference pattern; **holography** is the technique of producing holograms with coherent light, in practice laser light. (The name means "entire picture".)
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.7*

^def-a7-5-2

> [!remark] Remark: Recording and reconstruction
> - **Recording.** A laser beam is split by a partially silvered mirror. One part lights the object; the other, the **reference beam**, falls directly on the film. Light scattered by the object interferes with the reference beam, and the film records the resulting fringe pattern ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]), darkening where the interference was constructive. To the eye the developed film looks foggy; no lens is involved, which is why holography is sometimes called lens-less photography.
> - **Reconstruction.** Lit by the same kind of laser, the film acts as a collection of diffraction gratings of varying spacing ([[§A7.3 Diffraction Gratings|§A7.3]]). The diffracted light reproduces the wave that came from the object, forming a real and a virtual image. Moving the eye through this wave shows the object from different angles, as the object itself would: the image is truly three-dimensional.
> - **Why lasers.** The fringes are recorded only if the object and reference beams keep a fixed phase relation over the exposure: the light must be coherent and of one wavelength ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]]). Holography was invented by Dennis Gabor in the late 1940s (Nobel Prize 1971) but became practical only with the laser.

^rem-a7-5-3

> [!remark] Remark: Kinds of holograms
> A **transmission hologram** is viewed by laser light passing through it. The holograms on credit cards are **reflection holograms**, viewed in reflected white light; they look slightly blurred with rainbow edges, because a grating sends each wavelength in a different direction. Since the stored gratings are so precise, several holograms can be recorded on one film by changing its angle between exposures: holograms that change as one walks past are made this way. Holograms store three-dimensional information on statues, structures and organs, and serve as security features because they are hard to copy.

^rem-a7-5-4
