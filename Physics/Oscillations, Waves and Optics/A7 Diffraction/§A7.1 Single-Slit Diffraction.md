Interference seemed to need two sources ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference|§A6.1]]). Yet a single slit a little wider than the wavelength also throws a pattern of bright and dark fringes instead of a sharp shadow. By Huygens's principle every point of the wave front inside the slit is a source of wavelets ([[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^pr-a4-4-1|Principle §A4.4.1]]), and these infinitely many sources interfere with one another. This is diffraction ([[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^def-a4-4-2|Def. §A4.4.2]]) made quantitative: where the dark fringes fall, and how bright the pattern is everywhere.

## Where the dark fringes are

> [!model] Model §A7.1.1: Single Slit, Distant Screen
> A slit of width $D$, much longer than it is wide, is lit at normal incidence by a monochromatic plane wave of wavelength $\lambda$. Assumptions:
> - the wave is uniform and in phase across the opening, and every point of it acts as a Huygens source of the same strength;
> - the wavelets carry the phase of the wave at their source and add by superposition ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^pr-a2-5-1|Principle §A2.5.1]]). This goes beyond Huygens's principle as stated in [[§A4.4 Huygens's Principle#^pr-a4-4-1|Principle §A4.4.1]], which locates wave fronts but says nothing about amplitudes (the Huygens–Fresnel picture);
> - the screen is so far away (or placed in the focal plane of a lens) that the rays from all parts of the slit to a given point on it are parallel, at angle $\theta$ to the incident direction.
>
> This far-field limit is **Fraunhofer** diffraction; the near-field case and the justification of the Huygens sources are Chapter B11 (planned).
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.1*

^mod-a7-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.1.2: Single-Slit Minima
> In the single-slit pattern the intensity is zero at the angles
>
> $$
> D\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = \pm1, \pm2, \pm3, \dots
> $$
>
> Straight ahead ($\theta = 0$) is the bright central maximum.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.1, eq. (4.1)*

^thm-a7-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Straight ahead all wavelets travel equal distances and arrive in phase: a maximum. At angle $\theta$ the ray from the bottom edge travels $D\sin\theta$ farther than the ray from the top edge (figure; same construction as [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]]). Suppose $D\sin\theta = \lambda$. Split the slit into an upper and a lower half. Each point of the upper half has a partner $D/2$ below it in the lower half, whose ray is longer by $\frac{D}{2}\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{2}$: the two cancel ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]). Every wavelet is cancelled by its partner, and the intensity is zero. If $D\sin\theta = m\lambda$, split the slit into $2\lvert m\rvert$ strips instead; corresponding points of neighbouring strips are $D/(2\lvert m\rvert)$ apart and differ in path by $\lambda/2$, so the strips cancel in pairs. Negative $m$ are the mirror images on the other side.

^der-a7-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^mod-a7-1-1|Model §A7.1.1]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]

![[ph-wo-a7-1-1.svg]]
*A slit of width $D$ at the angle of its first minimum. The ray from the bottom edge travels $D\sin\theta = \lambda$ farther than the ray from the top edge (red); the ray from the centre travels $\lambda/2$ farther than the top ray (orange), and in the same way every ray of the upper half has a partner in the lower half half a wavelength behind it.*

> [!caution] Caution: Same formula, opposite meaning
> - $D\sin\theta = m\lambda$ gives the **dark** fringes of one slit of **width** $D$; $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ gives the **bright** fringes of two slits a **distance** $d$ apart ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]]). When both appear in one problem, use different letters for the two integers ([[§A7.2 Double-Slit Diffraction#^cau-a7-2-1|Caution: Two integers, two letters]]).
> - $m = 0$ is excluded: at $\theta = 0$ there is no cancellation.
> - In this chapter $D$ is the slit width (University Physics's notation), not the slit–screen distance of [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference|§A6.1]]; the screen distance is called $L$ here.

^cau-a7-1-1

## The intensity

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.1.3: Single-Slit Intensity
> Let $\beta = \dfrac{\pi D\sin\theta}{\lambda}$, half the phase difference between the wavelets from the two edges of the slit. The amplitude and intensity at angle $\theta$ are
>
> $$
> E = E_{\text{c}}\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}, \qquad I = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2,
> $$
>
> where $E_{\text{c}}$ and $I_0$ are the amplitude and intensity at the centre of the pattern.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.2, eqs. (4.2)–(4.4)*

^thm-a7-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Replace the slit by $N$ Huygens sources a distance $D/N$ apart, each giving a phasor of length $\Delta E_0$ at the screen. Neighbours differ in path by $(D/N)\sin\theta$, so each phasor is turned by $2\pi D\sin\theta/(\lambda N)$ from the previous one, and the first and last differ by $\phi = 2\pi D\sin\theta/\lambda = 2\beta$. As in [[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]], the phasors laid head to tail are chords of one circle. For $N \to \infty$ they become a circular arc of fixed length $N\Delta E_0 = E_{\text{c}}$ (the straight line it forms at $\theta = 0$) that turns through the total angle $\phi$, so its radius is $r = E_{\text{c}}/\phi$. The resultant is the chord of this arc:
>
> $$
> E = 2r\sin\frac{\phi}{2} = \frac{2E_{\text{c}}}{\phi}\sin\frac{\phi}{2} = E_{\text{c}}\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta} .
> $$
>
> The intensity is proportional to the amplitude squared ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]]). At $\theta = 0$, $\sin\beta/\beta \to 1$ by l'Hôpital's rule ([[§30 L'Hospital's Rule#^ex-30-1|451 Ex. §30.1]]), so $I_0$ is indeed the central intensity.

^der-a7-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]], [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^mod-a7-1-1|Model §A7.1.1]], [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]], [[§30 L'Hospital's Rule#^ex-30-1|451 Ex. §30.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The same result as a limit of many slits
> The formula is also the limit of the $N$-slit intensity ([[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]]) when $N \to \infty$ with the total width $D = Nd$ fixed: normalised to its central value, $\sin^2(N\phi/2)/\bigl(N^2\sin^2(\phi/2)\bigr)$ with $N\phi/2 = \beta$ tends to $\sin^2\beta/\beta^2$, because $N\sin(\beta/N) \to \beta$. A slit is a grating whose slits have merged. PHY 287 obtains it a third way, by integrating the wavelets $\sin(\omega t - kr)$ across the slit.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19 · PHY 287, week 14 slides*

^rem-a7-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: $\beta$ is not an angle in space
> $\beta$ is measured in radians but is not an angle one could measure with a protractor: it is half the phase difference between the edge wavelets, the angle through which the phasor arc turns, halved.

^rem-a7-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.1.4: Central and Secondary Maxima
> The zeros of $I$ are at $\beta = m\pi$ ($m \neq 0$), which is [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-2|Theorem §A7.1.2]]. Hence:
> - the **central maximum** extends from $\sin\theta = -\lambda/D$ to $\sin\theta = +\lambda/D$; for small angles its angular width is $\Delta\theta \approx 2\lambda/D$, and on a screen at distance $L$ its width is $2\lambda L/D$;
> - the **secondary maxima** lie where $\tan\beta = \beta$, at $\beta = 1.430\pi,\ 2.459\pi,\ 3.471\pi, \dots$, slightly short of $\frac32\pi, \frac52\pi, \frac72\pi$; their intensities are $0.047I_0$, $0.016I_0$, $0.008I_0$, falling off roughly as $1/\bigl((m + \frac12)\pi\bigr)^2$. The secondary maxima are about half as wide as the central one.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.1–4.2 · PHY 287, week 14 slides*

^thm-a7-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\sin\beta = 0$ for $\beta = m\pi$, and $\beta \neq 0$ there, so $I = 0$; $\beta = m\pi$ means $D\sin\theta = m\lambda$. The first zeros on either side are $m = \pm1$, and for small angles $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, giving $\Delta\theta \approx 2\lambda/D$ and, with $y = L\tan\theta \approx L\theta$, a width $2\lambda L/D$ on the screen. Between zeros, $I$ is extremal where the derivative of $\sin\beta/\beta$ vanishes:
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\beta}\,\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta} = \frac{\beta\cos\beta - \sin\beta}{\beta^2} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tan\beta = \beta ,
> $$
>
> whose nonzero roots, found numerically, are those listed. Near them $\lvert\sin\beta\rvert \approx 1$, so $I/I_0 \approx 1/\beta^2$; at $\beta = \frac32\pi$ this gives $0.045$, close to the exact $0.047$. Neighbouring side zeros are $\pi$ apart in $\beta$, the central ones $2\pi$.

^der-a7-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-3|Theorem §A7.1.3]], [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-2|Theorem §A7.1.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-1|451 Thm. §29.1]]

![[ph-wo-a7-1-2.svg]]
*The single-slit intensity (red), computed from Theorem §A7.1.3, against $D\sin\theta/\lambda$. The central maximum is twice as wide as the others and dominates; the dashed grey curve, the same function magnified ten times, shows the secondary maxima of $4.7\%$ and $1.6\%$ of $I_0$. The zeros fall at the nonzero integers.*

> [!remark] Remark: Wide slits, narrow peaks
> The central peak narrows as the slit widens. For $D \gg \lambda$ it is so narrow that the light goes essentially straight through, casting the sharp shadow of geometric optics ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^mod-a4-1-2|Model §A4.1.2]]). For $D < \lambda$ there is no zero at all, since $\sin\theta = \lambda/D$ would exceed $1$: the slit simply spreads the light over the whole forward half-space. That is why Chapter A6 could treat narrow slits as point sources. In the PHY 300 diffraction lab the relation is used backwards: scanning a photodiode across the pattern of a laser and measuring the distance between the first zeros gives the width of the slit.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 11*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A7.1.1: Slit Width from a Minimum
> Light of $\lambda = 550\ \mathrm{nm}$ produces its second minimum ($m = 2$) at $45.0^\circ$. By [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-2|Theorem §A7.1.2]] the slit width is
>
> $$
> D = \frac{m\lambda}{\sin\theta_2} = \frac{2(550 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm m)}{\sin 45.0^\circ} = 1.56 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm m,
> $$
>
> and the first minimum is at $\sin\theta_1 = \lambda/D = 0.354$, $\theta_1 = 20.7^\circ$. The slit is only a few wavelengths wide, as it must be for a pattern this broad: the central maximum spans about $41^\circ$, while the next maximum, between $20.7^\circ$ and $45.0^\circ$, is only $24^\circ$ wide.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 4.1*

^ex-a7-1-1

> [!example] Example §A7.1.2: Intensity between Two Minima
> Light of $550\ \mathrm{nm}$ passes a slit $D = 2.00\ \mu\mathrm m$ wide. The first two minima are at $\theta_1 = \sin^{-1}(\lambda/D) = 16.0^\circ$ and $\theta_2 = \sin^{-1}(2\lambda/D) = 33.4^\circ$. Halfway between them, at $\theta = 24.7^\circ$,
>
> $$
> \beta = \frac{\pi D\sin\theta}{\lambda} = \frac{\pi(2.00 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm m)\sin 24.7^\circ}{550 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm m} = 4.77\ \mathrm{rad} = 1.52\pi, \qquad \frac{I}{I_0} = \left(\frac{\sin 4.77}{4.77}\right)^2 = 0.044
> $$
>
> by [[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-3|Theorem §A7.1.3]]. The point is close to the first secondary maximum, and the result is close to its $0.047$: the side maxima carry only a few percent of the central intensity.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 4.2*

^ex-a7-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - $\lim_{\beta\to0}\sin\beta/\beta = 1$ is the standard $0/0$ limit ([[§30 L'Hospital's Rule#^ex-30-1|451 Ex. §30.1]]); the side maxima are interior extrema, where the derivative (by the quotient rule) vanishes ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-1|451 Thm. §29.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]]).
> - In general the far-field amplitude is the Fourier transform of the aperture; for a slit that transform is $\sin\beta/\beta$ (Fraunhofer diffraction and Fourier optics, Chapter B11, planned).
