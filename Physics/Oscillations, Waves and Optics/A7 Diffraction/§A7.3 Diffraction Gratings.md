As the number of slits grows, the principal maxima of multiple-slit interference stay in place while they sharpen and brighten, and the secondary maxima fade ([[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference|§A6.2]]). With thousands of slits only a set of sharp, bright lines remains, at angles that depend on the wavelength. This is the diffraction grating, the basic tool for taking light apart into its wavelengths.

**Setup.** A grating ([[§A7.3 Diffraction Gratings#^def-a7-3-1|Def. §A7.3.1]]) is the $N$-slit system of [[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference|§A6.2]] with $N$ large: monochromatic light at normal incidence, coherent across the illuminated lines, far field. The angles of the lines are exact in this model; the width of the orders and the resolving power: [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-7|Theorem §B11.2.7]].

## Gratings

> [!definition] Definition §A7.3.1: Diffraction Grating
> A **diffraction grating** is a large number of evenly spaced, parallel slits, or of parallel reflecting strips. The **grating spacing** $d$ is the distance between neighbouring lines; a grating ruled with $N'$ lines per unit length has $d = 1/N'$. A **transmission grating** is used in transmitted light, a **reflection grating** in reflected light.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.4*

^def-a7-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A7.3.1: Grating Equation
> Light of wavelength $\lambda$ falling normally on a grating of spacing $d$ is sent into sharp bright lines at the angles
>
> $$
> d\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots,
> $$
>
> with $\lvert m\rvert \le d/\lambda$. When many lines are illuminated, these lines are very narrow and very bright and nothing else is visible between them.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.4*

^thm-a7-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The illuminated lines form an $N$-slit array with $N$ very large. Its principal maxima are at $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ ([[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-1|Theorem §A6.2.1]]), and $\lvert\sin\theta\rvert \le 1$ bounds $m$. By [[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]] their height grows as $N^2$ while the zeros flanking each of them close in to $d\sin\theta = (m \pm 1/N)\lambda$. Between principal maxima the secondary maxima stay at a few percent of the principal height or less ([[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^cau-a6-2-1|Caution: How bright are the secondary maxima?]]), and with $1000$ lines per millimetre even a few millimetres of grating puts thousands of lines in the beam: only the principal maxima are seen, as sharp lines.

^der-a7-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Spectra
> For $m \neq 0$ the angle grows with $\lambda$, so a grating spreads light into its wavelengths: white light gives a white central line ($m = 0$, where all wavelengths agree) and a rainbow in each higher order. Because the lines are much sharper than double-slit fringes, closely spaced wavelengths are separated cleanly; gases, for instance, show their characteristic sets of lines (hydrogen's visible lines are at $656$, $486$, $434$ and $410\ \mathrm{nm}$, [[§A2.1 Atomic Spectra and the Nuclear Atom#^law-a2-1-1|QM Law §A2.1.1]]). How close two wavelengths may be and still be separated is the resolving power of the grating, [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-3|Theorem §A7.4.3]]. Gratings select wavelengths in monochromators for medical imaging and in optical-fibre technology.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 19*

^rem-a7-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Gratings in nature and in your wallet
> Regularly spaced reflecting structures act as reflection gratings and send different colours in different directions: the play of colours in opals, in some butterfly wings and in hummingbird feathers is **iridescence**, colour from interference rather than pigment. Diffraction gratings, like thin films and holograms ([[§A7.5 X-Ray Diffraction and Holography#^def-a7-5-2|Def. §A7.5.2]]), serve as security features on bank notes and cards because they are hard to copy.

^rem-a7-3-2

> [!caution] Caution: Overlapping orders
> The visible spectrum, taken from $380$ to $760\ \mathrm{nm}$ ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-3|Law §A4.3.3]]), in order $m$ runs from $\sin\theta = m(380\ \mathrm{nm})/d$ to $m(760\ \mathrm{nm})/d$. Since $2 \times 760 > 3 \times 380$, the second- and third-order spectra overlap: a line seen at some angle may be red light in order $2$ or violet in order $3$. Only the first-order spectrum stands alone.

^cau-a7-3-1

## Example

> [!example] Example §A7.3.1: The Rainbow from a Grating
> White light falls on a grating with $10\,000$ lines per centimetre, so $d = 1.00 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm m$, and the spectrum is caught on a screen $L = 2.00\ \mathrm m$ away. In first order ([[§A7.3 Diffraction Gratings#^thm-a7-3-1|Theorem §A7.3.1]]):
>
> $$
> \sin\theta_V = \frac{380 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm m}{1.00 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm m} = 0.380,\ \ \theta_V = 22.33^\circ; \qquad \sin\theta_R = 0.760,\ \ \theta_R = 49.46^\circ .
> $$
>
> On the screen the ends of the rainbow are at $y = L\tan\theta$: $y_V = 0.822\ \mathrm m$ and $y_R = 2.339\ \mathrm m$, so the first-order rainbow is $1.517\ \mathrm m$ long. (University Physics prints $y_V = 0.815\ \mathrm m$ and a length of $1.523\ \mathrm m$; $2.00\tan 22.33^\circ = 0.822$.) The highest order is $\lvert m\rvert \le d/\lambda$: $2.63$ for violet but only $1.32$ for red, so the second-order spectrum is cut off before its red end, and there is no third order. A large spread per unit wavelength is what makes such a grating a good spectroscopic tool.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 4.5*
