[[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width|§B10.2]] compared a field with itself a little later. This section compares the fields at two different points at the same time, and the answer depends on the size of the source. Young lit his two slits through a single pinhole because light from a broad source is not coherent across a double slit ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^rem-a6-1-1|Remark: Why two lamps give no fringes]]). Here that is made quantitative. The transverse coherence width of a source of angular size $\theta_s$ is about $\lambda/\theta_s$, and the van Cittert–Zernike theorem gives the exact dependence as a Fourier transform of the source, the same integral as a Fraunhofer diffraction pattern ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-1|Theorem §A7.4.1]]). Measuring that width with movable slits gives the angular diameters of stars. The second half turns [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-4|Theorem §B10.2.4]] around: recording the fringes of a Michelson interferometer and transforming them gives the spectrum of the light, which is Fourier-transform spectroscopy.

## Spatial coherence

> [!definition] Definition §B10.3.1: Longitudinal and Transverse Coherence; Transverse Coherence Width
> Consider the fields $E_1$, $E_2$ at two points $P_1$, $P_2$ in the light of a source, compared by $\gamma_{12}$ ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]]).
> - If $P_1$ and $P_2$ lie on one ray from a point source, at distances differing by $r_{12}$, $E_2$ is $E_1$ delayed by $r_{12}/c$. Their coherence, the **longitudinal coherence**, is the temporal coherence $\lvert\gamma(r_{12}/c)\rvert$ of [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width|§B10.2]], high when $r_{12} \ll l_c$.
> - If $P_1$ and $P_2$ are at the same distance from the source and a distance $l$ apart sideways, their coherence $\lvert\gamma_{12}(0)\rvert$ is the **transverse** (lateral) **coherence**. For a true point source it is $1$. It falls below $1$ only because the source is extended.
>
> The **transverse coherence width** $l_t$ is the separation at which $\lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert$ first falls to zero. It measures the width of the region over which the field is laterally coherent.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.7, Fig. 3.12, eqs. (3.42)–(3.43)*

^def-b10-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: What spatial coherence means in the lab
> Put the two slits of a Young experiment at $P_1$ and $P_2$, and look at the screen where the paths from the slits are equal. The two slit fields have equal intensity, and by [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]] the fringe visibility there is $\lvert\gamma_{12}(0)\rvert$. Transverse coherence is thus the visibility of Young's fringes as a function of slit separation. The total illumination of the slit plane is uniform. What varies with $l$ is the correlation between the fields, not the intensity.

^rem-b10-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.3.1: Coherence in the Light of Two Incoherent Point Sources
> Two identical, mutually incoherent, quasi-monochromatic point sources $S_a$, $S_b$ (mean wavelength $\lambda$), a distance $s$ apart, illuminate two points $P_1$, $P_2$ a distance $l$ apart along the direction of $S_aS_b$. The points lie at mean distance $r \gg s, l$, with $P_1$ equidistant from the sources. If the path differences involved are much less than the coherence length,
>
> $$
> \lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert = \Bigl\lvert\cos\frac{\pi s l}{\lambda r}\Bigr\rvert = \Bigl\lvert\cos\frac{\pi\theta_sl}{\lambda}\Bigr\rvert, \qquad \theta_s = \frac sr,
> $$
>
> where $\theta_s$ is the angle the two sources subtend. The transverse coherence first vanishes at
>
> $$
> l_t = \frac{r\lambda}{2s} = \frac{\lambda}{2\theta_s} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.7, eqs. (3.37)–(3.43), Figs. 3.13–3.14*

^thm-b10-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $E_1 = E_{1a} + E_{1b}$ and $E_2 = E_{2a} + E_{2b}$ for the contributions of the two sources. Cross terms such as $\langle E_{1a}E_{2b}^*\rangle$ vanish because the sources are incoherent, so
>
> $$
> \Gamma_{12}(0) = \langle E_{1a}E_{2a}^*\rangle + \langle E_{1b}E_{2b}^*\rangle .
> $$
>
> Far away, the field of $S_a$ at $P_2$ equals its field at $P_1$ delayed by $t_a = (r_{2a} - r_{1a})/c$, with $r_{ja}$ the distance from $S_a$ to $P_j$, and with essentially the same amplitude. So $\langle E_{1a}(t)E_{1a}^*(t - t_a)\rangle = I_a\gamma(-t_a) \approx I_ae^{-i\omega_0t_a}$, using $\gamma(\tau) \approx e^{i\omega_0\tau}$ for $\lvert\tau\rvert \ll \tau_0$ ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]]). The same holds for $S_b$. With $I_a = I_b$ and $I_1 = I_2 = 2I_a$,
>
> $$
> \gamma_{12} = \tfrac12\bigl(e^{-i\omega_0t_a} + e^{-i\omega_0t_b}\bigr), \qquad \lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert = \Bigl\lvert\cos\frac{\omega_0(t_a - t_b)}{2}\Bigr\rvert .
> $$
>
> Since $P_1$ is equidistant from the sources, $c(t_a - t_b) = r_{2a} - r_{2b}$. That is the path difference from two "slits" a distance $s$ apart to a point at angle $l/r$, namely $s\,l/r$ in the small-angle limit ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]]). With $\omega_0 = 2\pi c/\lambda$ the argument of the cosine is $\pi sl/(\lambda r)$, which first reaches $\pi/2$ at $l = r\lambda/2s$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.3.2: Van Cittert–Zernike Theorem (Far-Field Form)
> An extended, incoherent, quasi-monochromatic source of small angular size has intensity $I(\boldsymbol\alpha)$ per unit solid angle at angular position $\boldsymbol\alpha = (\alpha_x, \alpha_y)$, as seen from a distant observation plane. For two points of that plane separated by $\Delta\boldsymbol\rho$, with all path differences much less than the coherence length,
>
> $$
> \lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert = \frac{\Bigl\lvert\displaystyle\iint I(\boldsymbol\alpha)\,e^{ik\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\Delta\boldsymbol\rho}\,d^2\alpha\Bigr\rvert}{\displaystyle\iint I(\boldsymbol\alpha)\,d^2\alpha}, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}:
> $$
>
> the degree of coherence is the normalised two-dimensional Fourier transform of the source's angular intensity distribution. In Fowles's words, it equals the complex amplitude that a spherical wave converging on $P_1$ through an aperture of the size and shape of the source produces at $P_2$, which is a Fraunhofer diffraction pattern ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]]). For a source of angular size $\theta_s$ and $u = l\theta_s/\lambda$:
>
> | Source | $\lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert$ | $l_t$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | two points an angle $\theta_s$ apart | $\lvert\cos\pi u\rvert$ | $\lambda/2\theta_s$ |
> | uniform strip of angular width $\theta_s$ ($l$ across the strip) | $\lvert\sin\pi u/\pi u\rvert$ | $\lambda/\theta_s$ |
> | uniform disk of angular diameter $\theta_s$ | $\lvert 2J_1(\pi u)/\pi u\rvert$ | $1.22\,\lambda/\theta_s$ |
>
> Here $J_1$ is the Bessel function of order one, whose first zero is at $3.832 = 1.22\pi$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.7 ("Extended Sources. The Van Cittert–Zernike Theorem", stated without proof), eq. (3.44); the far-field form, its derivation and the strip case are worked out here*

^thm-b10-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Divide the source into elements $d^2\alpha$, mutually incoherent. As in [[§B10.3 Spatial Coherence and Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy#^thm-b10-3-1|Theorem §B10.3.1]], cross terms between elements vanish, and each element contributes $I(\boldsymbol\alpha)\,d^2\alpha\;e^{-ik(r_2 - r_1)}$ to $\Gamma_{12}(0)$, where $r_j$ is its distance to $P_j$. Let the source be at distance $R$, with the element at transverse position $\boldsymbol\sigma = R\boldsymbol\alpha$, and let $P_j$ have transverse position $\boldsymbol\rho_j$. Expanding $r_j = \sqrt{R^2 + \lvert\boldsymbol\rho_j - \boldsymbol\sigma\rvert^2} \approx R + \lvert\boldsymbol\rho_j - \boldsymbol\sigma\rvert^2/2R$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]),
>
> $$
> r_2 - r_1 \approx \frac{\lvert\boldsymbol\rho_2\rvert^2 - \lvert\boldsymbol\rho_1\rvert^2}{2R} - \boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\Delta\boldsymbol\rho .
> $$
>
> The first term is the same for every element. It contributes an overall phase factor to $\Gamma_{12}$ and drops out of $\lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert$. Summing over the elements and dividing by the total intensity gives the formula.
>
> The cases: two points at $\boldsymbol\alpha = \pm\tfrac12\theta_s\hat{\mathbf x}$ give $\tfrac12\bigl(e^{ik\theta_sl/2} + e^{-ik\theta_sl/2}\bigr) = \cos(\pi u)$, which is [[§B10.3 Spatial Coherence and Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy#^thm-b10-3-1|Theorem §B10.3.1]] again. A strip gives $\frac{1}{\theta_s}\int_{-\theta_s/2}^{\theta_s/2}e^{ik\alpha l}\,d\alpha = \frac{\sin(k\theta_sl/2)}{k\theta_sl/2} = \frac{\sin\pi u}{\pi u}$, first zero at $u = 1$. For a disk of angular radius $a = \theta_s/2$, the integral $\iint_{\lvert\boldsymbol\alpha\rvert < a}e^{ik\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\Delta\boldsymbol\rho}\,d^2\alpha = \pi a^2\cdot 2J_1(kal)/(kal)$ is the integral that gives the Airy pattern of a circular aperture ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]], where $J_1$ is given as a series), and $kal = \pi u$. Its first zero, at $\pi u = 3.832$, is the $1.22$ of [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-1|Theorem §A7.4.1]].

> [!remark] Remark: Shaping a source for a double-slit experiment
> - The slits of a Young experiment must be closer than $l_t$ for good fringes. A smaller pinhole raises $l_t$ but passes less light, and the experimenter balances the two.
> - A long, thin source (a straight lamp filament, or a slit) has a large coherence width across its narrow dimension and a tiny one along its length. The two slits should therefore be parallel to the filament and separated across it. Each point of one slit is then coherent with the facing point of the other, and the different points along the slits add their fringe patterns in intensity, all in register (Fowles, Problem 3.11).
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.7 and Problem 3.11*

## Measuring stars

> [!remark] Remark: Stellar interferometry
> - **Michelson's stellar interferometer.** Two mirrors in front of a telescope, a variable distance $l$ apart, feed light from a star into the telescope, which forms Young's fringes in its focal plane. As $l$ grows the fringes fade, and they vanish at $l = l_t = 1.22\lambda/\theta_s$ ([[§B10.3 Spatial Coherence and Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy#^thm-b10-3-2|Theorem §B10.3.2]]), which gives the star's angular diameter $\theta_s$. The mirrors can be much farther apart than the telescope aperture, and that is what beats the telescope's own diffraction limit.
> - **Intensity interferometry** (Hanbury Brown and Twiss). Two separate light collectors (searchlight mirrors were used) focus the starlight onto photocells. The product of the two photocurrents is averaged electronically, measuring $\langle\lvert E_1\rvert^2\lvert E_2\rvert^2\rangle$, the **second-order coherence**. It too depends on the separation of the collectors through the lateral coherence width, so it gives the same angular diameter. It needs no optical-quality components and no stable path lengths, because no optical phases are compared, and it reached stars smaller than Michelson's method could resolve.
> - **Radio interferometers.** Radio antennas record the wave's amplitude and phase directly, not just its intensity, so signals from separate dishes can be combined after the fact. A pair of dishes a distance $d$ apart resolves two sources whose phase difference across the pair, $2\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda$, is about $\pi$, i.e. $\theta \approx \lambda/2d$. That is the separation at which the two-point coherence of [[§B10.3 Spatial Coherence and Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy#^thm-b10-3-1|Theorem §B10.3.1]] first vanishes. The Very Large Array in New Mexico (27 dishes of 25 m, baselines up to 22 miles, about 35 km) works this way.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.7 ("Measurement of Stellar Diameters") and §3.8 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 18 §5, eq. (30)*

## Fourier-transform spectroscopy

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.3.3: Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy
> Light with power spectrum $G(k)$, $k = \omega/c = 2\pi/\lambda$, is divided into two equal beams in a Michelson interferometer. The intensity $I(x)$ at the output is recorded as a function of the path difference $x$. Each wave-number component contributes $(1 + \cos kx)\,G(k)\,dk$, so that $I(x) = \int_0^\infty(1 + \cos kx)\,G(k)\,dk$. Extend $G$ to $k < 0$ by $G(-k) = G(k)$ and define the **interferogram** $W(x) = 2I(x) - I(0)$. Then $W$ and $G$ are a Fourier-transform pair:
>
> $$
> W(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk, \qquad G(k) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}W(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx .
> $$
>
> Recording $I(x)$ and computing the transform gives the spectrum.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.9, eqs. (3.45)–(3.46), Figs. 3.18–3.19*

^thm-b10-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $x = c\tau$ and $G(k)\,dk$ the intensity in $[k, k + dk]$, [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-4|Theorem §B10.2.4]] is $I(x) = \int_0^\infty(1 + \cos kx)\,G(k)\,dk$, normalised so that $I(0) = 2\int_0^\infty G\,dk$. Then
>
> $$
> 2I(x) - I(0) = 2\int_0^\infty G(k)\cos kx\,dk = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk,
> $$
>
> because the even extension makes the $\sin kx$ part of $e^{ikx}$ integrate to zero and doubles the cosine part. This is the synthesis formula of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|Def. §B4.4.1]] with $x$ for $t$ and $k$ for $\omega$, so the inversion theorem ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|Theorem §B4.4.1]]) gives $G$ as the forward transform of $W$. Both $W$ and $G$ are real and even, so the sign convention of the exponent does not matter here.

^der-b10-3-3

*Uses:* [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-4|Theorem §B10.2.4]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|Def. §B4.4.1]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|Theorem §B4.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Resolution and advantages
> - **Resolution.** A scan that stops at path difference $X$ knows $W$ only on $[-X, X]$. The computed spectrum is then the true one smeared by the transform of that window, a sinc of first zero $\delta k = \pi/X$: the tone burst of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-5|Theorem §B4.4.5]], part 2, with $x$ and $k$ for $t$ and $\omega$. Two lines need roughly that separation, $\delta(1/\lambda) \approx 1/2X$, to be resolved, so the resolving power is $\lambda/\delta\lambda \approx 2X/\lambda$, twice the number of fringes recorded. Long scans resolve fine detail, by the same duration–bandwidth trade as in time. (This estimate is derived here, not in Fowles.)
> - **Advantages.** Every wavelength is recorded throughout the scan, and the instrument needs no narrow slit, so it uses the available light efficiently. That makes it the method of choice for weak sources and for the dense infrared absorption spectra of gases. The transform is done numerically.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.9*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B10.3.1: Coherence Width of a Pinhole and of the Sun
> By [[§B10.3 Spatial Coherence and Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy#^thm-b10-3-2|Theorem §B10.3.2]], a uniform disk of angular diameter $\theta_s$ gives $l_t = 1.22\lambda/\theta_s$.
> - A pinhole of diameter $1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$ seen from $1.0\ \mathrm m$ ($\theta_s = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad}$), with $\lambda = 600\ \mathrm{nm}$: $l_t = 1.22(600 \times 10^{-9})/(1.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 0.73\ \mathrm{mm}$. The slits of a Young experiment $1\ \mathrm m$ beyond it must be less than about $0.7\ \mathrm{mm}$ apart. A $0.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ pinhole $0.5\ \mathrm m$ away with sodium light ($590\ \mathrm{nm}$) gives the same angle and $l_t = 0.72\ \mathrm{mm}$.
> - Sunlight: the Sun subtends $\theta_s = 0.5^\circ = 8.7 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad}$, so with $\lambda = 600\ \mathrm{nm}$, $l_t = 1.22(600 \times 10^{-9})/(8.7 \times 10^{-3}) = 84\ \mathrm{\mu m}$. Direct sunlight is coherent only over patches about a tenth of a millimetre across. Young's sunlit double slit needed a pinhole in front of it, and the pinhole must be small.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.7 (pinhole) and Problems 3.9 (Sun), 3.10 (sodium pinhole)*

^ex-b10-3-1

> [!example] Example §B10.3.2: The Diameter of Betelgeuse
> Michelson's stellar interferometer gave Betelgeuse an angular diameter of $0.047''$. In radians, $\theta_s = 0.047/3600 \times \pi/180 = 2.28 \times 10^{-7}$. Taking $\lambda \approx 550\ \mathrm{nm}$, where the eye is most sensitive, the fringes vanish at a mirror separation
>
> $$
> l_t = \frac{1.22\lambda}{\theta_s} = \frac{1.22\,(550 \times 10^{-9}\ \mathrm m)}{2.28 \times 10^{-7}} = 2.9\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> That is why the mirrors had to be mounted on a beam several metres long, wider than the telescope. Read the other way: the separation at which the fringes disappeared, together with the wavelength, gave $\theta_s$, and the star's known distance then gave a linear diameter of about $280$ solar diameters. A single telescope would need an aperture of this order to resolve the disk by its own diffraction limit ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-1|Theorem §A7.4.1]]); the interferometer obtains the information from two small mirrors.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.7 ("Measurement of Stellar Diameters"); wavelength chosen here*

^ex-b10-3-2

> [!example] Example §B10.3.3: The Sodium Doublet in a Michelson Interferometer
> Sodium light consists of two lines of nearly equal strength at $\lambda_1 = 589.00\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $\lambda_2 = 589.59\ \mathrm{nm}$ ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^ex-a5-4-1|QM Example §A5.4.1]]). Treat each line as infinitely narrow, $G(k) = \tfrac12I_0\bigl[\delta(k - k_1) + \delta(k - k_2)\bigr]$ for $k > 0$. By [[§B10.3 Spatial Coherence and Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy#^thm-b10-3-3|Theorem §B10.3.3]],
>
> $$
> W(x) = I_0\bigl(\cos k_1x + \cos k_2x\bigr) = 2I_0\cos\frac{\Delta k\,x}{2}\,\cos\bar kx, \qquad \Delta k = k_1 - k_2 = \frac{2\pi\,\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_1\lambda_2} .
> $$
>
> The fringes $\cos\bar kx$ are modulated by the slow envelope $\cos(\Delta k\,x/2)$: beats in path difference ([[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]). The visibility $\lvert\cos(\Delta k\,x/2)\rvert$ vanishes first at
>
> $$
> x = \frac{\pi}{\Delta k} = \frac{\lambda_1\lambda_2}{2\,\Delta\lambda} = \frac{(589.00)(589.59)\ \mathrm{nm}^2}{2\,(0.59\ \mathrm{nm})} = 0.294\ \mathrm{mm},
> $$
>
> i.e. after $0.147\ \mathrm{mm}$ of mirror travel, and then every $0.589\ \mathrm{mm}$ of path ($0.294\ \mathrm{mm}$ of mirror travel). Between two visibility minima, $\lambda_1\lambda_2/(\Delta\lambda\,\bar\lambda) \approx \bar\lambda/\Delta\lambda = 999$ fringes pass. Counting them measures $\Delta\lambda = \bar\lambda/999 = 0.59\ \mathrm{nm}$ without any spectrometer. As a Fourier-transform spectrometer, the instrument separates the two lines once the scan reaches about $X \approx 0.3\ \mathrm{mm}$. That agrees with the resolution estimate $\lambda/\delta\lambda \approx 2X/\lambda$ ([[§B10.3 Spatial Coherence and Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy#^rem-b10-3-4|Remark: Resolution and advantages]]), which asks for $2X/\lambda \approx 1000$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.9, Fig. 3.19(c) (the interferogram of two narrow lines); line wavelengths from QM Example §A5.4.1*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Both halves of the section are Fourier transforms ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function|§B4.4]]). Transverse coherence is the transform of the source's angular intensity in the variable $k\,\Delta\boldsymbol\rho$, and the interferogram is the transform of the spectrum in the path difference. A wide source gives narrow coherence, and a broad spectrum a short interferogram, by the duration–bandwidth relation ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-5|Theorem §B4.4.5]]).
> - The disk integral with $J_1$, and the general link between apertures and Fourier transforms, belong to Fraunhofer diffraction ([[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-1|Theorem §B11.2.1]], [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]]).
> - The second-order coherence measured by Hanbury Brown and Twiss behaves differently for thermal light and for laser light. The difference is a statement about photon statistics (quantum optics, not covered at this level).
