Level A treats coherence as all or nothing: two sources either keep a fixed phase relation or do not ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]]). Real light lies in between. Its phase drifts, so a beam stays coherent with a delayed copy of itself only for delays up to some **coherence time**, and fringes fade as the path difference in an interferometer grows. This section measures the effect by a correlation function whose modulus is the fringe visibility of [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^def-b10-1-1|Def. §B10.1.1]]. It computes that function for Fowles's model of a source with random phase jumps, and shows that the function is the Fourier transform of the spectrum. The relation "coherence time ≈ 1/line width" is then the duration–bandwidth relation of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-5|Theorem §B4.4.5]]. Fields are complex, in the optics convention ([[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^cau-b10-1-1|Caution: The optics sign convention]]), and all beams share one polarization.

## Partial coherence

> [!definition] Definition §B10.2.1: Mutual Coherence Function; Degree of Partial Coherence
> Let $E_1(t)$ and $E_2(t)$ be the complex fields of two beams, each as it would arrive at a detector with the other blocked. Assume the light is **stationary**: time averages
>
> $$
> \langle f\rangle = \lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T} f(t)\,dt
> $$
>
> do not depend on the choice of the origin of time. The **mutual coherence function** and the **degree of partial coherence** are
>
> $$
> \Gamma_{12}(\tau) = \bigl\langle E_1(t)\,E_2^*(t + \tau)\bigr\rangle, \qquad \gamma_{12}(\tau) = \frac{\Gamma_{12}(\tau)}{\sqrt{I_1I_2}}, \qquad I_j = \langle\lvert E_j\rvert^2\rangle .
> $$
>
> For one field with itself, $\Gamma_{11}(\tau)$ is the **self-coherence** (autocorrelation) function, with $\Gamma_{11}(0) = I_1$, and $\gamma(\tau) = \Gamma_{11}(\tau)/I_1$ is the **degree of temporal coherence**. The light is **coherent** if $\lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert = 1$, **partially coherent** if $0 < \lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert < 1$, and **incoherent** if $\gamma_{12} = 0$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.4, eqs. (3.12)–(3.16)*

^def-b10-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Order of the factors
> Fowles puts the later time on the conjugated field, $\langle E_1(t)E_2^*(t + \tau)\rangle$. With $e^{-i\omega t}$ fields this makes $\gamma(\tau) \propto e^{+i\bar\omega\tau}$. Many optics texts use $\langle E_1(t + \tau)E_2^*(t)\rangle$ instead, which is the complex conjugate, $\propto e^{-i\bar\omega\tau}$. The modulus $\lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert$, which is all an intensity measurement sees, is the same.

^cau-b10-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.1: Interference of Partially Coherent Beams
> Let beam 1 travel a longer path than beam 2, arriving a time $\tau$ later. The time-averaged intensity at the detector is
>
> $$
> I = I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\,\operatorname{Re}\gamma_{12}(\tau), \qquad \lvert\gamma_{12}(\tau)\rvert \le 1 .
> $$
>
> For quasi-monochromatic light of mean frequency $\bar\omega$, $\gamma_{12}(\tau) = \lvert\gamma_{12}(\tau)\rvert e^{i(\bar\omega\tau + \alpha)}$ with a modulus and phase $\alpha$ that change little over one period $2\pi/\bar\omega$. Scanning $\tau$ then produces fringes with
>
> $$
> I_{\max,\min} = I_1 + I_2 \pm 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\,\lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert, \qquad \mathcal V = \frac{2\sqrt{I_1I_2}}{I_1 + I_2}\,\lvert\gamma_{12}(\tau)\rvert, \qquad \mathcal V = \lvert\gamma_{12}(\tau)\rvert\ \text{ if } I_1 = I_2 .
> $$
>
> This generalises [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]], which is the case $\lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert = 1$ (same polarization).
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.4, eqs. (3.11)–(3.21)*

^thm-b10-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The detector field is $E_1(t - \tau) + E_2(t)$. Time-averaging the square as in [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]],
>
> $$
> I = \langle\lvert E_1(t - \tau)\rvert^2\rangle + \langle\lvert E_2(t)\rvert^2\rangle + 2\operatorname{Re}\bigl\langle E_1(t - \tau)E_2^*(t)\bigr\rangle .
> $$
>
> By stationarity the first average is $I_1$, and shifting $t \to t + \tau$ turns the cross term into $\langle E_1(t)E_2^*(t + \tau)\rangle = \Gamma_{12}(\tau) = \sqrt{I_1I_2}\,\gamma_{12}(\tau)$. The time average $\langle f\,g^*\rangle$ is linear in $f$, conjugate-linear in $g$, and $\langle f f^*\rangle \ge 0$, so it is an inner product (more precisely a semi-inner product) on signals. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality holds for it ([[§17 Cauchy–Schwarz and the Induced Norm#^thm-17-1|556 Thm. §17.1]]; the proof uses only these properties): $\lvert\langle E_1(t)E_2^*(t + \tau)\rangle\rvert^2 \le \langle\lvert E_1\rvert^2\rangle\,\langle\lvert E_2\rvert^2\rangle = I_1I_2$, so $\lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert \le 1$. With $\gamma_{12} = \lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert e^{i(\bar\omega\tau + \alpha)}$, a change of $\tau$ by one period sweeps $\operatorname{Re}\gamma_{12}$ from $+\lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert$ to $-\lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert$ while the modulus stays put. That gives $I_{\max,\min}$, and $\mathcal V$ follows from [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^def-b10-1-1|Def. §B10.1.1]].

## A model source

> [!model] Model §B10.2.2: Quasi-Monochromatic Source with Random Phase Jumps
> The source field is
>
> $$
> E(t) = E_0\,e^{-i\omega_0t}\,e^{i\varphi(t)},
> $$
>
> with constant amplitude $E_0$ and a phase $\varphi(t)$ that is a random step function. It stays constant for an interval of length $\tau_0$, then jumps to a new value, uniformly distributed in $[0, 2\pi)$ and independent of all earlier values. The field is a sequence of perfectly sinusoidal wave trains of duration $\tau_0$ whose phases are unrelated to one another. It idealises an atom whose emission is interrupted by collisions, each collision resetting the phase. A real source has random interval lengths with some mean, and a fluctuating amplitude.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.5, eq. (3.22), Fig. 3.8*

^mod-b10-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.3: Degree of Coherence of Wave Trains with Random Phases
> For the source of [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^mod-b10-2-2|Model §B10.2.2]],
>
> $$
> \gamma(\tau) = \begin{cases} \Bigl(1 - \dfrac{\lvert\tau\rvert}{\tau_0}\Bigr)e^{i\omega_0\tau}, & \lvert\tau\rvert < \tau_0,\\[2mm] 0, & \lvert\tau\rvert \ge \tau_0 . \end{cases}
> $$
>
> In a two-beam interferometer with equal beams, the fringe visibility $\mathcal V = 1 - \lvert\tau\rvert/\tau_0$ falls linearly with the delay and is zero once the path difference exceeds $c\tau_0$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.5, eqs. (3.23)–(3.27), Figs. 3.9–3.10*

^thm-b10-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]], $\gamma(\tau) = \langle E(t)E^*(t + \tau)\rangle/E_0^2 = e^{i\omega_0\tau}\bigl\langle e^{i[\varphi(t) - \varphi(t + \tau)]}\bigr\rangle$. Take $0 \le \tau < \tau_0$ and average over one interval of constant phase, of length $\tau_0$.
> - For the first $\tau_0 - \tau$ of it, $t$ and $t + \tau$ lie in the same interval, the phases cancel, and the integrand is $1$.
> - For the last $\tau$ of it, $t + \tau$ lies in the next interval, and the integrand is $e^{i\Delta}$, with $\Delta$ the random jump.
>
> The interval therefore contributes $(1 - \tau/\tau_0) + (\tau/\tau_0)\,e^{i\Delta}$. Averaging over many intervals, the first part is the same every time, while $e^{i\Delta}$, with $\Delta$ uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$ and independent from interval to interval, averages to $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i\Delta}\,d\Delta = 0$. Hence $\gamma(\tau) = (1 - \tau/\tau_0)e^{i\omega_0\tau}$. For $\tau \ge \tau_0$ the two times always lie in different intervals, and everything averages to zero. For $\tau < 0$, stationarity gives $\gamma(-\tau) = \langle E(t)E^*(t - \tau)\rangle/E_0^2 = \langle E(t + \tau)E^*(t)\rangle/E_0^2 = \gamma(\tau)^*$, which replaces $\tau$ by $\lvert\tau\rvert$ in the modulus. The visibility is [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]] with $I_1 = I_2$.

^der-b10-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]], [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^mod-b10-2-2|Model §B10.2.2]], [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §B10.2.2: Coherence Time; Coherence Length
> The **coherence time** $\tau_0$ of a light source is the time over which the phase of its field stays predictable. In [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^mod-b10-2-2|Model §B10.2.2]] it is the length of the wave trains; for real sources it is their average length, or the delay at which $\lvert\gamma(\tau)\rvert$ has become small. The **coherence length** is
>
> $$
> l_c = c\,\tau_0,
> $$
>
> the length of an uninterrupted wave train. Two beams split from the source give visible fringes only if their path difference is less than about $l_c$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.5*

## Coherence and the spectrum

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.4: Degree of Coherence and the Power Spectrum
> Let a stationary field have the power spectrum $G(\omega)$, the intensity per unit angular frequency, defined for $\omega > 0$. Its degree of temporal coherence is the normalised Fourier transform of the spectrum:
>
> $$
> \gamma(\tau) = \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^\infty G(\omega)\,e^{i\omega\tau}\,d\omega}{\displaystyle\int_0^\infty G(\omega)\,d\omega} .
> $$
>
> In a two-beam interferometer with equal beams ($I_1 = I_2$, total $I(0)$ at zero delay), the intensity is therefore
>
> $$
> I(\tau) = \frac{I(0)}{2}\Bigl[1 + \frac{\int_0^\infty G(\omega)\cos\omega\tau\,d\omega}{\int_0^\infty G(\omega)\,d\omega}\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Each frequency makes its own fringe pattern $1 + \cos\omega\tau$, and the patterns add in intensity. The visibility at delay $\tau$ depends only on the distribution of intensity over frequency, not on any phases. Conversely, by Fourier inversion ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|Theorem §B4.4.1]]) the normalised spectrum is $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\gamma(\tau)e^{-i\omega\tau}\,d\tau$: the transform of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^def-b4-4-1|Def. §B4.4.1]] with $\tau$ in place of $t$, whose synthesis uses $e^{+i\omega\tau}$ exactly as here.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.9, eq. (3.45) (the interferogram as a sum over the spectrum) and §3.6 · statement for $\gamma$ derived here*

^thm-b10-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Represent the field over a long time as a sum of many monochromatic components, $E(t) = \sum_ja_je^{-i\omega_jt}$, $a_j = \lvert a_j\rvert e^{i\phi_j}$, with phases $\phi_j$ that are random and independent of one another. That different frequencies are uncorrelated is what stationarity of chaotic light amounts to; Fowles's interferogram (3.45) assumes it. Then
>
> $$
> \bigl\langle E(t)E^*(t + \tau)\bigr\rangle = \sum_{j,l}a_ja_l^*\,e^{i\omega_l\tau}\,\bigl\langle e^{i(\omega_l - \omega_j)t}\bigr\rangle = \sum_j\lvert a_j\rvert^2e^{i\omega_j\tau},
> $$
>
> because the time average of $e^{i(\omega_l - \omega_j)t}$ is $0$ for $\omega_l \ne \omega_j$ (and the products $a_ja_l^*$ average to zero over the random phases in any case). At $\tau = 0$ the sum is $\sum\lvert a_j\rvert^2 = I$. Collecting the components in $[\omega, \omega + d\omega]$ into $G(\omega)\,d\omega = \sum\lvert a_j\rvert^2$ turns both sums into integrals, and dividing gives $\gamma(\tau)$. With $I_1 = I_2 = I(0)/4$, [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]] gives $I(\tau) = \frac{I(0)}{2}\bigl[1 + \operatorname{Re}\gamma(\tau)\bigr]$, and $\operatorname{Re}e^{i\omega\tau} = \cos\omega\tau$. Intensities, not amplitudes, of different frequencies add, as for incoherent sources ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^rem-a6-1-2|Remark: Interference moves energy]]).

^der-b10-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^def-b10-2-1|Def. §B10.2.1]], [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]], [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|Theorem §B4.4.1]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^rem-a6-1-2|Remark: Interference moves energy]]

> [!remark] Remark: The Wiener–Khinchin theorem
> Theorem §B10.2.4 is the optical form of the **Wiener–Khinchin theorem**: the autocorrelation function of a stationary random signal and its power spectrum are a Fourier-transform pair. The derivation above, by a sum over independent components, is the physicist's version. A proof for general stationary processes needs probability theory and is not given here (Mathematical Methods, planned; statistical optics, Chapter C3, planned). For the triangle of [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]] it can be checked directly: the transform of $1 - \lvert\tau\rvert/\tau_0$ is a $\mathrm{sinc}^2$, the power spectrum of one wave train of length $\tau_0$ (Theorem §B10.2.5 below). A random sequence of such trains has the same power spectrum as one of them, because the cross terms between trains of unrelated phase average away.

^rem-b10-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.2.5: Coherence Time and Line Width
> A source whose spectral line has width $\Delta\nu$ (in ordinary frequency, $\Delta\omega = 2\pi\Delta\nu$) has a coherence time and coherence length
>
> $$
> \tau_0 \approx \frac{1}{\Delta\nu}, \qquad l_c = c\tau_0 \approx \frac{c}{\Delta\nu} = \frac{\bar\lambda^2}{\Delta\lambda},
> $$
>
> where $\Delta\lambda$ is the line width in wavelength. Precisely, for three line shapes:
> 1. **Wave trains of length $\tau_0$** ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^mod-b10-2-2|Model §B10.2.2]]): $G(\omega) \propto \mathrm{sinc}^2\bigl[(\omega - \omega_0)\tau_0/2\bigr]$, with $\mathrm{sinc}\,x = \sin x/x$. Its first zeros are at $\omega_0 \pm 2\pi/\tau_0$, so the half-width to the first zero is $\Delta\nu = 1/\tau_0$ exactly (its FWHM is $0.886/\tau_0$), and $\lvert\gamma\rvert = 1 - \lvert\tau\rvert/\tau_0$ reaches $0$ at $\tau = 1/\Delta\nu$.
> 2. **Lorentzian line** of FWHM $\Delta\nu$ ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^def-b1-4-1|Def. §B1.4.1]]): $\lvert\gamma(\tau)\rvert = e^{-\pi\Delta\nu\lvert\tau\rvert}$.
> 3. **Gaussian line** of FWHM $\Delta\nu$: $\lvert\gamma(\tau)\rvert = \exp\bigl[-(\pi\Delta\nu\,\tau)^2/(4\ln 2)\bigr]$.
>
> In every case the visibility is small, a few percent or zero, once the path difference reaches $c/\Delta\nu$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.6, eqs. (3.29)–(3.36) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 11 §1 (a pulse of width $\sigma_t$ needs a band $\sigma_\omega = 1/\sigma_t$); cases 2 and 3 computed here*

^thm-b10-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-4|Theorem §B10.2.4]] and its inversion, the normalised spectrum is $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\gamma(\tau)e^{-i\omega\tau}\,d\tau$. A spectrum of width $\Delta\omega$ is therefore paired with a $\gamma$ of width of order $1/\Delta\omega$ in $\tau$, by the duration–bandwidth relations ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-5|Theorem §B4.4.5]]). The three cases:
>
> 1. With $\gamma = (1 - \lvert\tau\rvert/\tau_0)e^{i\omega_0\tau}$ on $\lvert\tau\rvert < \tau_0$ ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-3|Theorem §B10.2.3]]) and $u = \omega - \omega_0$,
> $$
> \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\tau_0}^{\tau_0}\Bigl(1 - \frac{\lvert\tau\rvert}{\tau_0}\Bigr)e^{-iu\tau}\,d\tau = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\tau_0}\Bigl(1 - \frac{\tau}{\tau_0}\Bigr)\cos u\tau\,d\tau = \frac{1 - \cos u\tau_0}{\pi u^2\tau_0} = \frac{\tau_0}{2\pi}\,\mathrm{sinc}^2\frac{u\tau_0}{2},
> $$
> integrating by parts once and using $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2(x/2)$. This is $\lvert\tilde f\rvert^2$ for the tone burst of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-5|Theorem §B4.4.5]] (part 2), up to a constant: Fowles's eq. (3.31). Its first zeros are at $u\tau_0/2 = \pm\pi$. The FWHM follows from $\mathrm{sinc}^2x = \tfrac12$ at $x = 1.392$: $\Delta\omega = 4(1.392)/\tau_0$, i.e. $0.886/\tau_0$ in $\nu$.
> 2. $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{-a\lvert\tau\rvert}e^{-iu\tau}\,d\tau = \frac{1}{2\pi}\Bigl[\frac{1}{a + iu} + \frac{1}{a - iu}\Bigr] = \frac{a/\pi}{a^2 + u^2}$, a normalised Lorentzian of FWHM $2a$ in $\omega$. So by inversion a Lorentzian line of FWHM $2\pi\Delta\nu$ has $\gamma = e^{i\omega_0\tau}e^{-\pi\Delta\nu\lvert\tau\rvert}$.
> 3. By the Gaussian rule of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-2|Theorem §B4.4.2]], a spectrum $\propto e^{-u^2/2\sigma^2}$ has $\gamma = e^{i\omega_0\tau}e^{-\sigma^2\tau^2/2}$; its FWHM is $2\sqrt{2\ln 2}\,\sigma = 2\pi\Delta\nu$, which gives the stated exponent.
>
> At $\tau = 1/\Delta\nu$ the three moduli are $0$, $e^{-\pi} = 0.04$ and $e^{-\pi^2/4\ln 2} = 0.03$. Finally $\nu = c/\lambda$ gives $\lvert\Delta\nu\rvert = c\,\Delta\lambda/\lambda^2$ for a narrow line, so $c/\Delta\nu = \lambda^2/\Delta\lambda$. (The Lorentzian and Gaussian formally extend to $\omega < 0$; for $\Delta\nu \ll \bar\nu$ that part is negligible.)

> [!remark] Remark: Where line widths come from
> - **Collisions.** Fowles's model ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^mod-b10-2-2|Model §B10.2.2]]) is a gas in which collisions interrupt the phase. Higher pressure means more frequent collisions, a shorter $\tau_0$ and a broader line.
> - **Spontaneous decay.** An atom left alone radiates a wave train that dies away exponentially with the lifetime of the excited state. That is the ringing oscillator of [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^ex-b4-4-1|Example §B4.4.1]] (Fowles's Problem 3.12), whose spectrum is a Lorentzian of FWHM $\Delta\omega = 1/\tau_{\text{life}}$, with $\tau_{\text{life}}$ the lifetime of the energy. Multiplying by $\hbar$ gives the natural line width $\Gamma = \hbar/\tau_{\text{life}}$ of quantum mechanics ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^def-a3-5-1|QM Def. §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-3|QM Theorem §A3.5.3]]). For naturally broadened light $\lvert\gamma(\tau)\rvert = e^{-\lvert\tau\rvert/2\tau_{\text{life}}}$ (Theorem §B10.2.5, case 2): the coherence time is of the order of the lifetime of the emitting state.
> - **Lasers** ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^mod-a5-4-8|QM Model §A5.4.8]]) have coherence times from microseconds to milliseconds. Fowles notes that two independent lasers then give fringes with each other. The pattern holds still for about one coherence time and then jumps, because the relative phase of the two lasers wanders on that time scale. Level A's "coherent" ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]]) thus always refers to a time scale: two sources are coherent if their relative phase is constant over the time of the measurement.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.5–3.6 and Problem 3.12*

^rem-b10-2-2

> [!caution] Caution: What the visibility does and does not give
> - $\mathcal V = \lvert\gamma\rvert$ only for beams of equal intensity. Otherwise divide by $2\sqrt{I_1I_2}/(I_1 + I_2)$ first ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-1|Theorem §B10.2.1]]).
> - The visibility curve $\lvert\gamma(\tau)\rvert$ alone does not determine the spectrum, because the phase of $\gamma$ is lost. Recording the fringes themselves, $\operatorname{Re}\gamma(\tau)$, does determine it: that is Fourier-transform spectroscopy ([[§B10.3 Spatial Coherence and Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy#^thm-b10-3-3|Theorem §B10.3.3]]).
> - The sharp cutoff at $\lvert\tau\rvert = \tau_0$ belongs to the model with equal wave trains. With random train lengths, $\lvert\gamma\rvert$ fades smoothly, and only the scale $\tau_0 \sim 1/\Delta\nu$ is robust.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B10.2.1: Coherence Lengths of Real Sources
> By [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-5|Theorem §B10.2.5]], $l_c \approx \lambda^2/\Delta\lambda = c/\Delta\nu$ and $\tau_0 = l_c/c$:
>
> | Source | $\bar\lambda$ | Line width | $l_c$ | $\tau_0$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | spectral lamp (discharge tube) | $500\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $\Delta\lambda = 0.1\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $2.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ ($5000\,\lambda$) | $8\ \mathrm{ps}$ |
> | white light, as seen by the eye | $550\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $\Delta\lambda \approx 150\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $2\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ ($3.7\,\lambda$) | $7\ \mathrm{fs}$ |
> | white light through a $10\ \mathrm{nm}$ filter | $600\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $\Delta\lambda = 10\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $36\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ | $0.12\ \mathrm{ps}$ |
> | He–Ne laser with $l_c = 5\ \mathrm{km}$ | $633\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $\Delta\nu = 60\ \mathrm{kHz}$, $\Delta\lambda = 8.0 \times 10^{-17}\ \mathrm m$ | $5\ \mathrm{km}$ | $17\ \mathrm{\mu s}$ |
> | stabilised gas laser | $633\ \mathrm{nm}$ | $\Delta\nu = 10^3\ \mathrm{Hz}$ | $300\ \mathrm{km}$ | $1\ \mathrm{ms}$ |
>
> The white-light entry explains why a Michelson interferometer in white light shows only three or four fringes on each side of the zero-order fringe ([[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^rem-b10-1-4|Remark: Localized fringes, white light and the compensator]]). With $\Delta\lambda/\lambda \approx 0.3$ the formula is only an estimate there. The lamp entry means that fringes from a spectral lamp die out once the mirrors of a Michelson are unequal by about a millimetre. The laser entries show why lasers make interference easy. For the last line Fowles quotes "about $50\ \mathrm{km}$", from $\nu/\Delta\nu \approx 10^{14}/10^3$ wavelengths. With the actual $\nu = c/\lambda = 4.7 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $c/\Delta\nu = 300\ \mathrm{km}$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.6 (lamp, white light, gas laser) and Problems 3.7 (filter), 3.8 (He–Ne)*

> [!example] Example §B10.2.2: Scanning the Delay of a Mach–Zehnder Interferometer
> A Mach–Zehnder interferometer ([[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^rem-b10-1-5|Remark: The Mach–Zehnder interferometer]]) has a delay stage in one arm: a retroreflector that, moved by $x$, lengthens that arm by $2x$. The source follows [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^mod-b10-2-2|Model §B10.2.2]] with $\tau_0 = 10^{-10}\ \mathrm s$ and $\lambda = 1\ \mathrm{mm}$ (a millimetre-wave source), and the two beams are equal at the detector. Then $\tau = 2x/c$, and Theorems §B10.2.1 and §B10.2.3 give
>
> $$
> \frac{I(x)}{I(0)} = \frac12\Bigl[1 + \Bigl(1 - \frac{2\lvert x\rvert}{c\tau_0}\Bigr)\cos\frac{4\pi x}{\lambda}\Bigr] \quad (2\lvert x\rvert < c\tau_0), \qquad \frac{I(x)}{I(0)} = \frac12 \quad (2\lvert x\rvert \ge c\tau_0) .
> $$
>
> The coherence length is $c\tau_0 = 3.0\ \mathrm{cm}$, so the fringes, one every $\lambda/2 = 0.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ of stage travel, die out at $x = \pm1.5\ \mathrm{cm}$. That allows about $30$ fringes on each side of $x = 0$, inside a triangular envelope (figure above). The degree of coherence $\lvert\gamma_{12}(\tau)\rvert$ is the same triangle against $\tau$, reaching zero at $\tau_0 = 100\ \mathrm{ps}$. Far from zero delay the detector still receives light, $I(0)/2$, the sum of the two beams' intensities.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 10 (Question 3)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The time average $\langle f\,g^*\rangle$ behaves as an inner product, and $\lvert\gamma\rvert \le 1$ is its Cauchy–Schwarz inequality ([[§17 Cauchy–Schwarz and the Induced Norm#^thm-17-1|556 Thm. §17.1]]).
> - Theorem §B10.2.4 is Fourier analysis of the kind in [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function|§B4.4]]: $\gamma$ and the normalised spectrum are a transform pair (inversion: [[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function#^thm-b4-4-1|Theorem §B4.4.1]]). A wave packet ([[§B6.1 Wave Packets and Amplitude Modulation#^def-b6-1-1|Def. §B6.1.1]]) is one deterministic train; chaotic light is a random succession of them, with the same power spectrum.
> - The energy–time relation $\Delta E\,\Delta t \gtrsim \hbar$ ([[§A3.5 The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle#^thm-a3-5-3|QM Theorem §A3.5.3]]) is Theorem §B10.2.5 multiplied by $h$: line width times coherence time is of order one.
