Level A worked out the interference of two coherent sources of equal amplitude and the same polarization ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]) and counted the fringes of a Michelson interferometer as one mirror moves ([[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^thm-a6-4-1|Theorem §A6.4.1]]). This section redoes two-beam interference with complex vector fields, so that unequal intensities and arbitrary polarizations are included, and measures the result by the fringe visibility. It then explains the ring pattern a Michelson interferometer actually shows, and what the energy balance of a beam splitter implies about the two outputs. The visibility defined here is the quantity that the rest of the chapter uses to measure coherence.

## Fields, intensities and conventions

> [!caution] Caution: The optics sign convention
> From Chapter B8 on, fields are written as $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[\mathbf E\,e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r - \omega t)}\bigr]$, as Fowles does. The oscillator and impedance chapters B1–B7 used $e^{+i\omega t}$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]]). The two are complex conjugates of each other: a formula taken over from B1–B7 needs $i \to -i$ before it is combined with the formulas here. Intensities, visibilities and other measurable quantities are the same in both conventions. The full statement is in [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-1|§B8.1, Caution: The optics sign convention, opposite to Chapters B1–B7]].

^cau-b10-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Intensity in complex notation
> For a monochromatic field $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[\mathbf E\,e^{-i\omega t}\bigr]$ in a medium of index $n$, the time average of the real field squared is $\tfrac12\lvert\mathbf E\rvert^2$, and the intensity is $\tfrac12 nc\varepsilon_0\lvert\mathbf E\rvert^2$ ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]], with $\varepsilon_0 \to n^2\varepsilon_0$ and $c \to c/n$). Interference compares intensities in one medium, so the constant does not matter. Following Fowles, this chapter writes $I = \lvert\mathbf E\rvert^2$ for monochromatic light, and $I = \langle\lvert\mathbf E(t)\rvert^2\rangle$, a time average, when the amplitude and phase fluctuate ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width|§B10.2]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.1, §3.4*

^rem-b10-1-1

## Two-beam interference

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.1: Interference of Two Monochromatic Waves
> Two plane waves of the same frequency, $\mathbf E_1e^{i(\mathbf k_1\cdot\mathbf r - \omega t)}$ and $\mathbf E_2e^{i(\mathbf k_2\cdot\mathbf r - \omega t)}$, with complex vector amplitudes $\mathbf E_j = E_j\,\hat{\mathbf e}_je^{i\phi_j}$ ($E_j > 0$; $\hat{\mathbf e}_j$ a complex unit vector giving the polarization, with $\hat{\mathbf e}_j\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_j^* = 1$), superpose to the intensity
>
> $$
> I(\mathbf r) = I_1 + I_2 + 2\operatorname{Re}\Bigl[(\mathbf E_1\cdot\mathbf E_2^*)\,e^{i(\mathbf k_1 - \mathbf k_2)\cdot\mathbf r}\Bigr], \qquad I_j = \lvert\mathbf E_j\rvert^2 .
> $$
>
> For the same linear polarization ($\hat{\mathbf e}_1 = \hat{\mathbf e}_2$ real) this is
>
> $$
> I = I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\,\cos\theta, \qquad \theta = (\mathbf k_1 - \mathbf k_2)\cdot\mathbf r + \phi_1 - \phi_2 .
> $$
>
> The last term is the **interference term**. It vanishes at every point if the polarizations are orthogonal, $\hat{\mathbf e}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2^{\ast} = 0$. This holds for crossed linear polarizations and equally for any pair of orthogonal elliptical states, such as right and left circular. It averages to zero if $\phi_1 - \phi_2$ wanders at random (incoherent sources, [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^def-a6-1-1|Def. §A6.1.1]]). For $I_1 = I_2$ it reduces to $I = 4I_1\cos^2(\theta/2)$, the level-A result [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.1, eqs. (3.2)–(3.4), and §3.2 (orthogonal polarizations)*

^thm-b10-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The fields obey the superposition principle (Maxwell's equations in vacuum are linear, [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|EM Principle §A12.1.3]]), so the total complex field is the sum. For complex vectors $\lvert\mathbf a + \mathbf b\rvert^2 = (\mathbf a + \mathbf b)\cdot(\mathbf a + \mathbf b)^* = \lvert\mathbf a\rvert^2 + \lvert\mathbf b\rvert^2 + 2\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b^*)$. With $\mathbf a = \mathbf E_1e^{i(\mathbf k_1\cdot\mathbf r - \omega t)}$ and $\mathbf b = \mathbf E_2e^{i(\mathbf k_2\cdot\mathbf r - \omega t)}$ the factors $e^{-i\omega t}$ cancel in $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b^*$, which gives the first formula. Now $\mathbf E_1\cdot\mathbf E_2^* = E_1E_2\,(\hat{\mathbf e}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2^*)\,e^{i(\phi_1 - \phi_2)}$. For identical real $\hat{\mathbf e}_j$ the bracket is $1$, and $\operatorname{Re}e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta$, with $E_1E_2 = \sqrt{I_1I_2}$. For orthogonal states the bracket is $0$. If $I_1 = I_2$, then $2I_1(1 + \cos\theta) = 4I_1\cos^2(\theta/2)$.

^der-b10-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|EM Principle §A12.1.3]], [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^rem-b10-1-1|Remark: Intensity in complex notation]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §B10.1.1: Fringe Visibility
> In a fringe pattern whose intensity oscillates between $I_{\max}$ and $I_{\min}$, the **fringe visibility** (contrast) is
>
> $$
> \mathcal V = \frac{I_{\max} - I_{\min}}{I_{\max} + I_{\min}}, \qquad 0 \le \mathcal V \le 1 .
> $$
>
> $\mathcal V = 1$ means fringes with perfectly dark minima; $\mathcal V = 0$ means a uniform field.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.4, eq. (3.19)*

^def-b10-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.2: Visibility of Two Coherent Beams
> Let two mutually coherent beams of intensities $I_1$, $I_2$ and polarization states $\hat{\mathbf e}_1$, $\hat{\mathbf e}_2$ interfere ([[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]]), and let the phase $\theta$ run through many periods across the pattern while $I_1$, $I_2$ and the polarizations stay fixed. Then
>
> $$
> I_{\max,\min} = I_1 + I_2 \pm 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\,\lvert\hat{\mathbf e}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2^*\rvert, \qquad \mathcal V = \frac{2\sqrt{I_1I_2}}{I_1 + I_2}\,\lvert\hat{\mathbf e}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2^*\rvert .
> $$
>
> $\mathcal V = 1$ exactly when the beams are equally intense and in the same polarization state. For two linear polarizations at angle $\alpha$, $\lvert\hat{\mathbf e}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2^*\rvert = \lvert\cos\alpha\rvert$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.1, §3.4, eqs. (3.18)–(3.20) (there with $\lvert\gamma_{12}\rvert$ in place of the polarization factor)*

^thm-b10-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $\hat{\mathbf e}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2^* = \lvert\hat{\mathbf e}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2^*\rvert e^{i\psi}$. The interference term of [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]] is then $2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\,\lvert\hat{\mathbf e}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2^*\rvert\cos(\theta + \psi)$, whose cosine takes every value between $-1$ and $1$ across the pattern. That gives $I_{\max}$ and $I_{\min}$; their difference over their sum is $\mathcal V$. Two inequalities bound it by $1$. The first is $2\sqrt{I_1I_2} \le I_1 + I_2$, i.e. $(\sqrt{I_1} - \sqrt{I_2})^2 \ge 0$, with equality only for $I_1 = I_2$. The second is $\lvert\hat{\mathbf e}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2^*\rvert \le \lVert\hat{\mathbf e}_1\rVert\,\lVert\hat{\mathbf e}_2\rVert = 1$. This is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for the inner product $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b^*$ on $\mathbb C^3$ ([[§20 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-14|LADR Thm. 6.14]]), with equality only if $\hat{\mathbf e}_2$ is a multiple of $\hat{\mathbf e}_1$, the same polarization state. For real unit vectors at angle $\alpha$ the product is $\cos\alpha$.

^der-b10-1-2

*Uses:* [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]], [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^def-b10-1-1|Def. §B10.1.1]], [[§20 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-14|LADR Thm. 6.14]]

> [!remark] Remark: What reduces the visibility
> Three things wash out fringes, and this chapter separates them. Unequal intensities and mismatched polarizations act even for perfectly coherent light (Theorem §B10.1.2). The spread of frequencies in the source acts when the path difference becomes long ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width|§B10.2]]). The size of the source acts when the two beams come from points too far apart sideways ([[§B10.3 Spatial Coherence and Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy|§B10.3]]). In each case the visibility becomes a measurement: of an intensity ratio, of a line width, of the angular size of a star. Polarization states in Jones-vector form are in [[§B8.2 The Jones Calculus|§B8.2]].

## Ways of making two coherent beams

> [!definition] Definition §B10.1.2: Division of Wavefront; Division of Amplitude
> An interferometer obtains its mutually coherent beams from a single source in one of two ways.
> - **Division of wavefront.** Different parts of the wavefront from a point-like or line-like source are taken, by slits, mirrors or prisms, and brought together. Examples are Young's double slit, Lloyd's mirror, Fresnel's double mirror and Fresnel's biprism. The source must be small (see [[§B10.3 Spatial Coherence and Fourier-Transform Spectroscopy|§B10.3]]).
> - **Division of amplitude.** One beam is split by partial reflection into beams that travel different paths. Thin films ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^def-a6-3-1|Def. §A6.3.1]]) and the Michelson and Fabry–Perot interferometers work this way. Each point of the incident wavefront is copied into every beam, so an extended source can be used.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.2 ("Classification of Interference Methods")*

^def-b10-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Lloyd's mirror and the biprism
> In **Lloyd's mirror** a point source $S$ sits a little above a plane mirror, and light reaching the screen directly interferes with light reflected at grazing incidence. The reflected light seems to come from the mirror image $S'$, so the pair $S$, $S'$ acts like Young's two slits ([[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^mod-a6-1-2|Model §A6.1.2]]), with one difference: reflection at grazing incidence reverses the field ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]]). The fringe at the mirror plane, where the two paths are equal, is therefore dark, not bright. **Fresnel's double mirror** and **biprism** (a prism with an apex angle just under $180^\circ$) make two virtual images of one source by reflection or refraction, close together, and the analysis is again that of the double slit.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.2, Fig. 3.3*

## The Michelson interferometer

The instrument and the fringe count for a moving mirror are at level A ([[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^def-a6-4-1|Def. §A6.4.1]], [[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^thm-a6-4-1|Theorem §A6.4.1]]). What follows is the shape of the pattern.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.1.3: Fringes of Equal Inclination in a Michelson Interferometer
> In a Michelson interferometer with mirrors exactly perpendicular to their beams, let the arm lengths differ by $d = d_1 - d_2$. Light leaving the instrument at angle $\theta$ to its axis consists of two beams whose phases differ by
>
> $$
> \delta(\theta) = 2kd\cos\theta + \phi_0, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda},
> $$
>
> where $\phi_0$ is a constant set by the reflections at the beam splitter. The intensity seen in direction $\theta$ (with the eye relaxed, or in the focal plane of a lens) is $I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\delta(\theta)$. The fringes are therefore concentric rings about the axis, each ring a set of directions of equal inclination, and they appear to lie at infinity. The order of interference is largest at the centre, $N_0 = 2d/\lambda + \phi_0/2\pi$. When $\lvert d\rvert$ decreases, the rings contract toward the centre and vanish there, one for each $\lambda/2$ of mirror travel, as level A counted them ([[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^thm-a6-4-1|Theorem §A6.4.1]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.3, eq. (3.10), Fig. 3.5; ring geometry as in §4.2 · PHY 300, Worksheet 10*

^thm-b10-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Unfold the instrument as the observer sees it. The image $M_2'$ of the mirror $M_2$ in the beam splitter is parallel to $M_1$ and a distance $d$ from it. A point $S$ of the source is seen in $M_1$ at an image $S_1$ and in $M_2'$ at an image $S_2$. Both images lie on the same normal to the mirrors, a distance $2d$ apart (an image lies as far behind a plane mirror as the object is in front, [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]). The two images are copies of one source point, so they are coherent virtual sources. Rays from them that leave in the same direction $\theta$ are parallel and meet at one point of the focal plane of a lens. Their path difference is the projection of $S_1S_2$ on that direction, $2d\cos\theta$. This is the construction of [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]], with the two sources now one behind the other instead of side by side, so the cosine replaces the sine. The phase difference is $k\cdot 2d\cos\theta$ plus the fixed offset $\phi_0$ from the beam splitter ([[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^rem-a6-4-2|Remark: Constant phase offsets do not matter]]), and [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]] gives the intensity.
>
> $\delta$ depends on $\theta$ only, not on which point of the source the light came from. Every source point therefore produces the same ring pattern in the focal plane, and an extended source gives bright, sharp rings. The phase $\delta$ falls as $\theta$ grows, so it is greatest at the centre. Reducing $d$ by $\lambda/2$ lowers $\delta(0)$ by $2\pi$, which moves each ring inward to the place of the ring inside it.

^der-b10-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]], [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-3|Theorem §A6.1.3]], [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]], [[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^rem-a6-4-2|Remark: Constant phase offsets do not matter]]

> [!remark] Remark: Localized fringes, white light and the compensator
> - If one mirror is tilted slightly, $M_1$ and $M_2'$ form a thin wedge of air. One then sees nearly straight fringes that seem to lie near the mirrors (**localized fringes**): the fringes of an air wedge ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^rem-a6-3-3|Remark: Air wedges and Newton's rings]]).
> - With white light, fringes are seen only where $M_1$ and $M_2'$ cross, a few on each side of zero path difference. The reason is the short coherence length of white light, computed in [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^ex-b10-2-1|Example §B10.2.1]]. Seeing them requires the compensator plate ([[§A6.4 The Michelson Interferometer#^rem-a6-4-1|Remark: Why the two beams interfere]]). Without it the glass of the beam splitter adds a path difference that depends on wavelength, and no single mirror position equalises the paths for all colours at once.
> - With an uncoated glass beam splitter the zero-order white-light fringe is **dark**. One beam is reflected at the air–glass surface (phase change $\pi$, [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^law-a6-3-1|Law §A6.3.1]]) and the other inside the glass at the glass–air surface (no change). So $\phi_0 = \pi$ at zero path difference, for every colour.
> - The **Twyman–Green** interferometer is a Michelson lit by collimated light, with an optical element (lens, prism, mirror) placed in one arm. Defects of the element show up as distortions of otherwise straight fringes.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.3, Figs. 3.5–3.6*

^rem-b10-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: The Mach–Zehnder interferometer, and where the light goes at a dark fringe
> The **Mach–Zehnder** interferometer splits a beam at one beam splitter, sends the two halves along separate paths (one with an adjustable delay), and recombines them at a second beam splitter, which has two outputs, A and B. Each output receives one contribution from each arm. Take both splitters lossless, with amplitude coefficients of moduli $\sqrt R$, $\sqrt T$ ($R + T = 1$). In output A each arm contributes one reflection and one transmission, so both terms have intensity $RT$. In output B one arm contributes two reflections and the other two transmissions, giving intensities $R^2$ and $T^2$. The interference terms in A and B both have amplitude $2RT$ (by [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-1|Theorem §B10.1.1]], $2\sqrt{RT\cdot RT} = 2\sqrt{R^2T^2}$). Energy conservation demands $I_A + I_B = I_{\text{in}}$ at every delay, while the non-oscillating parts already add to $2RT + R^2 + T^2 = (R + T)^2 = 1$. The two interference terms must therefore cancel: they are $\pi$ out of phase. The outputs are complementary, and when A is dark all the light leaves through B. In a Michelson interferometer the second output goes back toward the source. This answers PHY 300's question about the phases picked up at the beam splitter in each arm. The individual phases depend on how the splitter is built (bare glass, metal or dielectric coating), but their combination must give this relative $\pi$ between the two outputs.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 10 (Questions 2 and 4); the energy argument is added here*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B10.1.1: The Rings of a Michelson Interferometer
> A Michelson interferometer is lit by an extended He–Ne source ($\lambda = 633\ \mathrm{nm}$) and viewed through a lens of focal length $f = 200\ \mathrm{mm}$. The arms differ by $d \approx 0.50\ \mathrm{mm}$, adjusted slightly so that the centre is bright. By [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]] the central order is $N_0 = 2d/\lambda \approx 1580$, an integer (absorbing $\phi_0$ into $d$). The $p$-th bright ring has order $N_0 - p$:
>
> $$
> 2d\cos\theta_p = (N_0 - p)\lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2d\Bigl(1 - \frac{\theta_p^2}{2}\Bigr) \approx 2d - p\lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_p \approx \sqrt{\frac{p\lambda}{d}},
> $$
>
> using $\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]). Here $\lambda/d = 1.27 \times 10^{-3}$, so
>
> $$
> \theta_1 = 35.6\ \mathrm{mrad}\ (2.04^\circ), \quad \theta_2 = 50.3\ \mathrm{mrad}, \quad \theta_3 = 61.6\ \mathrm{mrad}; \qquad \text{radii } f\theta_p = 7.1,\ 10.1,\ 12.3\ \mathrm{mm}.
> $$
>
> The radii grow as $\sqrt1 : \sqrt2 : \sqrt3$, so the rings crowd together outward. Moving the mirror until $d = 50\ \mathrm{\mu m}$ gives $\theta_1 = 0.113\ \mathrm{rad}$ ($6.4^\circ$, radius $22.5\ \mathrm{mm}$): fewer, wider rings. On the way, $2(0.50 - 0.05)\ \mathrm{mm}/\lambda \approx 1420$ rings have shrunk into the centre and disappeared. At $d = 0$ the central fringe fills the whole field.
>
> *Source: Fowles §3.3 (ring fringes) and Problem 4.4 (the $\sqrt p$ law, for the Fabry–Perot) · PHY 300, Worksheet 10 (Question 6); numbers chosen here*

^ex-b10-1-1

> [!example] Example §B10.1.2: An Unbalanced Beam Splitter and a Rotated Polarization
> A Mach–Zehnder interferometer ([[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^rem-b10-1-5|Remark: The Mach–Zehnder interferometer]]) is built with two lossless splitters of $R = 0.8$, $T = 0.2$, and lit by linearly polarized laser light of intensity $I_{\text{in}}$.
> - Output A: both beams have $RT\,I_{\text{in}} = 0.16\,I_{\text{in}}$. By [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-2|Theorem §B10.1.2]], $\mathcal V_A = 1$; the intensity swings between $0$ and $0.64\,I_{\text{in}}$ as the delay is scanned.
> - Output B: the beams have $R^2I_{\text{in}} = 0.64\,I_{\text{in}}$ and $T^2I_{\text{in}} = 0.04\,I_{\text{in}}$, so
>
> $$
> \mathcal V_B = \frac{2\sqrt{0.64 \times 0.04}}{0.64 + 0.04} = \frac{0.32}{0.68} = 0.47,
> $$
>
> and the intensity swings between $0.36\,I_{\text{in}}$ and $1.00\,I_{\text{in}}$, always in step opposite to A.
> - A half-wave plate in one arm, rotating its linear polarization by $45^\circ$, multiplies both visibilities by $\cos 45^\circ = 0.71$. Rotating it by $90^\circ$ makes them zero: each output then shows the constant sum of its two beam intensities, $0.32\,I_{\text{in}}$ and $0.68\,I_{\text{in}}$. A quarter-wave plate that makes one beam circular gives $\lvert\hat{\mathbf e}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}_2^*\rvert = \lvert(1, 0)\cdot(1, -i)^*\rvert/\sqrt2 = 1/\sqrt2$, again a factor $0.71$.
>
> A Michelson interferometer with the same splitter always has $\mathcal V = 1$ in its observed output. Each beam is reflected once and transmitted once, so both have intensity $RT\,I_{\text{in}}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 10 (Question 2, the Mach–Zehnder) · Fowles §3.1–3.2 (orthogonal polarizations do not interfere); numbers chosen here*

^ex-b10-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The bound $\mathcal V \le 1$ is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality ([[§20 Inner Products and Norms#^ladr-6-14|LADR Thm. 6.14]]) applied to polarization vectors. In [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width|§B10.2]] the same inequality, applied to time averages, bounds the degree of coherence.
> - The two outputs of a lossless beam splitter are complementary for single photons as well. Each photon leaves by one port, with probabilities given by the intensities above ([[§A3.2 Wave–Particle Duality#^law-a3-2-1|QM Law §A3.2.1]]).
> - The ring geometry $2d\cos\theta$ returns for the Fabry–Perot interferometer, where many beams instead of two interfere ([[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-1|Theorem §B10.4.1]]).
