*Sources: Fowles, Introduction to Modern Optics, §4.4 · Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lecture 15 §3–4.*

Level A designs a nonreflective coating from two rays, the reflections at the top and bottom of the film, and finds the thickness $\lambda/4n$ ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^ex-a6-3-1|Example §A6.3.1]]). It cannot say how much light is still reflected, or what many layers do. Here the fields in every layer are tracked exactly, with all reflections included, by imposing the electromagnetic boundary conditions at each interface. Each layer becomes a $2\times2$ matrix, a stack becomes a matrix product, and the reflectance of any multilayer follows from four matrix elements. Two designs come out at once: the quarter-wave antireflection coating, whose ideal index is the geometric mean of its neighbours (as in the impedance matching of [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^ex-b5-2-3|Example §B5.2.3]]), and the quarter-wave stack, a dielectric mirror more reflective than silver. Normal incidence throughout; fields in the optics convention ([[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^cau-b10-1-1|Caution: The optics sign convention]]).

## The transfer matrix

> [!definition] Definition §B10.5.1: Reduced Magnetic Field; Transfer Matrix
> At normal incidence, the fields of a wave along $z$ are parallel to the layers. Describe them at each plane $z$ by the complex tangential electric field $E(z)$ and the **reduced magnetic field** $\mathcal H(z) = Z_0H(z)$, with $Z_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0}$ and $H$ the tangential magnetic field, oriented so that a single wave travelling toward $+z$ in a non-magnetic medium of index $n$ has $\mathcal H = nE$, and one travelling toward $-z$ has $\mathcal H = -nE$. The **transfer matrix** $M$ of a layer between $z = 0$ and $z = l$ is the matrix with
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix}E(0)\\ \mathcal H(0)\end{pmatrix} = M\begin{pmatrix}E(l)\\ \mathcal H(l)\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.4, eqs. (4.22)–(4.24), (4.27) (Fowles writes the magnetic condition directly as $n_0E_0 - n_0E_0' = n_1E_1 - n_1E_1'$)*

^def-b10-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.5.1: Transfer Matrix of a Homogeneous Layer
> A layer of index $n_1$ and thickness $l$, at vacuum wavelength $\lambda_0$, has
>
> $$
> M = \begin{pmatrix}\cos kl & -\dfrac{i}{n_1}\sin kl\\[2mm] -in_1\sin kl & \cos kl\end{pmatrix}, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi n_1}{\lambda_0}, \qquad \det M = 1 .
> $$
>
> Because $E$ and $\mathcal H$ are continuous at every interface, a stack of layers $1, 2, \dots, N$, counted from the incident side, has the product $M = M_1M_2\cdots M_N$. Two cases recur:
> - a **quarter-wave layer** (optical thickness $n_1l = \lambda_0/4$, $kl = \pi/2$): $M = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -i/n_1\\ -in_1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$;
> - a **half-wave layer** ($kl = \pi$): $M = -\mathbb 1$. It changes nothing but an overall sign, and at that wavelength it is as if absent.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.4, eqs. (4.22)–(4.28), Problem 4.9*

^thm-b10-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Inside the layer, with $E_1$ and $E_1'$ the amplitudes of the forward and backward waves at $z = 0$,
>
> $$
> E(z) = E_1e^{ikz} + E_1'e^{-ikz}, \qquad \mathcal H(z) = n_1\bigl(E_1e^{ikz} - E_1'e^{-ikz}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The relation $\mathcal H = \pm nE$ is the plane-wave relation $B = E/v$ ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-5|EM Theorem §A12.2.5]] with $c \to v = c/n$) and $H = B/\mu_0$. Then $Z_0H = Z_0nE/(\mu_0c) = nE$, since $Z_0/\mu_0c = 1$. The sign flips with the direction of travel because $\mathbf E \times \mathbf H$ must point along the propagation. At $z = l$, solve for the two waves: $E_1e^{ikl} = \tfrac12\bigl(E(l) + \mathcal H(l)/n_1\bigr)$ and $E_1'e^{-ikl} = \tfrac12\bigl(E(l) - \mathcal H(l)/n_1\bigr)$. Substitute into the fields at $z = 0$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> E(0) &= E_1 + E_1' = \tfrac12\bigl(E + \mathcal H/n_1\bigr)e^{-ikl} + \tfrac12\bigl(E - \mathcal H/n_1\bigr)e^{ikl} = E(l)\cos kl - \frac{i}{n_1}\,\mathcal H(l)\sin kl,\\
> \mathcal H(0) &= n_1(E_1 - E_1') = -in_1E(l)\sin kl + \mathcal H(l)\cos kl .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> $\det M = \cos^2kl + \sin^2kl = 1$. At an interface without surface charges or currents, the tangential components of $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf H$ are continuous ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]]). So the vector $(E, \mathcal H)$ at the back of one layer is the vector at the front of the next, and the matrices multiply in order. The products of determinants are $1$ ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]). Setting $kl = \pi/2$ and $\pi$ gives the two special cases.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.5.2: Reflection and Transmission of a Multilayer
> A stack with total transfer matrix $M = \begin{pmatrix}A & B\\ C & D\end{pmatrix}$ lies between an incident medium of index $n_0$ and a substrate of index $n_T$. Its amplitude reflection and transmission coefficients $r = E_0'/E_0$ and $t = E_T/E_0$ are
>
> $$
> r = \frac{An_0 + Bn_Tn_0 - C - Dn_T}{An_0 + Bn_Tn_0 + C + Dn_T}, \qquad t = \frac{2n_0}{An_0 + Bn_Tn_0 + C + Dn_T} .
> $$
>
> The reflectance and transmittance are
>
> $$
> R = \lvert r\rvert^2, \qquad T = \frac{n_T}{n_0}\,\lvert t\rvert^2,
> $$
>
> and for non-absorbing layers (all indices real) $R + T = 1$. Equivalently, $r = (n_0 - Y)/(n_0 + Y)$ with $Y = (C + Dn_T)/(A + Bn_T)$, the ratio $\mathcal H/E$ at the front surface: the stack and substrate act on the incident wave like a single medium of "effective index" $Y$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.4, eqs. (4.25)–(4.31); the factor $n_T/n_0$ in $T$, the energy balance and the form with $Y$ added here*

^thm-b10-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the incident medium the incident and reflected waves give, at the front surface, $E = E_0(1 + r)$ and $\mathcal H = n_0E_0(1 - r)$ ([[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^def-b10-5-1|Def. §B10.5.1]]). In the substrate only the transmitted wave exists: $E = tE_0$, $\mathcal H = n_TtE_0$. By [[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^thm-b10-5-1|Theorem §B10.5.1]],
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix}1\\ n_0\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}1\\ -n_0\end{pmatrix}r = M\begin{pmatrix}1\\ n_T\end{pmatrix}t, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad 1 + r = (A + Bn_T)\,t, \quad n_0(1 - r) = (C + Dn_T)\,t .
> $$
>
> Multiply the first by $n_0$ and add the second: $2n_0 = (An_0 + Bn_Tn_0 + C + Dn_T)\,t$. Then $r = (A + Bn_T)t - 1$ gives $r$. Dividing the two equations instead gives $n_0(1 - r)/(1 + r) = Y$, i.e. $r = (n_0 - Y)/(n_0 + Y)$.
>
> *Energy.* The time-averaged power per unit area carried along $z$ is $\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(E\,H^{\ast}) = \operatorname{Re}(E\,\mathcal H^{\ast})/2Z_0$. For a single wave this is $\tfrac12nc\varepsilon_0\lvert E\rvert^2$ ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]] in a medium), so the intensity of a wave of amplitude $E$ is proportional to $n\lvert E\rvert^2$, not to $\lvert E\rvert^2$. Hence $T = n_T\lvert tE_0\rvert^2/n_0\lvert E_0\rvert^2$. At the front surface $\operatorname{Re}(E\mathcal H^{\ast}) = n_0\lvert E_0\rvert^2\operatorname{Re}\bigl[(1 + r)(1 - r^{\ast})\bigr] = n_0\lvert E_0\rvert^2(1 - \lvert r\rvert^2)$, because $r^{\ast} - r$ is imaginary. Inside a layer with real $n$, $\operatorname{Re}(E\mathcal H^{\ast})$ is the same at both faces: each of the two waves carries its own power, $\pm n\lvert E\rvert^2$, and their cross terms are imaginary. The flux is also continuous at the interfaces, so the front value equals the back value $n_T\lvert t\rvert^2\lvert E_0\rvert^2$, which is $1 - R = T$.

> [!caution] Caution: Transmittance is not just the squared amplitude
> Fowles writes $T = \lvert t\rvert^2$ after eq. (4.31). That is right only when the substrate and the incident medium have the same index. For a bare glass surface ($M = \mathbb 1$, $n_0 = 1$, $n_T = 1.52$), $t = 2/(2.52) = 0.794$ and $\lvert t\rvert^2 = 0.630$. The transmittance is $T = 1.52 \times 0.630 = 0.957$, which with $R = 0.043$ adds up to $1$. A wave in a denser medium carries more power for the same field amplitude ([[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^thm-b10-5-2|Theorem §B10.5.2]]; for a single interface, [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^cau-b9-2-2|§B9.2, Caution: T is not the square of t]]).

^cau-b10-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Impedance language
> Schwartz writes the normal-incidence reflection coefficient of light as that of a wave on a string, $E_R/E_I = (Z_2 - Z_1)/(Z_2 + Z_1)$, with optical impedance $Z = \mu v = Z_0/n$ for non-magnetic media, which turns into $(n_1 - n_2)/(n_1 + n_2)$. The electric field plays the part of the force and the magnetic field that of the velocity in [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]]. The index $n$ is an admittance, and $Y$ of Theorem §B10.5.2 is the input admittance of the stack loaded by the substrate, so that $r = (n_0 - Y)/(n_0 + Y) = (Z_Y - Z_0')/(Z_Y + Z_0')$, with $Z_0' = Z_0/n_0$ and $Z_Y = Z_0/Y$, is the load formula of [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-5|Theorem §B5.2.5]] written for the force, which reflects with the opposite sign to the velocity. A quarter-wave layer turns a load $n_T$ into $Y = n_1^2/n_T$ (from its matrix, $Y = (-in_1)/(-in_T/n_1)$). This is the "quarter-wave transformer" of transmission lines, and it matches $n_T$ to $n_0$ when $n_1 = \sqrt{n_0n_T}$. A half-wave layer returns $Y = n_T$ unchanged.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 15 §3–4, eqs. (14)–(15), (19)*

## Antireflection coatings

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.5.3: Single Layer; the Quarter-Wave Antireflection Coating
> A single layer of index $n_1$ and thickness $l$ on a substrate $n_T$, in a medium $n_0$, reflects with
>
> $$
> r = \frac{n_1(n_0 - n_T)\cos kl - i(n_0n_T - n_1^2)\sin kl}{n_1(n_0 + n_T)\cos kl - i(n_0n_T + n_1^2)\sin kl} .
> $$
>
> At the wavelength for which the layer is a quarter wave thick ($n_1l = \lambda_0/4$),
>
> $$
> R = \Bigl(\frac{n_0n_T - n_1^2}{n_0n_T + n_1^2}\Bigr)^2,
> $$
>
> which vanishes when $n_1 = \sqrt{n_0n_T}$. At the wavelength for which it is a half wave thick, $R = \bigl((n_0 - n_T)/(n_0 + n_T)\bigr)^2$, the reflectance of the uncoated substrate. Level A's condition $t = \lambda/4n$ ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^ex-a6-3-1|Example §A6.3.1]]) fixes the thickness. The exact theory adds that the reflection cancels completely only if the index is the geometric mean.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.4, eqs. (4.32)–(4.34) (there with $n_0 = 1$)*

^thm-b10-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Insert $A = D = \cos kl$, $B = -(i/n_1)\sin kl$, $C = -in_1\sin kl$ ([[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^thm-b10-5-1|Theorem §B10.5.1]]) into $r$ of [[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^thm-b10-5-2|Theorem §B10.5.2]]. The numerator is $(n_0 - n_T)\cos kl - i(n_0n_T/n_1 - n_1)\sin kl$, the denominator the same with plus signs. Multiply both by $n_1$. For $kl = \pi/2$ only the sine terms survive, giving $r = (n_0n_T - n_1^2)/(n_0n_T + n_1^2)$, which is zero iff $n_1^2 = n_0n_T$. For $kl = \pi$ only the cosine terms survive, giving $r = (n_0 - n_T)/(n_0 + n_T)$.

## High reflectors

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.5.4: Quarter-Wave Stack
> A stack of $2N$ alternating quarter-wave layers of high index $n_H$ and low index $n_L$, written $(HL)^N$ with an $H$ layer facing the incident medium $n_0$ and an $L$ layer on the substrate $n_T$, has at its design wavelength
>
> $$
> M = \begin{pmatrix}(-n_L/n_H)^N & 0\\ 0 & (-n_H/n_L)^N\end{pmatrix}, \qquad R = \Bigl(\frac{1 - x}{1 + x}\Bigr)^2, \qquad x = \frac{n_T}{n_0}\Bigl(\frac{n_H}{n_L}\Bigr)^{2N},
> $$
>
> so $R \to 1$ as $N$ grows, with $1 - R = 4x/(1 + x)^2 \approx 4(n_0/n_T)(n_L/n_H)^{2N}$. For $n_0 = n_T = 1$ this is Fowles's
>
> $$
> R = \Bigl(\frac{(n_H/n_L)^{2N} - 1}{(n_H/n_L)^{2N} + 1}\Bigr)^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.4, eqs. (4.35)–(4.37), Fig. 4.9 (there with $n_0 = n_T = 1$)*

^thm-b10-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With the quarter-wave matrices of [[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^thm-b10-5-1|Theorem §B10.5.1]],
>
> $$
> M_HM_L = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -i/n_H\\ -in_H & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0 & -i/n_L\\ -in_L & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-n_L/n_H & 0\\ 0 & -n_H/n_L\end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> and the $N$-th power of a diagonal matrix is the diagonal of $N$-th powers. With $B = C = 0$, [[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^thm-b10-5-2|Theorem §B10.5.2]] gives $r = (An_0 - Dn_T)/(An_0 + Dn_T)$. Dividing numerator and denominator by $An_0 = (-1)^Nn_0(n_L/n_H)^N$ leaves $r = (1 - x)/(1 + x)$ with $x = Dn_T/An_0$ as stated. Then $1 - R = \bigl[(1 + x)^2 - (1 - x)^2\bigr]/(1 + x)^2 = 4x/(1 + x)^2$. For Fowles's case, $x = (n_H/n_L)^{2N}$, and $(1 - x)^2 = (x - 1)^2$.

> [!remark] Remark: Why the stack reflects, and what else multilayers do
> - At the design wavelength every partial reflection in a quarter-wave stack returns in phase with the others. Each layer adds a half wave of round-trip path, and the reflection phases alternate in sign from $H \to L$ to $L \to H$ interfaces ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^law-a6-3-1|Law §A6.3.1]]), so the two effects compensate. Away from it the layers are no longer quarter waves, and the reflection falls off outside a band whose width grows with $n_H/n_L$ (figure (b)). Combining stacks of different thicknesses broadens the band.
> - **Antireflection multilayers.** Two layers, one high and one low index, give zero reflectance at one wavelength with available materials. Three suitable layers give zero at two wavelengths and an average below about $0.5\%$ across the visible.
> - **Fabry–Perot interference filter.** A half-wave spacer layer between two partially reflecting surfaces (today, two quarter-wave stacks) is a Fabry–Perot etalon of tiny spacing ([[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^def-b10-4-2|Def. §B10.4.2]]). It transmits a narrow band at the design wavelength $\lambda_0$ (with further orders near $\lambda_0/2$, $\lambda_0/3$, ...), whose width is set by the mirror reflectance through the Airy function ([[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-2|Theorem §B10.4.2]]). Other multilayers make "hot" and "cold" mirrors that reflect or transmit the infrared, and beam splitters.
> - **Oblique incidence.** The same formalism holds with $kl \to kl\cos\theta$ (the phase thickness at angle $\theta$ inside the layer) and $n \to p$, where $p = n\cos\theta$ for s (TE) polarization and $p = n/\cos\theta$ for p (TM) polarization (compare the denominators of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]]). The quarter-wave condition then holds at $\lambda_0\cos\theta$: a coating looks bluer when tilted, and differently so for the two polarizations.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.4, Figs. 4.8–4.11, Problem 4.10*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B10.5.1: Magnesium Fluoride on Glass, Exactly
> A quarter-wave layer of MgF₂ ($n_1 = 1.35$) on glass ($n_T = 1.52$) in air. By [[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^thm-b10-5-3|Theorem §B10.5.3]],
>
> $$
> r = \frac{1.52 - 1.35^2}{1.52 + 1.35^2} = \frac{1.52 - 1.8225}{3.3425} = -0.0905, \qquad R = 0.0082 = 0.82\%,
> $$
>
> against $R = (0.52/2.52)^2 = 4.3\%$ for bare glass: a factor of five less. The ideal coating index would be $\sqrt{1.52} = 1.23$. Fowles's rule of thumb, "about $1\%$, a quarter of the uncoated value", is for glass of $n \approx 1.5$. With University Physics's value $n_1 = 1.38$ for MgF₂ ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^ex-a6-3-1|Example §A6.3.1]]) the residual is $1.26\%$. The level-A two-ray estimate keeps only the first reflection, $r_1 = (1 - 1.35)/(1 + 1.35) = -0.149$, and the second, $r_2 = (1.35 - 1.52)/(1.35 + 1.52) = -0.059$, which returns half a wave late: $r \approx r_1 - r_2 = -0.0897$, $R \approx 0.80\%$. The exact result differs by $2\%$ of itself, because with reflections this weak the beams that bounce more than once hardly matter ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^cau-a6-3-1|Caution: Three things to get right]]). In a camera lens with ten or twelve air–glass surfaces, $4.3\%$ lost at each would cost about $40\%$ of the light; $0.8\%$ at each costs under $10\%$. Figure (a) shows that the coating stays below $2\%$ from $400$ to $750\ \mathrm{nm}$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.4 and Problem 4.7*

^ex-b10-5-1

> [!example] Example §B10.5.2: A Dielectric Mirror
> Eight quarter-wave layers ($N = 4$) of zinc sulfide ($n_H = 2.3$) and magnesium fluoride ($n_L = 1.35$). Here $(n_H/n_L)^8 = 1.704^8 = 71.0$, and [[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^thm-b10-5-4|Theorem §B10.5.4]] gives:
> - with $n_0 = n_T = 1$ (Fowles's formula): $R = (70.0/72.0)^2 = 0.945$;
> - on glass ($n_T = 1.52$), $H$ layer facing the air: $x = 1.52 \times 71.0 = 107.9$, $R = (106.9/108.9)^2 = 0.964$.
>
> Either way this exceeds the reflectance of silver in the visible, with essentially no absorption. Thirty layers ($N = 15$) give $1 - R \approx 4/(1.704)^{30} = 5 \times 10^{-7}$ with $n_0 = n_T = 1$. With $n_L = 1.4$ and $n_H = 2.8$, eight layers already give $(n_H/n_L)^8 = 256$ and $R = (255/257)^2 = 0.984$: the index ratio matters more than the number of layers. Fowles quotes "about $0.97$" for the ZnS/MgF₂ eight-layer stack, while his own eq. (4.37) gives $0.945$. The figure is close to the glass-substrate value $0.964$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.4 and Problem 4.8*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The transfer matrices have determinant $1$ and multiply in order. The determinant is multiplicative ([[§37 Determinants#^ladr-9-49|LADR Thm. 9.49]]), and the diagonal product of the quarter-wave stack is the simplest case of a matrix power. The same algebra of $2\times2$ matrices with unit determinant runs the ray-transfer (ABCD) matrices of [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses|§B12.1]].
> - The matching index $\sqrt{n_0n_T}$ is the geometric mean, as for the middle block of [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^ex-b5-2-3|Example §B5.2.3]]. There it maximises the transfer through two steps; here a quarter-wave delay makes the two reflections cancel exactly.
> - The boundary conditions used here, continuity of tangential $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf H$, are derived from Maxwell's equations in [[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]] (whose derivation in turn is [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-6|EM Theorem §B8.4.6]]). A single interface ($M = \mathbb 1$) gives back the normal-incidence Fresnel coefficients of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]], and the transmittance factor $n_T/n_0$ is that of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-3|Theorem §B9.2.3]].
