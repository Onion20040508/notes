The thin-film picture of level A keeps two reflected rays and warns that for highly reflecting surfaces the light bouncing back and forth dominates ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^cau-a6-3-1|Caution: Three things to get right]]). This section keeps all the bounces. Between two parallel partial mirrors the transmitted beams form a geometric series, and their sum, the Airy function, has sharp peaks when the mirrors reflect strongly. The peaks sharpen with reflectance much as the principal maxima of a grating sharpen with the number of lines ([[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]]). The Fabry–Perot interferometer built on this effect reaches resolving powers of a million, a hundred times those of a grating ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-3|Theorem §A7.4.3]]). The same pair of mirrors is an optical resonator, with a Lorentzian response and a quality factor exactly as in [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power|§B1.4]]. Complex amplitudes follow the optics convention ([[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^cau-b10-1-1|Caution: The optics sign convention]]).

## The Airy formula

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.4.1: Phase Difference between Successive Beams
> Two parallel partially reflecting surfaces a distance $d$ apart enclose a medium of index $n$. A plane wave of vacuum wavelength $\lambda_0$ travels inside at angle $\theta$ to the normal. Successive transmitted beams (and successive reflected beams) leave parallel to one another, each having made one more round trip than the last. Apart from the phases of reflection, they differ in phase by
>
> $$
> \delta = 2kd\cos\theta = \frac{4\pi}{\lambda_0}\,nd\cos\theta, \qquad k = \frac{2\pi n}{\lambda_0} .
> $$
>
> At normal incidence a round trip adds $2kd$. If each internal reflection adds the phase $\delta_r/2$, the total phase step between successive beams is $\Delta = \delta + \delta_r$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.1, eqs. (4.1)–(4.2), (4.7), Fig. 4.2 · PHY 300, Worksheet 10 (Questions 5–6)*

^thm-b10-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Follow two successive beams. The later one has crossed the gap twice more, a distance $2d/\cos\theta$ in the medium, which adds the phase $k\cdot 2d/\cos\theta$. The two leave the second surface at points a distance $2d\tan\theta$ apart along it. The earlier beam must therefore still travel an extra distance outside before both reach a common wavefront perpendicular to the emerging rays. That distance is the projection $2d\tan\theta\,\sin\theta_{\text{out}}$, in a medium of index $n_{\text{out}}$ with $n_{\text{out}}\sin\theta_{\text{out}} = n\sin\theta$ (Snell's law, [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|Law §A4.2.4]]). In optical path lengths ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^def-a6-3-2|Def. §A6.3.2]]) the difference is
>
> $$
> \frac{2nd}{\cos\theta} - n_{\text{out}}\,2d\tan\theta\sin\theta_{\text{out}} = \frac{2nd}{\cos\theta} - \frac{2nd\sin^2\theta}{\cos\theta} = 2nd\cos\theta,
> $$
>
> and multiplying by $2\pi/\lambda_0$ gives $\delta$. The later beam has also undergone two more internal reflections, adding $2\cdot\delta_r/2 = \delta_r$.

^der-b10-4-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^law-a4-2-4|Law §A4.2.4]], [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^def-a6-3-2|Def. §A6.3.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.4.2: Airy Formula for Multiple-Beam Transmission
> Let the two surfaces of [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-1|Theorem §B10.4.1]] be identical, each with amplitude transmission coefficients $t$ into the gap and $t'$ out of it, and internal amplitude reflection coefficient $r = \lvert r\rvert e^{i\delta_r/2}$, so that the **reflectance** and **transmittance** of one surface are $R = \lvert r\rvert^2$ and $T = \lvert tt'\rvert$. ($T$ is the same from either side; it equals $\lvert t\rvert^2$ only when the gap and the outside have the same index, [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^cau-b9-2-2|Caution: T is not the square of t]].) With no absorption between the surfaces, the transmitted intensity is
>
> $$
> I_T = I_0\,\frac{T^2}{\lvert 1 - Re^{i\Delta}\rvert^2} = I_0\,\frac{T^2}{(1 - R)^2}\,\frac{1}{1 + F\sin^2(\Delta/2)}, \qquad F = \frac{4R}{(1 - R)^2} .
> $$
>
> For two different surfaces the same formula holds with $T^2 \to T_1T_2$, $R \to \sqrt{R_1R_2}$, and $\delta_r$ the sum of the two internal reflection phases. (For an uncoated dielectric surface, $tt' = 4n_1n_2/(n_1 + n_2)^2$ at normal incidence, which is $1 - R$.)
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.1, eqs. (4.3)–(4.9), (4.11)–(4.12), Fig. 4.1*

^thm-b10-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The incident amplitude $E_0$ is transmitted into the gap ($t$) and out of it ($t'$), and on each round trip in between it is reflected twice ($r^2$) and advanced in phase by $\delta$. The transmitted beams therefore have amplitudes $E_0tt'$, $E_0tt'r^2e^{i\delta}$, $E_0tt'r^4e^{2i\delta}, \dots$, up to a phase common to all. Since $\lvert r^2e^{i\delta}\rvert = R < 1$, the geometric series converges ([[§14 Series#^ex-14-4|451 Ex. §14.4]], whose proof applies verbatim to a complex ratio):
>
> $$
> E_T = E_0tt'\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\bigl(r^2e^{i\delta}\bigr)^m = \frac{E_0tt'}{1 - r^2e^{i\delta}} = \frac{E_0tt'}{1 - Re^{i\Delta}},
> $$
>
> because $r^2 = Re^{i\delta_r}$. The light leaves into the same medium it came from, so $I_T/I_0 = \lvert E_T/E_0\rvert^2 = T^2/\lvert 1 - Re^{i\Delta}\rvert^2$. The denominator is $(1 - Re^{i\Delta})(1 - Re^{-i\Delta}) = 1 - 2R\cos\Delta + R^2$, and with $1 - \cos\Delta = 2\sin^2(\Delta/2)$ it becomes $(1 - R)^2 + 4R\sin^2(\Delta/2) = (1 - R)^2\bigl[1 + F\sin^2(\Delta/2)\bigr]$. For different surfaces the beams carry $t_1t_2'(r_1r_2e^{i\delta})^m$, with $\lvert r_1r_2\rvert = \sqrt{R_1R_2}$, and the same algebra applies.

^der-b10-4-2

*Uses:* [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-1|Theorem §B10.4.1]], [[§14 Series#^ex-14-4|451 Ex. §14.4]]

> [!definition] Definition §B10.4.1: Airy Function; Coefficient of Finesse; Order of Interference
> The **Airy function** is $\mathcal A(\Delta) = \bigl[1 + F\sin^2(\Delta/2)\bigr]^{-1}$, and $F = 4R/(1 - R)^2$ is the **coefficient of finesse**. The maxima of $\mathcal A$ are at $\Delta = 2\pi N$. The integer $N$, given by
>
> $$
> 2\pi N = \frac{4\pi}{\lambda_0}\,nd\cos\theta + \delta_r,
> $$
>
> is the **order of interference**: the equivalent path difference between successive beams, in wavelengths.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.1, eqs. (4.8)–(4.10)*

^def-b10-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.4.3: Maximum and Minimum Transmission
> Under the assumptions of [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-2|Theorem §B10.4.2]]:
> 1. The transmission is largest at every order, $\Delta = 2\pi N$, and smallest halfway between:
>    $$
>    \mathcal T_{\max} = \frac{T^2}{(1 - R)^2}, \qquad \mathcal T_{\min} = \frac{T^2}{(1 + R)^2}, \qquad \frac{\mathcal T_{\max}}{\mathcal T_{\min}} = 1 + F .
>    $$
> 2. If the surfaces absorb nothing ($R + T = 1$), then $\mathcal T_{\max} = 1$ for any $R$, however close to $1$. The reflected intensity is then the complement, $I_R/I_0 = F\sin^2(\Delta/2)/\bigl[1 + F\sin^2(\Delta/2)\bigr]$: narrow dark lines on a bright background.
> 3. If each surface absorbs a fraction $A$ ($A + R + T = 1$), then $\mathcal T_{\max} = \bigl[1 - A/(1 - R)\bigr]^2$.
> 4. For weak reflection, $R \ll 1$, $I_T/(I_0\mathcal T_{\max}) \approx 1 - 2R(1 - \cos\Delta)$. These are two-beam fringes of low visibility, the picture of [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^thm-a6-3-2|Theorem §A6.3.2]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.1, eqs. (4.13)–(4.15); parts 2 (reflection) and 4 derived here*

^thm-b10-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $\sin^2(\Delta/2)$ runs from $0$ to $1$, so $\mathcal A$ runs from $1$ down to $1/(1 + F)$, and $(1 - R)^2(1 + F) = (1 - R)^2 + 4R = (1 + R)^2$. (2) $T = 1 - R$ makes $T^2/(1 - R)^2 = 1$. Without absorption, energy conservation gives $I_R = I_0 - I_T$, and $1 - \mathcal A = F\sin^2/(1 + F\sin^2)$. (3) $T = 1 - R - A$, so $T/(1 - R) = 1 - A/(1 - R)$. (4) For $R \ll 1$, $F \approx 4R$ and $1/(1 + F\sin^2(\Delta/2)) \approx 1 - 4R\sin^2(\Delta/2) = 1 - 2R(1 - \cos\Delta)$ to first order in $R$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]). That is the cosine fringe of two interfering beams whose amplitudes are in the ratio $R$: the directly transmitted beam and the one that made a single round trip.

^der-b10-4-3

*Uses:* [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-2|Theorem §B10.4.2]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Why a little absorption costs so much
> A lossless Fabry–Perot transmits all the light at its peaks, even when each mirror alone transmits only $1\%$ (Theorem §B10.4.3, part 2). This happens because the light builds up inside to many times the incident intensity. That same build-up multiplies any loss. By part 3, mirrors with $R = 0.90$ and only $A = 0.05$ give $\mathcal T_{\max} = (1 - 0.5)^2 = 0.25$ ([[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^ex-b10-4-1|Example §B10.4.1]]). Metal coatings (silver, aluminium) are limited by absorption to useful reflectances of about $0.8$–$0.9$. Dielectric multilayers ([[§B10.5 Multilayer Films#^thm-b10-5-4|Theorem §B10.5.4]]) reach $0.99$ with almost no absorption.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.1, §4.3*

## The Fabry–Perot interferometer

> [!definition] Definition §B10.4.2: Fabry–Perot Interferometer; Etalon
> A **Fabry–Perot interferometer** consists of two optically flat plates of glass or quartz whose partially reflecting surfaces face each other and are held accurately parallel. If the spacing $d$ can be varied, the device is an interferometer; if the plates are fixed by spacers, it is an **etalon**. The surfaces must be flat and parallel to about $\lambda/20$–$\lambda/100$ (a $\lambda/4$ optical flat is not good enough). It is used in two ways.
> - **Rings.** Light from a broad source is collimated, passes the plates, and is focused by a lens. Each direction $\theta$ has its own $\Delta$, so the focal plane shows concentric rings, fringes of equal inclination as in [[§B10.1 Two-Beam Interference and the Michelson Interferometer#^thm-b10-1-3|Theorem §B10.1.3]], but sharp.
> - **Scanning.** A point source is imaged on a pinhole at the ring centre ($\theta = 0$), and $d$ or the index of the gas between the plates is varied while a photodetector records the transmitted intensity. The record is a plot of the Airy function, one for each frequency component of the light.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.2, Figs. 4.4–4.5*

^def-b10-4-2

> [!definition] Definition §B10.4.3: Free Spectral Range; Fringe Width; Reflecting Finesse
> At fixed $\theta$, the **free spectral range** (FSR) is the separation, in frequency or wavelength, between successive orders of transmission. The **fringe width** is the full width at half maximum of one transmission peak. The **reflecting finesse** is their ratio,
>
> $$
> \mathcal F = \frac{\text{free spectral range}}{\text{fringe width}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.2–4.3, eq. (4.20)*

^def-b10-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.4.4: Free Spectral Range, Fringe Width and Finesse
> For a Fabry–Perot with spacing $d$, index $n$ and reflectance $R$ (with $\delta_r$ independent of frequency):
>
> $$
> \Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}} = \frac{c}{2nd\cos\theta} \approx \frac{c}{2nd}, \qquad \Delta\lambda_{\text{FSR}} \approx \frac{\lambda_0^2}{2nd},
> $$
>
> $$
> \mathcal F = \frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt F = \frac{\pi\sqrt R}{1 - R}, \qquad \delta\nu = \frac{\Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}}}{\mathcal F}, \qquad \delta\omega = \frac cd\,\frac{1 - R}{\sqrt R}\ \ (n = 1,\ \theta = 0),
> $$
>
> where $\delta\nu$ is the fringe width. The finesse formula requires $F \gg 1$, i.e. narrow peaks.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.2–4.3, eqs. (4.16), (4.18)–(4.20)*

^thm-b10-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-1|Theorem §B10.4.1]], $\Delta = (2\omega/c)\,nd\cos\theta + \delta_r$ is linear in $\omega$ with slope $2nd\cos\theta/c$. Successive orders differ by $2\pi$ in $\Delta$, i.e. by $\pi c/(nd\cos\theta)$ in $\omega$ and $c/(2nd\cos\theta)$ in $\nu$. In wavelength, $\lvert\Delta\lambda\rvert = \lambda^2\Delta\nu/c$. The transmission falls to half its peak where $F\sin^2(\Delta/2) = 1$. Near the peak at $2\pi N$ put $\Delta = 2\pi N + \varepsilon$: $\sin^2(\varepsilon/2) = 1/F$, so $\varepsilon = \pm 2\arcsin F^{-1/2} \approx \pm 2/\sqrt F$ for $F \gg 1$. The full width is $4/\sqrt F$ in $\Delta$, against an order spacing of $2\pi$, so $\mathcal F = 2\pi/(4/\sqrt F) = (\pi/2)\sqrt F = \pi\sqrt R/(1 - R)$. Converting with the slope, for $n = 1$, $\theta = 0$: $\delta\omega = (c/2d)(4/\sqrt F) = (2c/d)(1 - R)/(2\sqrt R)$.

^der-b10-4-4

*Uses:* [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-1|Theorem §B10.4.1]], [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-2|Theorem §B10.4.2]], [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^def-b10-4-3|Def. §B10.4.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B10.4.5: Resolving Power of a Fabry–Perot Interferometer
> Take two spectral lines of equal intensity to be resolved when their transmission peaks are one fringe width apart (the **Taylor criterion**). Each curve then crosses the other at half intensity, so the summed intensity at the saddle point between them equals the peak intensity of either line alone. The resolving power is then
>
> $$
> \mathrm{RP} = \frac{\nu}{\delta\nu} = \frac{\lambda}{\lvert\delta\lambda\rvert} = N\mathcal F = N\,\frac{\pi\sqrt R}{1 - R}, \qquad N \approx \frac{2nd}{\lambda_0} .
> $$
>
> It grows with the order (with the spacing $d$) and with the reflectance.
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.3, eqs. (4.17)–(4.21), Fig. 4.6*

^thm-b10-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Two equal lines whose peaks are a fringe width apart each sit at half height at the midpoint between them, where the sum is therefore $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12$ of one peak. Fowles reaches the same separation, $\lvert\Delta - \Delta'\rvert = 4/\sqrt F$, by setting the sum at the midpoint equal to one peak. By [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-4|Theorem §B10.4.4]] the smallest resolvable $\delta\nu$ is the fringe width $\Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}}/\mathcal F$. At a peak, $\nu = N\,\Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}}$ when $\delta_r$ is negligible against $2\pi N$, as it is for $N$ of thousands or more ([[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^def-b10-4-1|Def. §B10.4.1]] with $\nu = c/\lambda_0$). Hence $\nu/\delta\nu = N\mathcal F$, and $\lambda/\lvert\delta\lambda\rvert = \nu/\delta\nu$.

^der-b10-4-5

*Uses:* [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-4|Theorem §B10.4.4]], [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^def-b10-4-1|Def. §B10.4.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Fabry–Perot against grating
> A grating resolves $\lambda/\Delta\lambda = mN$ with $N$ illuminated lines in order $m$ ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-3|Theorem §A7.4.3]]; derived from diffraction theory in [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-6|Theorem §B11.2.6]]), usually with $m$ of $1$ to $3$ and $N$ up to some $10^4$–$10^5$. In a Fabry–Perot the order $N = 2nd/\lambda$ is itself $10^4$–$10^6$. The finesse plays the role of the number of lines, being of the order of the number of beams that contribute appreciably: $R^m$ falls to $1/e$ after $m \approx 1/(1 - R) \approx \mathcal F/\pi$ round trips. The price is a free spectral range so small that lines farther apart than it overlap in neighbouring orders, so a Fabry–Perot is used behind a coarser instrument or on light already known to lie within one free spectral range. The Taylor criterion is the multiple-beam counterpart of Rayleigh's ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^def-a7-4-1|Def. §A7.4.1]]); both are conventions for "a visible dip".
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.3*

^rem-b10-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: The Fabry–Perot as a resonator
> Near a peak, $\Delta - 2\pi N = 2\pi(\nu - \nu_N)/\Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}}$ is small, and $\sin^2(\Delta/2) \approx \bigl[\pi(\nu - \nu_N)/\Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}}\bigr]^2$. The Airy function becomes
>
> $$
> \mathcal A \approx \frac{1}{1 + F\pi^2(\nu - \nu_N)^2/\Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}}^2} = \frac{(\delta\nu/2)^2}{(\nu - \nu_N)^2 + (\delta\nu/2)^2}, \qquad \delta\nu = \frac{2\,\Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}}}{\pi\sqrt F} = \frac{\Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}}}{\mathcal F},
> $$
>
> a Lorentzian ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^def-b1-4-1|Def. §B1.4.1]]). Each order is a resonance of the light stored between the mirrors, and its quality factor is $Q = \nu_N/\delta\nu = N\mathcal F$, equal to the resolving power. The width also fixes how long light stays inside. Per round trip, of duration $2nd/c$, the stored intensity is multiplied by $R^2$, so it decays at the rate $1/\tau_c = -\ln(R^2)\,c/2nd \approx (1 - R)c/nd$ for $R \to 1$. Then $2\pi\,\delta\nu\,\tau_c = 1/\sqrt R \approx 1$: the resonance is as narrow in frequency as the stored light is long-lived. This is the rule of [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^rem-b1-4-3|Remark: Narrow in frequency, long in time]] for the energy decay rate and the FWHM of a damped oscillator. A laser's optical resonator is such a pair of mirrors ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^mod-a5-4-8|QM Model §A5.4.8]]). Its axial modes $L = m\lambda/2$ are the transmission orders at $\theta = 0$ (each metal or high-index reflection contributes $\pi$, so $\delta_r = 2\pi$). They are the one-dimensional case of the cavity modes of [[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]], spaced by the free spectral range $c/2L$. Curved-mirror resonators and their stability: [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators|§B12.2]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §4.1–4.3 (Problem 4.3 calls the Fabry–Perot a laser resonator); the Lorentzian form and the decay time are derived here*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B10.4.1: A Silver-Coated Fabry–Perot
> Each plate is silvered to $R = 0.90$, $T = 0.05$, $A = 0.05$. By [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-3|Theorem §B10.4.3]] and [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-4|Theorem §B10.4.4]],
>
> $$
> \mathcal T_{\max} = \frac{(0.05)^2}{(0.10)^2} = 0.25, \qquad \mathcal T_{\min} = \frac{(0.05)^2}{(1.90)^2} = 6.9 \times 10^{-4}, \qquad F = \frac{3.6}{0.01} = 360, \qquad \mathcal F = \frac{\pi\sqrt{0.9}}{0.1} = 29.8 .
> $$
>
> Three quarters of the light is lost at the peaks, although each plate absorbs only $5\%$ ([[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^cau-b10-4-1|Caution: Why a little absorption costs so much]]), and the contrast between peak and trough is $1 + F = 361$. With the plates $d = 1.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ apart in air and $\lambda = 500\ \mathrm{nm}$ near normal incidence,
>
> $$
> N = \frac{2d}{\lambda} = 4.0 \times 10^4, \qquad \mathrm{RP} = N\mathcal F = 1.2 \times 10^6, \qquad \delta\lambda = \frac{\lambda}{\mathrm{RP}} = 4.2 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{nm},
> $$
>
> with a free spectral range $c/2d = 15\ \mathrm{GHz}$ ($\lambda^2/2d = 0.0125\ \mathrm{nm}$) and a fringe width of $0.50\ \mathrm{GHz}$. A grating needs $1.2 \times 10^6$ illuminated lines in first order to match this ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-3|Theorem §A7.4.3]]). Any two lines to be compared must, however, lie within $0.0125\ \mathrm{nm}$ of each other, or their orders overlap.
>
> *Source: Fowles Problems 4.1–4.2*

^ex-b10-4-1

> [!example] Example §B10.4.2: A Laser Resonator
> The mirrors of a He–Ne laser resonator ($\lambda = 633\ \mathrm{nm}$) have $R = 0.99$ and are $d = 1.0\ \mathrm m$ apart. As a passive Fabry–Perot (Theorem §B10.4.4):
>
> $$
> \Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}} = \frac{c}{2d} = 150\ \mathrm{MHz}, \qquad \mathcal F = \frac{\pi\sqrt{0.99}}{0.01} = 313, \qquad \delta\nu = \frac{150\ \mathrm{MHz}}{313} = 480\ \mathrm{kHz}, \qquad \delta\lambda = \frac{\lambda^2\,\delta\nu}{c} = 6.4 \times 10^{-16}\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> Its quality factor is $Q = \nu/\delta\nu = (4.74 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz})/(4.8 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{Hz}) = 9.9 \times 10^8$. The stored light decays in $\tau_c \approx d/c(1 - R) = 0.33\ \mathrm{\mu s}$, about $50$ round trips of $6.7\ \mathrm{ns}$ ([[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^rem-b10-4-2|Remark: The Fabry–Perot as a resonator]]). The axial modes the gain medium can amplify are $150\ \mathrm{MHz}$ apart. With the gain making up for the losses, an operating laser's line can be far narrower than this passive width: line widths of $10^3\ \mathrm{Hz}$ ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^ex-b10-2-1|Example §B10.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: Fowles Problem 4.3*

^ex-b10-4-2

> [!example] Example §B10.4.3: The Channeled Spectrum of a Glass Plate
> White light falls normally on an uncoated plate of index $n = 2.5$ and thickness $d = 1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$. Each face reflects $R = \bigl((n - 1)/(n + 1)\bigr)^2 = (1.5/3.5)^2 = 0.184$ (the normal-incidence reflectance, [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^rem-a6-3-1|Remark: The same rule as for waves on a string]]; derived in [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]]). Both internal reflections are from glass toward air, with no phase change ([[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^law-a6-3-1|Law §A6.3.1]]), so $\delta_r = 0$. The plate is a lossless Fabry–Perot of low finesse: $F = 4(0.184)/(0.816)^2 = 1.10$, $\mathcal F = 1.65$. By [[§B10.4 Multiple-Beam Interference and the Fabry–Perot Interferometer#^thm-b10-4-3|Theorem §B10.4.3]] the transmission oscillates between
>
> $$
> \mathcal T_{\max} = 1 \quad\text{at}\quad \lambda_N = \frac{2nd}{N}, \qquad \mathcal T_{\min} = \frac{(1 - R)^2}{(1 + R)^2} = 0.476 .
> $$
>
> Near $600\ \mathrm{nm}$ the channels are $\lambda^2/2nd = (600\ \mathrm{nm})^2/(5.0\ \mathrm{mm}) = 0.072\ \mathrm{nm}$ apart: the spectrum is finely "channeled". The average over one channel is $(1 + F)^{-1/2} = 0.690 = (1 - R)/(1 + R)$, which is exactly what one gets by adding the multiply reflected beams' intensities instead of their amplitudes. Light whose coherence length is shorter than the round trip $2nd = 5\ \mathrm{mm}$ ([[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-5|Theorem §B10.2.5]]), or a spectrometer too coarse to see $0.07\ \mathrm{nm}$, therefore sees only this average. That is why a thick window shows no interference colours.
>
> *Source: Fowles Problems 4.5–4.6; the average over a channel is computed here*
