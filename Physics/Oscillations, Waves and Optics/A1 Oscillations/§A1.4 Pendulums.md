A pendulum is restored by gravity, or by a twisted wire, rather than by a spring, and it turns about an axis rather than sliding along a line. Its restoring torque is not exactly proportional to the angle, but for small swings it is, and the rotational second law then reproduces the oscillator equation of [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion|§A1.1]] with the angle in place of $x$. This section finds the periods of the simple, the physical and the torsion pendulum.

## The simple pendulum

> [!model] Model §A1.4.1: Simple Pendulum
> A point mass $m$ (the **bob**) hangs from a fixed frictionless pivot on a massless, inextensible string of length $L$ and swings in a vertical plane; air drag is neglected. Its position is the angle $\theta$ of the string from the vertical, positive counterclockwise; the bob is then at arc length $s = L\theta$ from the lowest point. The forces on the bob are its weight and the tension of the string.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.4*

^mod-a1-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.2: Period of a Simple Pendulum
> The angle of a simple pendulum obeys
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\frac gL\sin\theta .
> $$
>
> For small angles, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, the motion is simple harmonic, $\theta(t) = \Theta\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, with
>
> $$
> \omega = \sqrt{\frac gL}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac Lg} ,
> $$
>
> independent of the mass of the bob and of the angular amplitude $\Theta$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.4, eqs. (15.17)–(15.19)*

^thm-a1-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take torques about the pivot. The tension acts along the string, through the pivot, and has no torque. The weight acts at distance $L$ from the pivot, and only its component $mg\sin\theta$ perpendicular to the string has a torque, $\tau = -mgL\sin\theta$; the minus sign says it turns the bob back toward $\theta = 0$. The bob's moment of inertia about the pivot is $I = mL^2$. The rotational second law $I\,d^2\theta/dt^2 = \tau$ gives
>
> $$
> mL^2\,\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -mgL\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -\frac gL\sin\theta .
> $$
>
> (Equivalently, along the arc: $m\,d^2s/dt^2 = -mg\sin\theta$ with $s = L\theta$.) By Taylor's theorem $\sin\theta = \theta + R$ with $|R| \le |\theta|^3/6$. Dropping $R$ gives $d^2\theta/dt^2 = -(g/L)\,\theta$, the oscillator equation of [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]] with $k/m$ replaced by $g/L$.

> [!remark] Remark: How small is small?
> The relative error of $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ is about $\theta^2/6$: below $1\%$ for $\theta < 0.245\ \mathrm{rad} \approx 14^\circ$, and about $1\%$ at $15^\circ$. In terms of the arc length $s = L\theta$, the cubic term is negligible when $s \ll \sqrt6\,L$. The exact equation is not linear; it has no elementary solution, and its period grows slowly with the amplitude. For amplitudes below about $15^\circ$ the small-angle period is good to better than $1\%$, which is why even simple pendulum clocks keep good time.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 7*

^rem-a1-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why the mass drops out
> Both the restoring torque, $mgL\sin\theta$, and the rotational inertia, $mL^2$, are proportional to $m$, so a 100 kg bob swings with the same period as a 10 kg bob on the same string. A pendulum measures $g$, not $m$; and since $T \propto \sqrt L$, a clock whose rod expands in the heat runs slow ([[§A1.2 Thermal Expansion#^ex-a1-2-3|TH Example §A1.2.3]]).

^rem-a1-4-2

## The physical pendulum

> [!definition] Definition §A1.4.1: Physical Pendulum
> A **physical pendulum** is any rigid body that swings under gravity about a fixed horizontal axis through a point $O$ other than its centre of mass, and that cannot be treated as a point mass on a string. Its relevant properties are its mass $m$, its moment of inertia $I$ about the axis through $O$, and the distance $L$ from $O$ to the centre of mass.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.4*

^def-a1-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.3: Period of a Physical Pendulum
> For small angles a physical pendulum performs SHM with
>
> $$
> \omega = \sqrt{\frac{mgL}{I}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{mgL}} ,
> $$
>
> where $I$ is the moment of inertia about the pivot and $L$ the distance from the pivot to the centre of mass. For a point mass on a string, $I = mL^2$ and $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ again.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.4, eqs. (15.20)–(15.21)*

^thm-a1-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Gravity acts on the body as if at its centre of mass, at distance $L$ from $O$. With $\theta$ the angle of the line from $O$ to the centre of mass from the vertical, the torque of the weight about $O$ is $-mgL\sin\theta$; the force of the pivot acts at $O$ and has no torque. The rotational second law gives $I\,d^2\theta/dt^2 = -mgL\sin\theta \approx -mgL\,\theta$ for small angles, so $d^2\theta/dt^2 = -(mgL/I)\,\theta$, and [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]] applies with $k/m$ replaced by $mgL/I$.

^der-a1-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A1.4 Pendulums#^def-a1-4-1|Def. §A1.4.1]], [[§A6.3 Torque and the Second Law for Rotation#^thm-a6-3-1|CM Theorem §A6.3.1]], [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Which $L$, which $I$?
> - $L$ is the pivot-to-centre-of-mass distance, not the length of the body. For a rod pivoted at one end, $L$ is half the rod's length.
> - $I$ is taken about the pivot, not about the centre of mass; the parallel-axis theorem $I = I_{\text{cm}} + mL^2$ ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-3|CM Theorem §A6.2.3]]) converts one into the other.

## The torsion pendulum

> [!model] Model §A1.4.4: Torsion Pendulum
> A rigid body hangs from a wire or fibre and turns about the wire's axis. Twisting the wire through an angle $\theta$ makes it exert a restoring torque proportional to the angle,
>
> $$
> \tau = -\kappa\theta ,
> $$
>
> where the **torsion constant** $\kappa$ (in $\mathrm{N\cdot m/rad}$) depends on the wire's material and dimensions. The torque comes from the shear of the wire; the linear relation holds for small twists, in the same way as Hooke's law for a spring.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.4*

^mod-a1-4-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.4.5: Period of a Torsion Pendulum
> A torsion pendulum with moment of inertia $I$ about the wire's axis oscillates harmonically, $\theta(t) = \Theta\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, with
>
> $$
> \omega = \sqrt{\frac\kappa I}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac I\kappa} ,
> $$
>
> for any amplitude within the range where $\tau = -\kappa\theta$ holds.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.4, eq. (15.22)*

^thm-a1-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The rotational second law with the torque of the model is $I\,d^2\theta/dt^2 = -\kappa\theta$, the oscillator equation with $m \to I$ and $k \to \kappa$; apply [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]. No small-angle approximation is needed beyond the linearity of the wire.

^der-a1-4-5

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.4.1: Measuring g with a Pendulum
> A simple pendulum of length $L = 75.000\ \mathrm{cm}$ has period $T = 1.7357\ \mathrm s$. Squaring $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ ([[§A1.4 Pendulums#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]]) and solving for $g$,
>
> $$
> g = \frac{4\pi^2 L}{T^2} = \frac{4\pi^2\,(0.75000\ \mathrm m)}{(1.7357\ \mathrm s)^2} = 9.8281\ \mathrm{m/s^2} .
> $$
>
> The method can be very precise, provided the small-angle approximation is better than the five-figure data. Keeping the relative error $\theta^2/6$ of $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ ([[§A1.4 Pendulums#^rem-a1-4-1|Remark: How small is small?]]) below $10^{-5}$ needs amplitudes under about $0.5^\circ$; this is a safe bound, since the error of the period itself is smaller.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 15.3*

^ex-a1-4-1

> [!example] Example §A1.4.2: A Pendulum to Counter the Sway of a Skyscraper
> A uniform beam of mass $M = 100$ metric tons, pivoted at one end, is to swing at the building's sway frequency $f = 0.50\ \mathrm{Hz}$ ($T = 2.0\ \mathrm s$). How long must it be?
>
> For a beam of length $\ell$ pivoted at an end, $I = \tfrac13 M\ell^2$ ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-2|CM Theorem §A6.2.2]]) and the centre of mass is at $L = \ell/2$ from the pivot. By [[§A1.4 Pendulums#^thm-a1-4-3|Theorem §A1.4.3]],
>
> $$
> T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\tfrac13 M\ell^2}{Mg\,\ell/2}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2\ell}{3g}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ell = \frac{3g}{2}\Bigl(\frac{T}{2\pi}\Bigr)^2 = \frac{3\,(9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2})}{2}\Bigl(\frac{2.0\ \mathrm s}{2\pi}\Bigr)^2 = 1.5\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> The mass drops out. Using the full length $\ell$ for $L$, a common slip, would give twice this, $3.0$ m. (A simple pendulum with the same period would be $g(T/2\pi)^2 = 0.99$ m long: the beam must be longer because its mass is spread out toward the pivot.)
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 15.4*

^ex-a1-4-2

> [!example] Example §A1.4.3: Measuring a Torsion Constant
> A uniform rod of length $\ell = 0.30\ \mathrm m$ and mass $M = 4.00\ \mathrm{kg}$ hangs from a string attached at its centre and twists back and forth about the string with period $T = 0.50\ \mathrm s$. About its centre, $I = \tfrac1{12}M\ell^2 = 0.0300\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2}$ ([[§A6.2 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Energy#^thm-a6-2-2|CM Theorem §A6.2.2]]). Solving $T = 2\pi\sqrt{I/\kappa}$ ([[§A1.4 Pendulums#^thm-a1-4-5|Theorem §A1.4.5]]) for $\kappa$,
>
> $$
> \kappa = I\Bigl(\frac{2\pi}{T}\Bigr)^2 = (0.0300\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2})\Bigl(\frac{2\pi}{0.50\ \mathrm s}\Bigr)^2 = 4.74\ \mathrm{N\cdot m/rad} .
> $$
>
> Timing the oscillation is how torsion constants are measured; with $\kappa$ known, a torsion balance measures tiny torques by the angle they produce.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 15.5*

^ex-a1-4-3
