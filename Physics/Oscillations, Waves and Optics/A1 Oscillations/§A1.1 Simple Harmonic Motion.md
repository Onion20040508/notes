A plucked guitar string, a child on a swing, a block on a spring: each moves back and forth about an equilibrium position and repeats itself. This section names the quantities that describe such periodic motion and solves the simplest case, a restoring force proportional to the displacement. The motion is a pure cosine whose frequency is set by stiffness and mass alone; energy, pendulums, damping and driving in the rest of the chapter build on this one solution.

## Periodic motion

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.1: Periodic Motion; Period; Frequency
> A motion is **periodic** if it repeats itself at regular time intervals. One complete repetition is a **cycle** (an *oscillation*). The **period** $T$ is the time for one cycle; the **frequency** $f$ is the number of cycles per unit time:
>
> $$
> f = \frac1T .
> $$
>
> The SI unit of frequency is the **hertz**, $1\ \mathrm{Hz} = 1$ cycle per second $= 1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.1, eq. (15.1)*

^def-a1-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.2: Simple Harmonic Motion; Restoring Force
> A system oscillating about an **equilibrium position** $x = 0$ (where the net force on it vanishes) performs **simple harmonic motion** (SHM) if its acceleration, and hence the net force on it, is proportional to the displacement $x$ and points opposite to it. Such a net force, always directed back toward equilibrium, is a **restoring force**. A system that performs SHM is a **simple harmonic oscillator**.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.1*

^def-a1-1-2

> [!definition] Definition §A1.1.3: Amplitude, Angular Frequency, Phase
> A sinusoidal motion is written
>
> $$
> x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad A \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> - The **amplitude** $A$ is the largest displacement from equilibrium; $x$ runs between $-A$ and $+A$.
> - The **angular frequency** $\omega$, in $\mathrm{rad/s}$ (dimension $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$), is $2\pi$ times the frequency: $\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi/T$, because the cosine repeats when its argument grows by $2\pi$.
> - The argument $\omega t + \phi$ is the **phase** of the motion, and the constant $\phi$ (in radians) is the **phase constant**, which University Physics calls the *phase shift*. It sets where in its cycle the motion is at $t = 0$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.1, eqs. (15.2)–(15.3)*

^def-a1-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: Angular frequency and circular motion
> The name comes from uniform circular motion, where $\omega$ is the rate at which the angle grows ([[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^def-a2-7-2|CM Def. §A2.7.2]]). [[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion|§A1.3]] shows that this is more than an analogy: SHM is the shadow of uniform circular motion.

^rem-a1-1-1

## The mass on a spring

> [!model] Model §A1.1.1: Simple Harmonic Oscillator
> A block of mass $m$ slides on a frictionless horizontal surface, attached to one end of a spring whose other end is fixed. The spring is massless and obeys Hooke's law ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^law-a3-6-3|CM Law §A3.6.3]]) with force constant $k$; there is no friction, drag or other damping. The position $x$ of the block is measured from the point where the spring is relaxed. Weight and normal force cancel, so the net force is the spring force
>
> $$
> F_x = -kx .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.1*

^mod-a1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.2: Motion of the Simple Harmonic Oscillator
> The block of [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^mod-a1-1-1|Model §A1.1.1]] obeys
>
> $$
> \frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\frac{k}{m}\,x ,
> $$
>
> and every solution of this equation is
>
> $$
> x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac km}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac mk}, \qquad f = \frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac km} .
> $$
>
> The amplitude and phase constant are fixed by the initial position $x_0$ and velocity $v_0$:
>
> $$
> A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + \Bigl(\frac{v_0}{\omega}\Bigr)^2}, \qquad \cos\phi = \frac{x_0}{A}, \qquad \sin\phi = -\frac{v_0}{\omega A} .
> $$
>
> The period depends only on $m$ and $k$, not on the amplitude.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.1, eqs. (15.9)–(15.11) · PHY 287, Problem Set 7 (initial conditions)*

^thm-a1-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The equation.* Newton's second law with the net force of the model gives $m\,d^2x/dt^2 = -kx$.
>
> *The cosine solves it.* For $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ the chain rule gives $d^2x/dt^2 = -\omega^2 A\cos(\omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2 x$. This equals $-(k/m)x$ at all times exactly when $\omega^2 = k/m$. The cosine repeats when $\omega t$ grows by $2\pi$, so $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$.
>
> *Any initial state can be matched.* We need $x(0) = A\cos\phi = x_0$ and $v(0) = -A\omega\sin\phi = v_0$, i.e. $(A\cos\phi, A\sin\phi) = (x_0, -v_0/\omega)$. That is the point $(x_0, -v_0/\omega)$ of the plane in polar coordinates: $A$ is its distance from the origin and $\phi$ its polar angle.
>
> *There are no other solutions.* If $x_1$ and $x_2$ are solutions with the same $x_0$ and $v_0$, their difference $d = x_1 - x_2$ is a solution with $d(0) = 0$, $d'(0) = 0$. The quantity $\tfrac12 m d'^2 + \tfrac12 k d^2$ has derivative $d'(m d'' + kd) = 0$, so it is constant ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]) and equal to its initial value $0$. A sum of two squares that vanishes forces $d \equiv 0$.

^der-a1-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^mod-a1-1-1|Model §A1.1.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the result says
> - **Stiffer means faster, heavier means slower:** $T \propto \sqrt{m/k}$. A heavy diver bounces more slowly on a board than a light one.
> - **Isochronism:** the period does not depend on how far the oscillator is pulled. A guitar string sounds the same note whether plucked gently or hard. This holds because the force is exactly linear in $x$; for other forces it holds only for small amplitudes ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-3|Theorem §A1.2.3]]).
> - **Other forms of the same solution.** Expanding the cosine, $x(t) = x_0\cos\omega t + (v_0/\omega)\sin\omega t$, with the two constants read off directly from the initial state. Since $\cos\theta = \sin(\theta + \pi/2)$, a sine with a different phase constant describes the same motion.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 8 slides*

^rem-a1-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Finding the phase constant
> - $\tan\phi = -v_0/(\omega x_0)$ fixes $\phi$ only up to $\pi$: a calculator's $\arctan$ returns an angle between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$, which is wrong whenever $x_0 < 0$. Choose the quadrant from the signs of $\cos\phi = x_0/A$ and $\sin\phi = -v_0/(\omega A)$ (worked case: [[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^ex-a1-3-1|Example §A1.3.1]]). The minus sign in $\sin\phi$ is easily lost.
> - With $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, a positive $\phi$ shifts the graph of $x(t)$ to the *left* (earlier) by $\phi/\omega$.
> - The phase is in radians: set the calculator accordingly.

^cau-a1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.3: Velocity and Acceleration in SHM
> If $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, then
>
> $$
> v(t) = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi), \qquad a(t) = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2 x(t) .
> $$
>
> The speed is largest, $v_{\max} = A\omega$, at the equilibrium position; the acceleration has its largest magnitude, $a_{\max} = A\omega^2$, at the turning points $x = \pm A$, where it points toward equilibrium.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.1, eqs. (15.4)–(15.8)*

^thm-a1-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate with the chain rule: $\frac{d}{dt}\cos(\omega t + \phi) = -\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi)$ and $\frac{d}{dt}\sin(\omega t + \phi) = \omega\cos(\omega t + \phi)$. The sine is $\pm1$ exactly where the cosine is $0$, i.e. at $x = 0$; the cosine is $\pm 1$ at $x = \pm A$, where $a = -\omega^2 x = \mp A\omega^2$.

^der-a1-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^def-a1-1-3|Def. §A1.1.3]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]

## Vertical spring

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.1.4: Mass on a Vertical Spring
> A block of mass $m$ hangs from an ideal spring of force constant $k$. In equilibrium the spring is stretched by $\Delta y = mg/k$. Measured from this new equilibrium position, the net force on the block is $-ky$, so the block performs SHM about it,
>
> $$
> y(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac km},
> $$
>
> with the same angular frequency as on a horizontal table. Gravity only shifts the equilibrium.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.1*

^thm-a1-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take $y$ upward and let $y_0$ be the position of the spring's lower end when it is relaxed. At position $y$ the spring is stretched by $y_0 - y$ and pulls up with $k(y_0 - y)$; the weight pulls down with $mg$. The net force $F = k(y_0 - y) - mg$ vanishes at $y_1 = y_0 - mg/k$, so $\Delta y = y_0 - y_1 = mg/k$. Substituting $mg = k(y_0 - y_1)$,
>
> $$
> F = k(y_0 - y) - k(y_0 - y_1) = -k(y - y_1) .
> $$
>
> Moving the origin to $y_1$ gives $F = -ky$, and [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]] applies with $x$ replaced by $y$.

^der-a1-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.6 Common Forces#^law-a3-6-3|CM Law §A3.6.3]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The electrical twin
> A charged capacitor discharging through an inductor obeys the same equation with $q$ for $x$, $L$ for $m$ and $1/C$ for $k$; its charge oscillates as $q_0\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$ with $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ ([[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^thm-a10-5-4|EM Theorem §A10.5.4]]; the full dictionary is in [[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^rem-a10-5-2|EM §A10.5]]). Any system whose net force or torque is proportional to minus its displacement is a simple harmonic oscillator; pendulums are the next examples ([[§A1.4 Pendulums|§A1.4]]).

^rem-a1-1-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.1.1: Equations of Motion of a Block on a Spring
> A block of mass $m = 2.00\ \mathrm{kg}$ on a frictionless table is attached to a spring with $k = 32.00\ \mathrm{N/m}$, pulled to $x = +0.020\ \mathrm m$ and released from rest. By [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]],
>
> $$
> \omega = \sqrt{\frac km} = \sqrt{\frac{32.00\ \mathrm{N/m}}{2.00\ \mathrm{kg}}} = 4.00\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 1.57\ \mathrm s .
> $$
>
> Released from rest at $x_0 = +0.020$ m: $A = 0.020\ \mathrm m$ and $\phi = 0$. Then $v_{\max} = A\omega = 0.080\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $a_{\max} = A\omega^2 = 0.32\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, and
>
> $$
> x(t) = (0.020\ \mathrm m)\cos(4.00\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\,t), \quad v(t) = -(0.080\ \mathrm{m/s})\sin(4.00\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\,t), \quad a(t) = -(0.32\ \mathrm{m/s^2})\cos(4.00\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\,t).
> $$
>
> At $t = T/4 = 0.39\ \mathrm s$ the block crosses equilibrium moving in the $-x$ direction at its top speed $0.080$ m/s.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 15.2*

^ex-a1-1-1

> [!example] Example §A1.1.2: When Does a Mass Leave a Vibrating Platform?
> A horizontal platform moves vertically in SHM, $y = A\cos\omega t$ with $A = 5.0\ \mathrm{cm}$ and $\omega = 20\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ ($y$ up, measured from the centre of the motion). A small mass is placed on it at the lowest point. Where does it leave the platform?
>
> While in contact, the mass moves with the platform, so $a_y = -\omega^2 y$ ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]]). The forces on it are the normal force $N$ (up) and its weight, so $N - mg = -m\omega^2 y$, i.e.
>
> $$
> N = m\bigl(g - \omega^2 y\bigr) .
> $$
>
> A contact force can only push, $N \ge 0$, so contact requires $y \le g/\omega^2 = (9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2})/(400\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}) = 2.45\ \mathrm{cm}$. Since $A = 5.0$ cm exceeds this, the mass leaves on the way up, at $y = +2.45\ \mathrm{cm}$ above the centre. There the platform starts to decelerate faster than $g$, and the mass, now in free fall, cannot follow. It stays on for the whole cycle only if $A\omega^2 \le g$.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Midterm 1 practice*

^ex-a1-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The derivatives of the sinusoid use the chain rule ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]); uniqueness of the solution uses that a function with zero derivative is constant ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]).
> - Several masses joined by springs oscillate in normal modes, each of them a simple harmonic motion: coupled oscillators are level B (planned).
