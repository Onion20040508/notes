Why does the oscillator move as a cosine, and why is $\omega$ called an angular frequency? Light a peg on a turning wheel from above: its shadow on the floor moves back and forth exactly like a block on a spring. Simple harmonic motion is the projection of uniform circular motion onto a diameter, and this picture gives the phase a geometric meaning. It also leads to the complex-number shortcut used for damped and driven oscillators, and to phasors, the rotating arrows with which sinusoids of one frequency (and so interfering waves) are added.

## The reference circle

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.1: Reference Circle
> For an SHM $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, the **reference circle** is the circle of radius $A$ about the equilibrium position, traversed counterclockwise at constant angular velocity $\omega$ by a **reference point** $P$ whose angle with the $+x$ axis at time $t$ is the phase $\omega t + \phi$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.3*

^def-a1-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.3.1: SHM Is the Projection of Uniform Circular Motion
> The projection of the reference point onto the $x$ axis moves as
>
> $$
> x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad v_x(t) = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi), \qquad a_x(t) = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t + \phi) ,
> $$
>
> which is the SHM of [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]]. The angular velocity of the reference point is the angular frequency of the oscillation; its speed $A\omega$ is $v_{\max}$ and its centripetal acceleration $A\omega^2$ is $a_{\max}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.3, eqs. (15.14)–(15.16)*

^thm-a1-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Uniform circular motion of radius $A$ starting on the $+x$ axis has $\vec r = A\cos\omega t\,\hat\imath + A\sin\omega t\,\hat\jmath$, $\vec v$ of magnitude $A\omega$ tangent to the circle and $\vec a = -\omega^2\vec r$ of magnitude $A\omega^2$ toward the centre ([[§A2.7 Uniform Circular Motion#^thm-a2-7-2|CM Theorem §A2.7.2]]). Starting instead at angle $\phi$ replaces $\omega t$ by $\omega t + \phi$ throughout. The $x$-components are the three formulas. Since the projection of the acceleration is $a_x = -\omega^2 x$, the shadow obeys the oscillator equation with $\omega^2 = k/m$ ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]).

^der-a1-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: What the circle explains
> - **The phase is an angle.** $\phi$ is where $P$ starts; a phase difference between two oscillations of the same frequency is the angle between their reference points, which stays fixed as both turn.
> - **Speed is largest at the centre.** At $x = 0$ the circle's velocity is parallel to the axis, so the shadow moves at the full $A\omega$; at $x = \pm A$ it is perpendicular and the shadow stops.
> - **Amplitude independence.** Every circle turned at the same $\omega$ takes the same time $2\pi/\omega$ per revolution, whatever its radius.
> - **The other projection.** The $y$-coordinate $A\sin(\omega t + \phi) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi - \pi/2)$ is also an SHM, a quarter period behind $x$. The SHM along one axis is half of a circular motion; two of them, a quarter period apart, recombine into one.

## Complex representation

> [!remark] Remark: The reference point as a complex number
> Put the reference circle in the complex plane: the reference point is
>
> $$
> z(t) = A\,e^{i(\omega t + \phi)} = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) + iA\sin(\omega t + \phi)
> $$
>
> by Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, and the oscillation is its real part, $x = \operatorname{Re} z$. Differentiation multiplies by $i\omega$, which turns the point by $90^\circ$ and stretches it by $\omega$: $\dot z = i\omega z$ is the tangent velocity of the circle, and $\ddot z = -\omega^2 z$ is the centripetal acceleration. Because the oscillator equation is linear with real coefficients, the real part of a complex solution is a real solution. This turns derivatives into multiplications and is the standard shortcut for damped and driven oscillators ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations|§A1.5]], [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance|§A1.6]]). The statements stay real; only intermediate steps use $z$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 9 slides · PHY 300, Worksheet 1 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1*

## Phasors

> [!definition] Definition §A1.3.2: Phasor; Phasor Diagram
> The **phasor** of a sinusoid $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ is the arrow from the centre of its reference circle to the reference point ([[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^def-a1-3-1|Def. §A1.3.1]]): length $A$, at angle $\omega t + \phi$ from the $+x$ axis, rotating counterclockwise at $\omega$. The sinusoid is its projection on the horizontal axis; in the complex plane the phasor is $z = Ae^{i(\omega t + \phi)}$ and $x = \operatorname{Re} z$ ([[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^rem-a1-3-2|Remark: The reference point as a complex number]]). Phasors of sinusoids with the same angular frequency rotate together, so the angle between two of them is their phase difference and does not change. A **phasor diagram** draws them at one instant, usually $t = 0$, where each points at its phase constant.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.3 · UP3 §4.2 (the phasor method)*

^def-a1-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.3.2: Adding Sinusoids of the Same Frequency
> A sum of sinusoids with the same angular frequency $\omega$ is a sinusoid with that frequency, and its phasor is the vector sum (head to tail) of their phasors. For two,
>
> $$
> A_1\cos(\omega t + \phi_1) + A_2\cos(\omega t + \phi_2) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi),
> $$
>
> $$
> A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1A_2\cos(\phi_2 - \phi_1), \qquad A\cos\phi = A_1\cos\phi_1 + A_2\cos\phi_2, \qquad A\sin\phi = A_1\sin\phi_1 + A_2\sin\phi_2 .
> $$
>
> The amplitude ranges from $A_1 + A_2$ (phasors parallel, phase difference $0, \pm 2\pi, \dots$) down to $|A_1 - A_2|$ (antiparallel, phase difference $\pm\pi, \pm3\pi, \dots$). For equal amplitudes $A_1 = A_2 = A_0$,
>
> $$
> A_0\cos(\omega t + \phi_1) + A_0\cos(\omega t + \phi_2) = 2A_0\cos\frac{\phi_2 - \phi_1}{2}\,\cos\Bigl(\omega t + \frac{\phi_1 + \phi_2}{2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.2 (the phasor method) · PHY 287, week 9 slides (complex representation)*

^thm-a1-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The sinusoids are the real parts of $A_1e^{i\phi_1}e^{i\omega t}$ and $A_2e^{i\phi_2}e^{i\omega t}$ ([[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^def-a1-3-2|Def. §A1.3.2]]). The real part of a sum is the sum of the real parts, so their sum is the real part of $(A_1e^{i\phi_1} + A_2e^{i\phi_2})\,e^{i\omega t}$. Write the complex number in brackets in polar form, $A_1e^{i\phi_1} + A_2e^{i\phi_2} = Ae^{i\phi}$ with $A \ge 0$: it is the vector sum of the two phasors at $t = 0$, and its real and imaginary parts are the two component equations. The sum is then $\operatorname{Re}\,Ae^{i(\omega t + \phi)} = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$. Multiplying by $e^{i\omega t}$ turns all phasors by the same angle $\omega t$, so the vector-sum picture holds at every instant. Squaring and adding the components,
>
> $$
> A^2 = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1A_2(\cos\phi_1\cos\phi_2 + \sin\phi_1\sin\phi_2) = A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1A_2\cos(\phi_2 - \phi_1),
> $$
>
> the law of cosines for the triangle of phasors; $\cos(\phi_2 - \phi_1)$ runs between $-1$ and $1$, which gives the range. For equal amplitudes, factor out the mean phase: $A_0e^{i\phi_1} + A_0e^{i\phi_2} = A_0e^{i(\phi_1 + \phi_2)/2}\bigl(e^{-i\Delta/2} + e^{i\Delta/2}\bigr) = 2A_0\cos(\Delta/2)\,e^{i(\phi_1 + \phi_2)/2}$ with $\Delta = \phi_2 - \phi_1$; take the real part after multiplying by $e^{i\omega t}$. More than two sinusoids add the same way, one phasor after another.

*The phasor diagram of [[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^ex-a1-3-2|Example §A1.3.2]] at $t = 0$: phasors of amplitude $3.0$ at $0$ and $2.0$ at $60^\circ$ (blue), placed head to tail, add to the red phasor of amplitude $4.36$ at $23.4^\circ$ (the faint dashed arrow is the second phasor drawn from the origin). > [!remark] Remark: Where phasors are used
> - **Interference.** Two waves of the same frequency and amplitude meeting at a point are two sinusoids in time that differ only in phase; the equal-amplitude case gives the resultant amplitude $2A\lvert\cos(\phi/2)\rvert$ of [[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-2|Theorem §A2.5.2]] and, squared, the two-source intensity of [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]]. A wave $A\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)$ is, at a fixed point, a sinusoid whose phasor turns clockwise (because of $-\omega t$); only the angles between phasors matter, and they add in the same way.
> - **Many sources.** $N$ equal phasors, each turned by the same angle from the previous one, form a regular polygon: the multiple-slit pattern ([[§A6.2 Multiple-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-2-2|Theorem §A6.2.2]]) and, in the limit of many small phasors, the arc of single-slit diffraction ([[§A7.1 Single-Slit Diffraction#^thm-a7-1-3|Theorem §A7.1.3]]).
> - **Circuits.** In ac circuits ([[§A11.1 AC Sources and Simple AC Circuits#^def-a11-1-2|EM Def. §A11.1.2]], [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC#^thm-a11-2-1|EM Theorem §A11.2.1]]) University Physics writes sinusoids as sines and projects phasors on the vertical axis. The angles between phasors, and every result, are the same.
> - **One frequency only.** Phasors of different frequencies turn at different rates, so the angle between them drifts and the sum has no fixed amplitude: beats ([[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.3.1: Amplitude and Phase from the Initial State
> The block of [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^ex-a1-1-1|Example §A1.1.1]] ($\omega = 4.00\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$) is started at $x_0$ with velocity $v_0$. At $t = 0$ the reference point sits at $(A\cos\phi, A\sin\phi) = (x_0, -v_0/\omega)$ ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]): $x_0$ fixes its horizontal position, the velocity its vertical one. Three starts:
> - $x_0 = +0.010\ \mathrm m$, $v_0 = -0.069\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (moving left): the point is at $(0.010, 0.0173)$ m, in the first quadrant. $A = \sqrt{0.010^2 + 0.0173^2}\ \mathrm m = 0.020\ \mathrm m$ and $\phi = +\pi/3$.
> - $x_0 = +0.010\ \mathrm m$, $v_0 = +0.069\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (moving right): the point is at $(0.010, -0.0173)$ m, fourth quadrant, $\phi = -\pi/3$.
> - $x_0 = -0.010\ \mathrm m$, $v_0 = +0.069\ \mathrm{m/s}$: the point is at $(-0.010, -0.0173)$ m, third quadrant, so $\phi = -2\pi/3$. Here $\arctan\bigl(-v_0/(\omega x_0)\bigr) = \arctan(\sqrt3) = +\pi/3$ points the opposite way: the calculator answer is off by $\pi$.
>
> In all three cases $A = 0.020$ m: the amplitude depends on the energy, $\tfrac12 kA^2 = \tfrac12 kx_0^2 + \tfrac12 mv_0^2$, not on the direction of motion.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 7 (the general formulas; numbers chosen here)*

^ex-a1-3-1

> [!example] Example §A1.3.2: Adding Two Oscillations with Phasors
> A point is moved by two oscillations of the same angular frequency, $x_1 = (3.0\ \mathrm{cm})\cos\omega t$ and $x_2 = (2.0\ \mathrm{cm})\cos(\omega t + \pi/3)$. Find the resulting motion.
>
> By [[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^thm-a1-3-2|Theorem §A1.3.2]] the sum is $A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, with the components of the summed phasor at $t = 0$:
>
> $$
> A\cos\phi = 3.0 + 2.0\cos 60^\circ = 4.0\ \mathrm{cm}, \qquad A\sin\phi = 2.0\sin 60^\circ = 1.73\ \mathrm{cm},
> $$
>
> so $A = \sqrt{4.0^2 + 1.73^2}\ \mathrm{cm} = 4.36\ \mathrm{cm}$ (check: $A^2 = 9.0 + 4.0 + 2(3.0)(2.0)\cos 60^\circ = 19.0\ \mathrm{cm^2}$) and $\phi = \arctan(1.73/4.0) = 23.4^\circ = 0.41\ \mathrm{rad}$, first quadrant since both components are positive. The amplitude is less than $3.0 + 2.0 = 5.0$ cm because the two are not in phase, and the phase of the sum lies between the two phases, closer to that of the larger one.
>
> *Source: UP3 §4.2 (the phasor method); numbers chosen here*

^ex-a1-3-2

> - The complex representation rests on the arithmetic of complex numbers ([[§1 Rⁿ and Cⁿ#^ladr-1-1|LADR Def. 1.1]]); Euler's formula follows by comparing the power series of $e^{i\theta}$, $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ term by term.
