The spring force is conservative, so an undamped oscillator keeps its mechanical energy while trading it back and forth between the spring and the moving block. This section computes that energy from the solution of [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion|§A1.1]] and turns it into a speed–position relation. It then explains why simple harmonic motion is everywhere: near any stable equilibrium the potential energy is a parabola, and small oscillations are harmonic.

**Setup.** The system is still [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^mod-a1-1-1|Model §A1.1.1]] (classical, undamped, ideal spring), with $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$ measured from equilibrium; Theorems §A1.2.1–§A1.2.2 are exact for it. Theorem §A1.2.3 widens the class to any particle in one dimension near a stable minimum of a smooth potential $U(x)$, and is an approximation for small displacements.

## Energy of the oscillator

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.2.1: Energy of a Simple Harmonic Oscillator
> For the oscillator of [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^mod-a1-1-1|Model §A1.1.1]] moving as $x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, the kinetic energy $K = \tfrac12 mv^2$ and the elastic potential energy $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$ are
>
> $$
> K = \tfrac12 kA^2\sin^2(\omega t + \phi), \qquad U = \tfrac12 kA^2\cos^2(\omega t + \phi),
> $$
>
> and their sum is constant and proportional to the square of the amplitude:
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac12 kx^2 + \tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12 kA^2 .
> $$
>
> *Model:* [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^mod-a1-1-1|Model §A1.1.1]]; *applies:* as in the opening, exact; with damping the energy decays: [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]].
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.2, eq. (15.12)*

^thm-a1-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The elastic potential energy is $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$, zero at equilibrium ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-2|CM Theorem §A4.4.2]]). With $v = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi)$ ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]]) and $m\omega^2 = k$,
>
> $$
> K = \tfrac12 m A^2\omega^2\sin^2(\omega t + \phi) = \tfrac12 kA^2\sin^2(\omega t + \phi),
> $$
>
> and $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$ gives $K + U = \tfrac12 kA^2$. The constancy is no surprise: only the spring force does work, and it is conservative ([[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^thm-a4-6-2|CM Theorem §A4.6.2]]).

^der-a1-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]], [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-3|Theorem §A1.1.3]], [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-2|CM Theorem §A4.4.2]], [[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|CM Def. §A4.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.2.2: Speed at a Given Position
> At displacement $x$ the oscillator's speed is
>
> $$
> |v| = \sqrt{\frac km}\,\sqrt{A^2 - x^2} = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2} .
> $$
>
> It is largest, $\omega A$, at $x = 0$ and zero at the turning points $x = \pm A$.
>
> *Model:* [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^mod-a1-1-1|Model §A1.1.1]]; *applies:* as in the opening, exact.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.2, eq. (15.13) · PHY 287, Problem Set 7*

^thm-a1-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Solve $\tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12 kA^2$ ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-1|Theorem §A1.2.1]]) for $v^2 = (k/m)(A^2 - x^2)$. This is the general rule $|v| = \sqrt{2(E - U)/m}$ of [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|CM Theorem §A4.7.1]] for the parabola $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$.

*Uses:* [[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-1|Theorem §A1.2.1]], [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-1|CM Theorem §A4.7.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: How the energy flows
> Pulled out to $x = A$ and released, the block starts with all its energy in the spring. The spring force speeds it up; at $x = 0$ (after $T/4$) the spring is relaxed and the energy is all kinetic, $\tfrac12 m v_{\max}^2 = \tfrac12 kA^2$. The block overshoots, compresses the spring, and stops at $x = -A$ (after $T/2$) with the energy back in the spring. Averaged over a cycle, $\overline{\sin^2} = \overline{\cos^2} = \tfrac12$, so the energy is on average half kinetic and half potential: $\overline K = \overline U = \tfrac12 E$.

^rem-a1-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Energy, amplitude, and the vertical spring
> - $E \propto A^2$: doubling the amplitude quadruples the energy. At equal amplitude, oscillators store energy in proportion to their force constants: a stiff one holds far more than a loose one.
> - For a block on a vertical spring, measure $y$ from the equilibrium position *with* the block hanging ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]]). Then $\tfrac12 ky^2$ is the spring energy *plus* the gravitational energy, up to a constant: with $y_1 = y_0 - mg/k$, one finds $\tfrac12 k(y - y_0)^2 + mgy = \tfrac12 k(y - y_1)^2 + \text{const}$. Adding $mgy$ again would count gravity twice. (The energy diagram of this case: [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^ex-a4-4-3|CM Example §A4.4.3]].)

## Oscillations about a stable equilibrium

A marble in a bowl rolls back to the bottom; balanced on an upturned bowl, it rolls off. The bottom is a stable equilibrium, the top an unstable one: at a minimum of the potential energy the force $F = -dU/dx$ points back toward the equilibrium from both sides, at a maximum it points away ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^def-a4-7-2|CM Def. §A4.7.2]], [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|CM Theorem §A4.7.2]]). Near a minimum the force is approximately a spring force with effective constant $U''(x_0)$. Combined with [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion|§A1.1]], this fixes the frequency of every small oscillation.

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.2.3: Small Oscillations about a Stable Equilibrium
> Let a particle of mass $m$ move in one dimension in a potential energy $U(x)$ with a minimum at $x_0$, $U'(x_0) = 0$ and $U''(x_0) > 0$. For small displacements from $x_0$ it performs simple harmonic motion,
>
> $$
> x(t) \approx x_0 + A\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{U''(x_0)}{m}} ,
> $$
>
> with **effective force constant** $k_{\text{eff}} = U''(x_0)$. "Small" means that the next term of the force is negligible: $|x - x_0| \ll \bigl|\,2U''(x_0)/U'''(x_0)\bigr|$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.2 · PHY 287, week 9 slides · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1*

^thm-a1-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Near the minimum the force is $F \approx -U''(x_0)(x - x_0)$ ([[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|CM Theorem §A4.7.2]]): Hooke's law with $k = U''(x_0)$ for the displacement $x - x_0$. Newton's second law then gives $m\,d^2(x - x_0)/dt^2 = -U''(x_0)(x - x_0)$, and [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]] gives the motion with $\omega^2 = U''(x_0)/m$.
>
> *How small is small.* The next term of the Taylor expansion of $U$ about $x_0$, $\tfrac16 U'''(x_0)(x - x_0)^3$, adds $-\tfrac12 U'''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2$ to the force ($F = -dU/dx$). This is negligible against the linear term when $\tfrac12|U'''(x_0)|\,|x - x_0| \ll U''(x_0)$, and the higher terms are smaller still.

^der-a1-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.7 Energy Diagrams and Stability#^thm-a4-7-2|CM Theorem §A4.7.2]], [[§A4.5 Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces#^thm-a4-5-2|CM Theorem §A4.5.2]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why oscillators are everywhere
> Close enough to equilibrium, every restoring force is linear: bending trees, swings, tyres, atoms in a molecule or a crystal. The same Taylor argument applied to a force that depends on velocity as well gives, to first order, $F = -kx - b\,\dot x$, which is the damped oscillator of [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations|§A1.5]]. That is why the oscillator equation describes so many systems, and why Hooke's law, an empirical rule for springs, holds so widely. The price is that it holds only for small amplitudes; a system with $U''(x_0) = 0$ is not harmonic at all, and University Physics calls an equilibrium where the force points back only from one side *half-stable* (it is unstable).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1*

^rem-a1-2-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.2.1: Liquid in a U-Tube
> A U-tube of uniform cross-section $S$ holds a length $L$ of incompressible liquid of density $\rho$. The liquid is pushed down by $x$ on one side, so that it rises by $x$ on the other, and released. Neglect friction.
> - *Kinetic energy.* All the liquid, of mass $\rho SL$, moves with speed $|\dot x|$: $K = \tfrac12 \rho SL\,\dot x^2$.
> - *Potential energy.* Relative to equilibrium, it is as if a column of length $x$ had been taken from the low side and placed on top of the high side, raising its centre of mass by $x$: $U = (\rho S x)\,g\,x = \rho S g\,x^2$.
>
> The energy $E = \tfrac12 \rho SL\,\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 (2\rho S g)\,x^2$ has the form of [[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-1|Theorem §A1.2.1]] with "mass" $\rho SL$ and "force constant" $2\rho Sg$, so the liquid oscillates harmonically with
>
> $$
> \omega = \sqrt{\frac{2\rho S g}{\rho S L}} = \sqrt{\frac{2g}{L}} ,
> $$
>
> independent of density and cross-section. Reading $m_{\text{eff}}$ and $k_{\text{eff}}$ off the energy is often quicker than finding the forces. (A tuned liquid-column damper on a skyscraper is a U-tube whose $L$ is chosen so that this frequency matches the building's sway.)
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 7*

^ex-a1-2-1

> [!example] Example §A1.2.2: Vibration of a Diatomic Molecule
> The potential energy of two neutral atoms at separation $x$ is often modelled by the Lennard-Jones potential
>
> $$
> U(x) = 4\varepsilon\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{\sigma}{x}\Bigr)^{12} - \Bigl(\frac{\sigma}{x}\Bigr)^{6}\Bigr],
> $$
>
> with $\varepsilon$ and $\sigma$ fitted to experiment. The force is strongly repulsive at short range and weakly attractive at long range, and not at all linear; yet small vibrations are harmonic. Differentiate:
>
> $$
> U'(x) = 4\varepsilon\Bigl[-\frac{12\sigma^{12}}{x^{13}} + \frac{6\sigma^6}{x^7}\Bigr] = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; x_{\min} = 2^{1/6}\sigma \approx 1.12\,\sigma,
> $$
>
> $$
> U''(x_{\min}) = 4\varepsilon\Bigl[\frac{156\,\sigma^{12}}{x_{\min}^{14}} - \frac{42\,\sigma^6}{x_{\min}^{8}}\Bigr] = \frac{4\varepsilon}{x_{\min}^2}\Bigl[\frac{156}{4} - \frac{42}{2}\Bigr] = \frac{72\,\varepsilon}{2^{1/3}\sigma^2} \approx 57.1\,\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma^2},
> $$
>
> using $\sigma^6/x_{\min}^6 = \tfrac12$. The well depth is $U(x_{\min}) = -\varepsilon$. By [[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-3|Theorem §A1.2.3]], a light atom of mass $m$ bound to a much heavier one (which stays practically at rest) vibrates with $\omega = \sqrt{57.1\,\varepsilon/(m\sigma^2)}$, as long as the amplitude is a small fraction of $\sigma$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.2 (the potential); the expansion is worked out here*

^ex-a1-2-2
