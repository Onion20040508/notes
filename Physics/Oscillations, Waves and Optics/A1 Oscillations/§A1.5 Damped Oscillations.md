A plucked guitar string falls silent after a few seconds, and a swing stops unless someone keeps pushing it. Real oscillators lose energy to friction or drag, and the motion of [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion|§A1.1]] dies away. This section adds the simplest dissipative force, one proportional to the velocity, and solves the resulting equation. Depending on the strength of the damping, the oscillator either rings down inside an exponential envelope or creeps back to equilibrium without oscillating at all.

## The damped oscillator

> [!model] Model §A1.5.1: Linear Damping
> The oscillator of [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^mod-a1-1-1|Model §A1.1.1]] moves through a viscous fluid (or is otherwise retarded) by a damping force proportional to its velocity and opposite to it,
>
> $$
> F_D = -bv ,
> $$
>
> with a constant **damping coefficient** $b > 0$, in $\mathrm{kg/s}$. This is linear drag ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed#^law-a3-10-2|CM Law §A3.10.2]]); it is a good description when the speeds are small. Gravity, if present, only shifts the equilibrium ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.5*

^mod-a1-5-1

> [!definition] Definition §A1.5.1: Natural Angular Frequency; Under-, Critically and Overdamped
> The **natural angular frequency** of a mass–spring system is the angular frequency it would have without damping or driving,
>
> $$
> \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac km} .
> $$
>
> A damped oscillator is **underdamped** if $b < 2\sqrt{mk}$ (equivalently $b/2m < \omega_0$), **critically damped** if $b = 2\sqrt{mk}$, and **overdamped** if $b > 2\sqrt{mk}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.5, eq. (15.25)*

^def-a1-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.5.2: Motion of a Damped Oscillator
> The oscillator with linear damping obeys
>
> $$
> m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = 0 .
> $$
>
> Every solution has one of the following forms, with two constants fixed by $x(0)$ and $v(0)$:
> - **Underdamped:** the oscillator oscillates inside a decaying exponential envelope,
>
> $$
> x(t) = A_0\,e^{-bt/2m}\cos(\omega' t + \phi), \qquad \omega' = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \Bigl(\frac{b}{2m}\Bigr)^2} < \omega_0 .
> $$
>
> - **Critically damped:** $\;x(t) = (A + Bt)\,e^{-bt/2m}$.
> - **Overdamped:** with $\kappa = \sqrt{(b/2m)^2 - \omega_0^2}$, which is less than $b/2m$,
>
> $$
> x(t) = e^{-bt/2m}\bigl(A\,e^{\kappa t} + B\,e^{-\kappa t}\bigr),
> $$
>
> a sum of two decaying exponentials with no oscillation.
>
> In every case $x \to 0$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.5, eqs. (15.23)–(15.26) (underdamped case; the other two are named there) · PHY 287, week 10 slides and Problem Set 8 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1*

^thm-a1-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The equation.* The net force is $-kx - bv$, and Newton's second law gives $ma = -kx - bv$.
>
> *Strip off the decay.* Write $\beta = b/2m$ and put $x = e^{-\beta t}y$. Then $x' = e^{-\beta t}(y' - \beta y)$ and $x'' = e^{-\beta t}(y'' - 2\beta y' + \beta^2 y)$. Divide the equation by $m$ (so $b/m = 2\beta$, $k/m = \omega_0^2$) and substitute:
>
> $$
> e^{-\beta t}\Bigl[y'' - 2\beta y' + \beta^2 y + 2\beta(y' - \beta y) + \omega_0^2 y\Bigr] = e^{-\beta t}\Bigl[y'' + (\omega_0^2 - \beta^2)\,y\Bigr] = 0 .
> $$
>
> The damping term has disappeared: $y'' = (\beta^2 - \omega_0^2)\,y$.
> - If $\beta < \omega_0$: $y'' = -\omega'^2 y$, the undamped oscillator equation with $\omega'$ in place of $\omega$, so $y = A_0\cos(\omega't + \phi)$ and nothing else ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]).
> - If $\beta = \omega_0$: $y'' = 0$, so $y'$ is constant and $y = A + Bt$ ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]], twice).
> - If $\beta > \omega_0$: $y'' = \kappa^2 y$. Then $w = y' - \kappa y$ satisfies $w' = -\kappa w$, so $(we^{\kappa t})' = 0$ and $w = w(0)e^{-\kappa t}$; likewise $u = y' + \kappa y$ satisfies $u' = \kappa u$, so $u = u(0)e^{\kappa t}$. Hence $y = (u - w)/2\kappa = Ae^{\kappa t} + Be^{-\kappa t}$.
>
> Multiplying back by $e^{-\beta t}$ gives $x$. In each case two constants match $x(0)$ and $v(0)$, and since $\kappa < \beta$ both exponents $-\beta \pm \kappa$ in the overdamped case are negative.

^der-a1-5-2

> [!remark] Remark: The three regimes
> - **Light damping** barely changes the frequency, $\omega' \approx \omega_0$, so a lightly damped oscillator keeps its period while its amplitude shrinks by the same factor $e^{-bT'/2m}$ every cycle.
> - **More damping** slows the oscillation ($\omega' < \omega_0$, since the damping force opposes the motion in both directions). At $b = 2\sqrt{mk}$, $\omega'$ reaches zero and the oscillation disappears.
> - **Critical damping** returns the system to equilibrium as fast as possible without overshoot: the goal for car shock absorbers, and for a system pushed by a constant force to a new equilibrium. **Overdamping** also avoids overshoot but is slower: the slowly decaying exponential $e^{-(\beta - \kappa)t}$ lingers.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.5*

^rem-a1-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: The exponential shortcut
> Since exponentials are proportional to their derivatives, try $x = e^{\alpha t}$, possibly with complex $\alpha$. Substituting gives $m\alpha^2 + b\alpha + k = 0$, so
>
> $$
> \alpha = -\frac{b}{2m} \pm \sqrt{\Bigl(\frac{b}{2m}\Bigr)^2 - \omega_0^2} .
> $$
>
> The sign under the root decides the regime: two negative real roots (overdamped), a double root (critical), or $\alpha = -b/2m \pm i\omega'$ (underdamped), whose solutions $e^{-bt/2m}e^{\pm i\omega't}$ combine, by Euler's formula, into the real $e^{-bt/2m}\cos(\omega't + \phi)$ ([[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^rem-a1-3-2|§A1.3]]). This is how PHY 287 and Schwartz solve the equation.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 10 slides · PHY 300, Worksheet 2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1*

^rem-a1-5-2

> [!caution] Caution: Three different frequencies, and the meaning of $A_0$
> - $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ is the natural (undamped) angular frequency, $\omega'$ the frequency of the damped oscillation; University Physics writes $\omega$ for the latter, and in [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance|§A1.6]] $\omega$ will be the driving frequency. Keep the three apart.
> - $A_0$ is the amplitude of the envelope, not exactly the initial displacement. Released from rest at $x_0$, the oscillator needs $\tan\phi = -(b/2m)/\omega'$ and $A_0 = x_0\,\omega_0/\omega'$, slightly larger than $x_0$; the difference is negligible for light damping (the same point for circuits: [[§A10.6 RLC Circuits#^cau-a10-6-1|EM §A10.6]]).

^cau-a1-5-1

## Energy loss and the quality factor

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.5.3: Energy Loss of a Damped Oscillator
> The mechanical energy $E = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2$ of a damped oscillator decreases at the rate
>
> $$
> \frac{dE}{dt} = -bv^2 \le 0 ,
> $$
>
> the power of the damping force. For light damping ($b/2m \ll \omega_0$), the energy averaged over a cycle decays exponentially,
>
> $$
> E(t) \approx \tfrac12 kA_0^2\,e^{-bt/m},
> $$
>
> twice as fast as the amplitude $A_0e^{-bt/2m}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 8 and week 10 slides*

^thm-a1-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate: $dE/dt = mv\,\dot v + kx\,\dot x = v\,(ma + kx) = v\,(-bv) = -bv^2$, using the equation of motion. This is the power $F_Dv$ delivered by the damping force ([[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]]); the energy goes into heat. For light damping the motion over one cycle is nearly an SHM of amplitude $A(t) = A_0e^{-bt/2m}$, whose energy is $\tfrac12 kA(t)^2$ ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-1|Theorem §A1.2.1]]); squaring the envelope gives $e^{-bt/m}$.

^der-a1-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]], [[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]], [[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-1|Theorem §A1.2.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A1.5.2: Damping Rate; Quality Factor
> The **damping rate** $\gamma = b/m$ is the rate at which the energy of a lightly damped oscillator decays. The **quality factor** compares the natural frequency with it:
>
> $$
> Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\gamma} = \frac{m\omega_0}{b} = \frac{\sqrt{mk}}{b} .
> $$
>
> In this notation $m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = 0$ reads $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0$, and $\omega'^2 = \omega_0^2\bigl(1 - 1/(4Q^2)\bigr)$. Critical damping is $Q = \tfrac12$; overdamping $Q < \tfrac12$; light damping $Q \gg 1$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 10 slides · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1*

^def-a1-5-2

> [!remark] Remark: Reading Q
> - **Ringing time.** In $Q$ cycles (time $2\pi Q/\omega'$) the amplitude falls by $e^{-\pi\omega_0/\omega'} \approx e^{-\pi} \approx 1/23$: a high-$Q$ oscillator rings for about $Q$ cycles. Tuning fork, $Q \approx 10^3$; atomic clock, $Q \approx 10^{11}$; silly putty, $Q \sim 0.01$.
> - **Loss per cycle.** In one period the energy falls by the factor $e^{-\gamma T'} \approx 1 - 2\pi/Q$: a fraction $2\pi/Q$ is lost per cycle.
> - **Resonance width.** The same $Q$ fixes how sharp the resonance of the driven oscillator is ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-3|Theorem §A1.6.3]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1*

^rem-a1-5-3

> [!remark] Remark: The electrical twin
> A series RLC circuit is a damped oscillator with $q$ for $x$, $L$ for $m$, $R$ for $b$ and $1/C$ for $k$; its three regimes are those of [[§A10.6 RLC Circuits#^thm-a10-6-2|EM Theorem §A10.6.2]], with critical resistance $R = 2\sqrt{L/C}$. Electromagnetism writes $\gamma = R/2L$ for the *amplitude* decay rate, which corresponds to $b/2m$ here, half of the energy decay rate $\gamma = b/m$ used above and in PHY 287. The quality factor is the same quantity in both: $Q = \omega_0L/R = m\omega_0/b$.

^rem-a1-5-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.5.1: Damping from the Frequency Shift
> A mass $m = 0.20\ \mathrm{kg}$ on a spring with $k = 80\ \mathrm{N/m}$ has $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} = 20\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. With linear damping its oscillation frequency is observed to be $\omega' = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\omega_0 = 17.3\ \mathrm{rad/s}$.
> - *Damping.* From $\omega'^2 = \omega_0^2 - (b/2m)^2$: $(b/2m)^2 = \tfrac14\omega_0^2$, so $b/2m = 10\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $b = 4.0\ \mathrm{kg/s}$ and $\gamma = b/m = 20\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
> - *Quality factor.* $Q = \omega_0/\gamma = 1.0$: strongly damped, though still underdamped ($Q > \tfrac12$).
> - *Doubling the mass* at fixed $k$ and $b$: $Q = \sqrt{mk}/b$ grows by $\sqrt2$ to $1.4$; the heavier oscillator is relatively less damped.
> - *Decay.* One damped period is $T' = 2\pi/\omega' = 0.363\ \mathrm s$, and in that time the amplitude falls by $e^{-(b/2m)T'} = e^{-3.63} = 0.027$; after ten periods the factor is $e^{-36.3} \approx 2\times10^{-16}$. With $Q \approx 1$ there is hardly a second swing to see, in line with "about $Q$ cycles".
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 8*

^ex-a1-5-1

> [!example] Example §A1.5.2: Damping from a Decay Measurement
> A lightly damped oscillator's amplitude falls to half its initial value in $10\ \mathrm s$, during which it makes 25 oscillations. The amplitude is $A_0e^{-bt/2m}$, so
>
> $$
> e^{-(b/2m)(10\ \mathrm s)} = \tfrac12 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{b}{2m} = \frac{\ln 2}{10\ \mathrm s} = 0.069\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}, \qquad \gamma = \frac bm = 0.139\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} .
> $$
>
> The frequency is $f' = 25/(10\ \mathrm s) = 2.5\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $\omega' = 15.7\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. The damping shifts it by a relative amount $\tfrac12(b/2m\omega')^2 \approx 10^{-5}$, so $\omega_0 = 15.7\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ as well, and
>
> $$
> Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\gamma} = \frac{15.7}{0.139} = 113 .
> $$
>
> The energy halves in $5\ \mathrm s$, twice as fast as the amplitude. The resonance of this oscillator when driven is worked out in [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^ex-a1-6-1|Example §A1.6.1]].
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Midterm 1 practice*

^ex-a1-5-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The substitution $x = e^{-\beta t}y$ uses the product rule ([[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]]); that a zero derivative means a constant ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]) gives the critical and overdamped solutions and their uniqueness.
> - The first-order version $m\,dv/dt = -bv$, a body coasting against linear drag, decays as $e^{-bt/m}$ with no oscillation ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed|CM §A3.10]]).
