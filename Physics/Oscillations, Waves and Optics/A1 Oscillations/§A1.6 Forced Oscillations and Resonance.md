A damped oscillator left alone stops ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations|§A1.5]]); to keep it going, something must push it periodically, like a parent pushing a swing. This section drives the damped oscillator with a sinusoidal force. After a transient, the oscillator moves at the *driving* frequency, with an amplitude and phase lag that depend on how close that frequency is to its natural one. Near the natural frequency a small force produces a large response: resonance. Its height and width are set by the same damping that sets the free decay.

## The driven oscillator

> [!definition] Definition §A1.6.1: Driven Oscillator; Transient; Steady State
> A **driven** (or **forced**) oscillator is acted on, besides its restoring and damping forces, by an external periodic **driving force**, here $F(t) = F_0\cos\omega t$. Its **driving angular frequency** $\omega$ is set from outside and is in general different from the natural angular frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^def-a1-5-1|Def. §A1.5.1]]). After the driving starts, the motion is first irregular (the **transient**) and then settles into a periodic motion at the driving frequency (the **steady state**).
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.6*

^def-a1-6-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.6.1: Steady-State Response
> A mass–spring oscillator with linear damping and driving force $F_0\cos\omega t$ obeys
>
> $$
> m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + b\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = F_0\cos\omega t .
> $$
>
> It has the steady-state solution $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t - \delta)$, with amplitude and phase lag
>
> $$
> A = \frac{F_0}{\sqrt{m^2\bigl(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\bigr)^2 + b^2\omega^2}}, \qquad \tan\delta = \frac{b\omega}{m\bigl(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\bigr)}, \quad 0 \le \delta \le \pi .
> $$
>
> Every solution is this steady state plus a solution of the undriven equation (the transient), which dies out ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]; for an underdamped oscillator as $e^{-bt/2m}$). The initial conditions affect only the transient; once it has decayed, the motion is the steady state, whatever the start.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.6, eqs. (15.27)–(15.29) · PHY 287, week 11 slides and Problem Set 9 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2*

^thm-a1-6-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Steady state.* Try $x = C\cos\omega t + D\sin\omega t$. Substituting and collecting the $\cos\omega t$ and $\sin\omega t$ terms (they must match separately for all $t$), with $M = m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)$ and $N = b\omega$:
>
> $$
> MC + ND = F_0, \qquad MD - NC = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C = \frac{F_0M}{M^2 + N^2}, \qquad D = \frac{F_0N}{M^2 + N^2} .
> $$
>
> Writing $C = A\cos\delta$, $D = A\sin\delta$ (so that $x = A\cos(\omega t - \delta)$) gives $A = \sqrt{C^2 + D^2} = F_0/\sqrt{M^2 + N^2}$ and $\tan\delta = D/C = N/M$. Since $D \ge 0$, $\sin\delta \ge 0$ and $\delta$ lies between $0$ and $\pi$.
>
> *General solution.* If $x$ and $x_p$ both solve the driven equation, their difference solves it with $F_0 = 0$, because the equation is linear: the left side of $x - x_p$ is the difference of the left sides. So $x = x_p + x_h$ with $x_h$ a solution of the undriven equation, which decays by [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]].

> [!remark] Remark: Three regimes of driving
> - **Slow driving**, $\omega \ll \omega_0$: $A \approx F_0/k$ and $\delta \approx 0$. The spring has time to follow the force, as if it were applied statically. (The paddle ball follows a slowly moving finger.)
> - **At the natural frequency**, $\omega = \omega_0$: $\delta = \pi/2$, so the displacement lags a quarter period and the *velocity* is in phase with the force. The force then always pushes in the direction of motion, which is how a swing is pushed.
> - **Fast driving**, $\omega \gg \omega_0$: $A \approx F_0/(m\omega^2) \to 0$ and $\delta \to \pi$. Inertia dominates; the mass barely moves, and opposite to the force.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.6 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2*

^rem-a1-6-1

> [!remark] Remark: The complex shortcut
> Replace $F_0\cos\omega t$ by $F_0e^{i\omega t}$ and look for $z = Ze^{i\omega t}$. Each derivative multiplies by $i\omega$, so the differential equation becomes the algebraic one $\bigl(m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) + ib\omega\bigr)Z = F_0$. The modulus of $Z = F_0/\bigl(m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) + ib\omega\bigr)$ is $A$ and its argument is $-\delta$; the real part of $z$ is the steady state. PHY 287 and Schwartz (who uses $e^{-i\omega t}$) derive the response this way.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 11 slides · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2*

^rem-a1-6-2

> [!caution] Caution: Sine or cosine drive
> University Physics drives with $F_0\sin\omega t$ and writes the steady state with a phase $+\phi$. Shifting the origin of time by a quarter period turns one into the other: the amplitude $A$ is the same, only the reference for the phase changes. Here $\delta \ge 0$ is the *lag* of the displacement behind the force.

## Resonance

> [!definition] Definition §A1.6.2: Resonance
> **Resonance** is the large-amplitude response of a driven oscillator when the driving frequency is close to its natural frequency; a small driving force then produces a large oscillation, and the system is said to **resonate**.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.6*

^def-a1-6-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.6.2: Amplitude at and near Resonance
> For an underdamped oscillator with $b^2 < 2mk$, the steady-state amplitude $A(\omega)$ of [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]] is largest at
>
> $$
> \omega_m = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \frac{b^2}{2m^2}}, \qquad\text{where}\qquad A_{\max} = \frac{F_0}{b\,\omega'}, \quad \omega' = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \Bigl(\frac{b}{2m}\Bigr)^2}
> $$
>
> is the frequency of free damped oscillation; if $b^2 \ge 2mk$, $A$ decreases steadily from $A(0) = F_0/k$. For light damping ($Q \gg 1$) the peak is at $\omega_m \approx \omega_0$, with
>
> $$
> A_{\max} \approx A(\omega_0) = \frac{F_0}{b\,\omega_0} = Q\,\frac{F_0}{k} :
> $$
>
> the response at resonance is $Q$ times the static deflection. The smaller the damping, the higher and narrower the peak; without damping it would be infinite.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.6 · PHY 287, week 11 slides and Problem Set 9*

^thm-a1-6-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $A$ is largest where $f(u) = m^2(u - \omega_0^2)^2 + b^2u$, with $u = \omega^2 \ge 0$, is smallest. $f$ is a parabola in $u$ with $f'(u) = 2m^2(u - \omega_0^2) + b^2$, which vanishes at $u_m = \omega_0^2 - b^2/2m^2$. If $u_m > 0$ this is the minimum, and
>
> $$
> f(u_m) = m^2\Bigl(\frac{b^2}{2m^2}\Bigr)^2 + b^2\Bigl(\omega_0^2 - \frac{b^2}{2m^2}\Bigr) = b^2\Bigl(\omega_0^2 - \frac{b^2}{4m^2}\Bigr) = b^2\omega'^2,
> $$
>
> so $A_{\max} = F_0/(b\omega')$. If $u_m \le 0$, $f$ increases for all $u \ge 0$ and $A$ is largest at $\omega = 0$. At $\omega = \omega_0$ the first term of $f$ vanishes and $A = F_0/(b\omega_0)$; with $Q = m\omega_0/b$ ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]) and $k = m\omega_0^2$ this is $QF_0/k$. For $Q \gg 1$, $\omega_m$, $\omega'$ and $\omega_0$ differ only by terms of order $1/Q^2$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §A1.6.3: Absorbed Power and Bandwidth
> In the steady state the driving force delivers, averaged over a period, the power
>
> $$
> \overline P = \tfrac12 F_0\,\omega A\sin\delta = \tfrac12 b\,\omega^2A^2 = \frac{F_0^2}{2m}\,\frac{\gamma\,\omega^2}{\bigl(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\bigr)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2}, \qquad \gamma = \frac bm ,
> $$
>
> all of which the damping force turns into heat. $\overline P$ is largest exactly at $\omega = \omega_0$, where $\overline P_{\max} = F_0^2/2b$. It is half of that at
>
> $$
> \omega_\pm = \tfrac12\Bigl(\sqrt{\gamma^2 + 4\omega_0^2} \pm \gamma\Bigr),
> $$
>
> so the full width of the power peak at half its maximum, the **bandwidth**, is exactly $\Delta\omega = \omega_+ - \omega_- = \gamma = b/m$, and
>
> $$
> Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\Delta\omega} .
> $$
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 11 slides and Problem Set 10 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2 · PHY 300, Midterm 1 practice (formula sheet)*

^thm-a1-6-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The power of the driving force is $P = Fv$ ([[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]]) with $v = -\omega A\sin(\omega t - \delta) = -\omega A(\sin\omega t\cos\delta - \cos\omega t\sin\delta)$:
>
> $$
> P = F_0\omega A\bigl(\cos^2\omega t\,\sin\delta - \sin\omega t\cos\omega t\,\cos\delta\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Over a period $\overline{\cos^2\omega t} = \tfrac12$ and $\overline{\sin\omega t\cos\omega t} = \overline{\tfrac12\sin 2\omega t} = 0$, so $\overline P = \tfrac12F_0\omega A\sin\delta$. From the derivation of [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]], $A\sin\delta = D = F_0b\omega/(M^2 + N^2) = b\omega A^2/F_0$, hence $\overline P = \tfrac12b\omega^2A^2$, which is also the average of the dissipated power $bv^2$ ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]). Inserting $A$ and dividing numerator and denominator by $m^2$ gives the third form. Divide its numerator and denominator by $\gamma^2\omega^2$:
>
> $$
> \overline P = \frac{F_0^2}{2b}\,\frac{1}{1 + \bigl(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2\bigr)^2/(\gamma\omega)^2} ,
> $$
>
> largest, $F_0^2/2b$, exactly when $\omega = \omega_0$, and half of that when $(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 = \gamma^2\omega^2$, i.e. $\omega^2 \pm \gamma\omega - \omega_0^2 = 0$. The positive roots are $\omega_\pm$, whose difference is $\gamma$.

^der-a1-6-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]], [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]], [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]], [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Which resonance frequency, and which Q?
> - The amplitude peaks at $\omega_m$, slightly *below* $\omega_0$; the absorbed power, and the velocity amplitude $\omega A$, peak *exactly* at $\omega_0$; the free oscillation runs at $\omega'$. For light damping all three agree to order $1/Q^2$, which is why University Physics simply says that the amplitude is largest at $\omega_0$, with $A_{\max} = F_0/(b\omega_0)$.
> - The quality factor is $Q = \omega_0/\Delta\omega = m\omega_0/b$, large for a sharp resonance. University Physics §15.6 states the ratio the other way up ("spread at half the amplitude divided by $\omega_0$"); use $\omega_0/\Delta\omega$, as in PHY 287, Schwartz and the circuit version ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^def-a11-4-1|EM Def. §A11.4.1]]). The width is measured on the *power* curve; the amplitude curve is wider at half its height.

^cau-a1-6-2

> [!remark] Remark: A sharp resonance rings long
> The bandwidth $\Delta\omega = b/m$ of the driven oscillator equals the energy decay rate $\gamma$ of the free one ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]). A narrow resonance and a long ringing are one property: an oscillator that responds only to a narrow band of frequencies takes about $Q$ cycles to start up and to die down. A radio tuned to one station needs a high-$Q$ circuit for this reason, and measuring either the free decay or the resonance curve gives the same $\gamma$.

^rem-a1-6-3

> [!remark] Remark: The electrical twin
> A series RLC circuit driven by an emf $V_0\sin\omega t$ is this oscillator with $L \leftrightarrow m$, $R \leftrightarrow b$, $1/C \leftrightarrow k$, $V_0 \leftrightarrow F_0$ ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^rem-a11-4-1|EM §A11.4]]). The current $i = dq/dt$ corresponds to the velocity, which is why the current amplitude peaks exactly at $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-4-2|EM Theorem §A11.4.2]]), and the circuit's bandwidth $R/L$ ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-4-3|EM Theorem §A11.4.3]]) is the mechanical $b/m$.

^rem-a1-6-4

> [!remark] Remark: Driving without damping, and beats
> With $b = 0$ and the oscillator starting from rest, the transient does not decay. The full solution is
>
> $$
> x(t) = \frac{F_0/m}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}\bigl(\cos\omega t - \cos\omega_0 t\bigr) = \frac{2F_0/m}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}\,\sin\frac{(\omega_0 + \omega)t}{2}\,\sin\frac{(\omega_0 - \omega)t}{2} .
> $$
>
> Near resonance this is a fast oscillation at the mean frequency inside a slow envelope that swells and fades with period $2\pi/|\omega_0 - \omega|$: beats ([[§A3.4 Beats|§A3.4]]). Exactly at resonance the limit $\omega \to \omega_0$ (L'Hôpital's rule) gives $x = \dfrac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}\,t\sin\omega_0t$: the amplitude grows in proportion to time, with each push adding to the swing, until damping or breakage intervenes.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 11 slides and Problem Set 9*

^rem-a1-6-5

> [!remark] Remark: Resonance in the world
> A note sung at a piano with the dampers lifted sets the matching strings ringing; a parent pushes a swing gently at its natural frequency rather than with one hard shove; MRI makes hydrogen nuclei resonate with radio waves near $100\ \mathrm{MHz}$. Resonance can also destroy: in 1940 moderate winds drove the Tacoma Narrows bridge at a resonant frequency, its damping dropped when support cables slipped, and it failed; London's Millennium Bridge was closed and modified for the same reason. Tall buildings carry *tuned* dampers, an oscillator with the building's own natural frequency that takes up and dissipates the energy of the sway.
>
> *Source: UP1 §15.6 and chapter introduction*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A1.6.1: The Resonance Curve from a Free Decay
> The oscillator of [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^ex-a1-5-2|Example §A1.5.2]] (amplitude halves in 10 s during 25 oscillations) is driven by a sinusoidal force. Sketch the absorbed power against the driving frequency.
>
> From the free decay, $\gamma = b/m = 0.139\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\omega_0 = 15.7\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ ($f_0 = 2.50\ \mathrm{Hz}$). By [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-3|Theorem §A1.6.3]] the power curve is a single peak centred on $\omega_0 = 15.7\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, of full width at half maximum
>
> $$
> \Delta\omega = \gamma = 0.139\ \mathrm{rad/s}, \qquad \Delta f = \frac{\gamma}{2\pi} = 0.022\ \mathrm{Hz},
> $$
>
> nearly symmetric because $\Delta\omega \ll \omega_0$, with no modulations; its height is $F_0^2/2b$. The quality factor $Q = \omega_0/\Delta\omega = 113$ agrees with the decay measurement, and on resonance the amplitude is $113$ times the static deflection $F_0/k$. To map the peak, the driving frequency must be stepped in increments well below $0.02$ Hz.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Midterm 1 practice*

^ex-a1-6-1

> [!example] Example §A1.6.2: Recognising the Motion from the Equation
> Five oscillators, with $t$ in seconds:
>
> $$
> \text{(1)}\ \ddot y + 16y = 0, \quad \text{(2)}\ \ddot y + 4\dot y + 16y = 0, \quad \text{(3)}\ \ddot y + 5\dot y + 5y = 5\cos 2t, \quad \text{(4)}\ \ddot y + 9y = 0, \quad \text{(5)}\ \ddot y + 11y = \cos 3t .
> $$
>
> Read $\gamma = b/m$ off the $\dot y$ term and $\omega_0^2$ off the $y$ term ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]).
> - (1) and (4): undamped and undriven, so pure SHM ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]) with $\omega_0 = 4$ and $3\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, periods $1.57\ \mathrm s$ and $2.09\ \mathrm s$.
> - (2): $\omega_0 = 4\ \mathrm{rad/s}$, $\gamma = 4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $Q = 1$. Since $\gamma/2 = 2 < \omega_0$, it is underdamped: it oscillates at $\omega' = \sqrt{16 - 4} = 3.46\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ inside the envelope $e^{-2t}$ and has died out after about 2 s.
> - (3): $\omega_0 = \sqrt5 = 2.24\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ and $\gamma/2 = 2.5 > \omega_0$, so the transient is overdamped, two exponentials with rates $2.5 \pm \sqrt{6.25 - 5} = 3.62$ and $1.38\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. What remains is the steady state at the driving frequency $2\ \mathrm{rad/s}$ (period $\pi$ s): $A = 5/\sqrt{(5 - 4)^2 + (5\cdot2)^2} = 0.50$ and $\tan\delta = 10/1$, $\delta = 84^\circ$ ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]]). A graph that wobbles irregularly at first and then settles into a steady oscillation is this one.
> - (5): undamped, driven at $\omega = 3$ near $\omega_0 = \sqrt{11} = 3.32\ \mathrm{rad/s}$. Started from rest it beats forever ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^rem-a1-6-5|remark above]]): $y = \tfrac12(\cos 3t - \cos 3.32t) = \sin(3.16\,t)\,\sin(0.158\,t)$, with the envelope returning every $2\pi/(\sqrt{11} - 3) = 19.8\ \mathrm s$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Final practice*

^ex-a1-6-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The exact-resonance limit of the undamped response is a $0/0$ limit, evaluated by L'Hôpital's rule ([[§30 L'Hospital's Rule#^thm-30-1|451 Thm. §30.1]]); the location of the amplitude peak is a one-variable minimisation ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-1|451 Thm. §29.1]]).
> - A periodic but non-sinusoidal driving force is a sum of sinusoids (Fourier series); by linearity the response is the sum of the responses to each: level B (planned).
