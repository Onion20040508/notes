How sharp is a resonance, where exactly is its peak, and how fast does the phase turn over? Level A located the peak of the displacement amplitude ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-2|Theorem §A1.6.2]]), computed the absorbed power and showed that its full width at half maximum is exactly $\gamma$ ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-3|Theorem §A1.6.3]]), and warned that several "resonance frequencies" coincide only for light damping ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^cau-a1-6-2|Caution: Which resonance frequency, and which Q?]]). Working with the response function $\chi(\omega)$ of [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^def-b1-3-1|Def. §B1.3.1]], this section makes those statements exact: it orders the frequencies and their thresholds, gives the exact width of the displacement peak and the exact phase at the half-power points, splits the response into an elastic and an absorptive part to show which one takes power, and proves $Q = 2\pi \times$ (energy stored)/(energy lost per cycle) exactly for steady driving at resonance. A closing table sorts the $Q$ relations into exact and approximate.

## The resonance peak

> [!definition] Definition §B1.4.1: Lorentzian; Full Width at Half Maximum
> The **Lorentzian** of centre $\omega_0$ and width $\Gamma$ is
>
> $$
> \mathcal L(\omega) = \frac{(\Gamma/2)^2}{(\omega - \omega_0)^2 + (\Gamma/2)^2} .
> $$
>
> It equals $1$ at $\omega_0$ and $\tfrac12$ at $\omega_0 \pm \Gamma/2$. The **full width at half maximum** (FWHM) of a peaked curve is the distance between the two points where it falls to half its maximum; for $\mathcal L$ it is $\Gamma$.
>
> *Source: Series I §5.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2 §2.3 · PHY 300, Worksheet 2*

^def-b1-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.4.1: The Resonance Frequencies
> For the steady state $x = A\cos(\omega t - \delta)$ of [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], with $Q = \omega_0/\gamma$:
> 1. The **displacement** amplitude $A(\omega)$ is largest at $\omega_m = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/2} = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - 1/2Q^2}$ if $Q > 1/\sqrt2$, with $A_{\max} = f_0/(\gamma\omega_d)$, i.e. $A_{\max}/(F_0/k) = Q/\sqrt{1 - 1/4Q^2}$; if $Q \le 1/\sqrt2$ it decreases steadily from $F_0/k$.
> 2. The **velocity** amplitude $V(\omega) = \omega A(\omega)$ is largest exactly at $\omega_0$, for every $\gamma > 0$, with $V_{\max} = f_0/\gamma = F_0/b$.
> 3. The **acceleration** amplitude $\omega^2A(\omega)$ is largest at $\omega_a = \omega_0^2/\omega_m$, above $\omega_0$ (for $Q > 1/\sqrt2$).
> 4. The free oscillation runs at $\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$ (for $Q > \tfrac12$).
>
> Exactly, $\omega_m^2 = \omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/2 < \omega_d^2 = \omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4 < \omega_0^2 < \omega_a^2$; for $Q \gg 1$, $\omega_m \approx \omega_0(1 - 1/4Q^2)$ and $\omega_d \approx \omega_0(1 - 1/8Q^2)$, differences of order $1/Q^2$. For $\tfrac12 < Q \le 1/\sqrt2$ the free motion oscillates but the driven displacement has no peak.
>
> This extends [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-2|Theorem §A1.6.2]] and makes exact the comparison in [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^cau-a1-6-2|Caution: Which resonance frequency, and which Q?]].
>
> *Source: Series I §5.1, §5.2, §5.5 · PHY 287, week 11 slides and Problem Set 9 (Problem 2)*

^thm-b1-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) As in [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^der-a1-6-2|the derivation of Theorem §A1.6.2]]: with $u = \omega^2$, $A = f_0/\sqrt{g(u)}$ and, completing the square,
>
> $$
> g(u) = (\omega_0^2 - u)^2 + \gamma^2u = \bigl(u - \omega_m^2\bigr)^2 + \gamma^2\omega_d^2, \qquad \omega_m^2 = \omega_0^2 - \tfrac{\gamma^2}{2} .
> $$
>
> If $\omega_m^2 > 0$ the minimum over $u \ge 0$ is $\gamma^2\omega_d^2$ at $u = \omega_m^2$; otherwise $g$ increases on $u \ge 0$. Then $A_{\max}k/F_0 = \omega_0^2/(\gamma\omega_d) = Q\,\omega_0/\omega_d$. (2) Dividing inside the root by $\omega^2$, $V = f_0\big/\sqrt{(\omega_0^2/\omega - \omega)^2 + \gamma^2}$, largest where the square vanishes, at $\omega = \omega_0$. (3) With $v = 1/\omega^2$, $\omega^2A = f_0\big/\sqrt{(\omega_0^2v - 1)^2 + \gamma^2v}$; the radicand has derivative $2\omega_0^2(\omega_0^2v - 1) + \gamma^2$, zero at $v = \omega_m^2/\omega_0^4$. The expansions use $\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon} \approx 1 - \varepsilon/2$ with $\gamma^2/\omega_0^2 = 1/Q^2$.

^der-b1-4-1

*Uses:* [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-2|Theorem §A1.6.2]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^thm-29-1|451 Thm. §29.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.4.2: Width of the Displacement Resonance
> For $Q > 1/\sqrt2$ the squared amplitude $A^2$ falls to half its maximum exactly where
>
> $$
> \omega^2 = \omega_m^2 \pm \gamma\omega_d ,
> $$
>
> so its width measured in $\omega^2$ is exactly $2\gamma\omega_d$ (the lower point exists only if $\omega_m^2 > \gamma\omega_d$). For $Q \gg 1$, near $\omega_0$,
>
> $$
> A^2(\omega) \approx \frac{f_0^2}{4\omega_0^2}\,\frac{1}{(\omega - \omega_0)^2 + \gamma^2/4} = \Bigl(\frac{QF_0}{k}\Bigr)^2\mathcal L(\omega), \qquad \Gamma = \gamma ,
> $$
>
> a Lorentzian of FWHM $\gamma$, so that $Q \approx \omega_0/\Delta\omega$ for this curve too. The amplitude $A$ itself, at half its height, is wider: about $\sqrt3\,\gamma$.
>
> *Source: Series I §5.3*

^thm-b1-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From the completed square in the previous derivation, $A^2 = f_0^2/g$ is half its maximum where $g(u) = 2\gamma^2\omega_d^2$, i.e. $(u - \omega_m^2)^2 = \gamma^2\omega_d^2$. Near $\omega_0$ keep the small difference $\omega - \omega_0$ and set $\omega = \omega_0$ elsewhere: $\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 = (\omega_0 - \omega)(\omega_0 + \omega) \approx 2\omega_0(\omega_0 - \omega)$ and $\gamma^2\omega^2 \approx \gamma^2\omega_0^2$, which gives the Lorentzian, with $f_0/(\gamma\omega_0) = QF_0/k$. In that approximation $A = \tfrac12A_{\max}$ where $(\omega - \omega_0)^2 + \gamma^2/4 = 4\cdot\gamma^2/4$, i.e. $\omega - \omega_0 = \pm\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\gamma$.

## The phase across resonance

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.4.3: Phase Across Resonance
> The phase lag of [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]] is, exactly,
>
> $$
> \delta(\omega) = \frac\pi2 + \arctan\frac{\omega^2 - \omega_0^2}{\gamma\omega} \in (0, \pi) .
> $$
>
> 1. $\delta(\omega_0) = \pi/2$ for every $\gamma$: at the natural frequency the displacement lags by a quarter period and the velocity is in phase with the force.
> 2. At the half-power frequencies $\omega_\mp = \tfrac12\bigl(\sqrt{\gamma^2 + 4\omega_0^2} \mp \gamma\bigr)$ of [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-3|Theorem §A1.6.3]], $\delta = \pi/4$ and $3\pi/4$ exactly.
> 3. The slope at $\omega_0$ is $d\delta/d\omega = 2/\gamma = 2Q/\omega_0$ exactly.
> 4. Near $\omega_0$, $\delta \approx \pi/2 + \arctan\bigl(2(\omega - \omega_0)/\gamma\bigr)$: the phase turns from $0$ to $\pi$ over a band of width about $\gamma$, sharpening into the step of the undamped oscillator ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]]) as $\gamma \to 0$.
>
> The velocity $\dot x = \omega A\cos(\omega t - \delta + \pi/2)$ leads the force by $\pi/2 - \delta$, which runs from $+\pi/2$ through $0$ at $\omega_0$ to $-\pi/2$.
>
> *Source: Series I §5.4 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2 §2.2*

^thm-b1-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From $\cos\delta \propto \omega_0^2 - \omega^2$ and $\sin\delta \propto \gamma\omega > 0$, $\cot\delta = (\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)/(\gamma\omega)$. Since $\cot\delta = -\tan(\delta - \pi/2)$ and $\delta - \pi/2 \in (-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, $\delta - \pi/2 = \arctan\bigl((\omega^2 - \omega_0^2)/\gamma\omega\bigr)$. (1): the argument vanishes. (2): at the half-power points $(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 = \gamma^2\omega^2$, so $\cot\delta = \pm1$, with $+1$ below $\omega_0$. (3): $\tfrac{d}{d\omega}\tfrac{\omega^2 - \omega_0^2}{\gamma\omega} = \tfrac{\omega^2 + \omega_0^2}{\gamma\omega^2}$, which is $2/\gamma$ at $\omega_0$, and $\arctan'(0) = 1$. (4): near $\omega_0$, $(\omega^2 - \omega_0^2)/\gamma\omega \approx 2(\omega - \omega_0)/\gamma$. Finally $-\sin(\omega t - \delta) = \cos(\omega t - \delta + \pi/2)$.

^der-b1-4-3

## Power: the absorptive part

> [!definition] Definition §B1.4.2: Elastic and Absorptive Amplitudes
> Split the response function into real and imaginary parts, $\chi(\omega) = \chi'(\omega) - i\chi''(\omega)$ with
>
> $$
> \chi' = \frac{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2}, \qquad \chi'' = \frac{\gamma\omega}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2} \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> Then the steady state is $x = f_0\bigl[\chi'\cos\omega t + \chi''\sin\omega t\bigr]$: the **elastic amplitude** $f_0\chi'$ is the part in phase (or in antiphase) with the force, the **absorptive amplitude** $f_0\chi''$ the part a quarter period behind it.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2 §2.3 (his $A$ and $B$)*

^def-b1-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.4.4: Absorbed Power
> In the steady state the driving force delivers the instantaneous power
>
> $$
> P(t) = F(t)\,\dot x(t) = F_0f_0\,\omega\Bigl[\chi''\cos^2\omega t - \tfrac12\chi'\sin2\omega t\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> The elastic part averages to zero (energy lent to the oscillator and returned within each cycle); only the absorptive part absorbs power on average:
>
> $$
> \langle P\rangle = \tfrac12F_0f_0\,\omega\chi'' = \tfrac12b\,\omega^2A^2 = \frac{F_0^2}{2b}\,\frac{1}{1 + Q^2\bigl(\omega/\omega_0 - \omega_0/\omega\bigr)^2} .
> $$
>
> 1. $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12bV^2$ is largest exactly at $\omega_0$, together with the velocity amplitude, with $\langle P\rangle_{\max} = F_0^2/2b$.
> 2. $\langle P\rangle$ is unchanged under $\omega \to \omega_0^2/\omega$: the curve is symmetric on a logarithmic frequency axis, not a linear one. The half-power points satisfy exactly $\omega_+ - \omega_- = \gamma$ and $\omega_+\omega_- = \omega_0^2$: $\omega_0$ is their geometric, not arithmetic, mean.
> 3. For $Q \gg 1$, near $\omega_0$, $\langle P\rangle \approx \langle P\rangle_{\max}\,\mathcal L(\omega)$ with $\Gamma = \gamma$; the relative error is of order $\lvert\omega - \omega_0\rvert/\omega_0$.
> 4. As a function on the whole real $\omega$ axis $\langle P\rangle$ is even, with peaks of width $\gamma$ at $\pm\omega_0$.
>
> This extends [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-3|Theorem §A1.6.3]], which found $\langle P\rangle$ and the bandwidth $\gamma$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2 §2.3 · Series I §5.5 · PHY 287, week 11 slides and Problem Set 10 (Problem 1) · PHY 300, Worksheet 3*

^thm-b1-4-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> From $x = f_0(\chi'\cos\omega t + \chi''\sin\omega t)$, $\dot x = f_0\omega(-\chi'\sin\omega t + \chi''\cos\omega t)$; multiply by $F_0\cos\omega t$ and use $\sin\omega t\cos\omega t = \tfrac12\sin2\omega t$. Over a period $\langle\cos^2\omega t\rangle = \tfrac12$ and $\langle\sin2\omega t\rangle = 0$, so $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12F_0f_0\omega\chi''$. (Equivalently, by [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|Theorem §B1.1.6]] with $\hat F = F_0$ and $\hat v = i\omega f_0\chi$: $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\hat F\hat v^*) = \tfrac12F_0f_0\omega\operatorname{Re}(-i\chi^*) = \tfrac12F_0f_0\omega\chi''$.) Since $\chi'' = \gamma\omega A^2/f_0^2$ and $F_0\gamma = bf_0$, this is $\tfrac12b\omega^2A^2$. Dividing numerator and denominator of $\tfrac{F_0^2}{2m}\tfrac{\gamma\omega^2}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2}$ by $\gamma^2\omega^2$, and using $(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)/(\gamma\omega) = Q(\omega_0/\omega - \omega/\omega_0)$, gives the last form.
>
> (1): the square vanishes only at $\omega = \omega_0$. (2): $\omega \to \omega_0^2/\omega$ swaps $\omega/\omega_0$ and $\omega_0/\omega$. Half power means $Q(\omega/\omega_0 - \omega_0/\omega) = \pm1$, i.e. $\omega^2 \mp \gamma\omega - \omega_0^2 = 0$; the positive roots are $\omega_\pm$, with difference $\gamma$ and, since the transformation maps one to the other, product $\omega_0^2$. (3): $\omega/\omega_0 - \omega_0/\omega = (\omega - \omega_0)(\omega + \omega_0)/(\omega\omega_0) = \tfrac{2(\omega - \omega_0)}{\omega_0}\bigl(1 + O\bigl(\tfrac{\omega - \omega_0}{\omega_0}\bigr)\bigr)$, so $Q^2(\cdots)^2 \approx (\omega - \omega_0)^2/(\gamma/2)^2$. (4): $\langle P\rangle$ depends on $\omega$ only through $\omega^2$.

> [!remark] Remark: Mechanical impedance and the electrical twin
> The velocity response is $\hat v = \hat F/Z_m$ with $Z_m = m\,p(i\omega)/(i\omega) = b + i(m\omega - k/\omega)$, the oscillator's mechanical impedance (force over velocity; the wave version is the subject of [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission|§B5.2]]). It is real, $Z_m = b$, exactly at $\omega_0$: velocity resonance. In the series RLC circuit ($L \leftrightarrow m$, $R \leftrightarrow b$, $1/C \leftrightarrow k$) the current plays the velocity, $Z = R + i(\omega L - 1/\omega C)$ is the impedance of [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC#^def-a11-2-2|EM Def. §A11.2.2]], the current peaks exactly at $\omega_0$ ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-4-2|EM Theorem §A11.4.2]]), and the half-power frequencies obey $\omega_+\omega_- = \omega_0^2$ ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-4-3|EM Theorem §A11.4.3]]), item for item [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]].

## Stored energy and Q

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.4.5: Stored Energy and Q
> In the steady state the mechanical energy averaged over a period is
>
> $$
> \langle E\rangle = \tfrac14m\bigl(\omega^2 + \omega_0^2\bigr)A^2 ,
> $$
>
> and at $\omega = \omega_0$ the energy is constant in time, $E = \tfrac12kA^2$. The ratio of stored energy to energy dissipated per driving period $T = 2\pi/\omega$ is
>
> $$
> 2\pi\,\frac{\langle E\rangle}{\langle P\rangle T} = \frac Q2\Bigl(\frac{\omega}{\omega_0} + \frac{\omega_0}{\omega}\Bigr) \ \ge\ Q ,
> $$
>
> with equality exactly at $\omega = \omega_0$. So
>
> $$
> Q = 2\pi\,\frac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy dissipated per cycle}}
> $$
>
> holds exactly for steady driving at resonance; for a freely decaying oscillator it holds to leading order in $1/Q$ ([[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]).
>
> *Source: Series I §5.3 · PHY 300, Midterm 1 revised practice (Problem 5)*

^thm-b1-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $x = A\cos(\omega t - \delta)$, $\tfrac12m\dot x^2 = \tfrac12m\omega^2A^2\sin^2(\omega t - \delta)$ and $\tfrac12kx^2 = \tfrac12m\omega_0^2A^2\cos^2(\omega t - \delta)$; each square averages to $\tfrac12$. At $\omega = \omega_0$ the two coefficients are equal and $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$. The energy dissipated per period is $\langle P\rangle T = \tfrac12b\omega^2A^2\cdot2\pi/\omega = \pi b\omega A^2$ ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]]), so
>
> $$
> 2\pi\frac{\langle E\rangle}{\langle P\rangle T} = \frac{m(\omega^2 + \omega_0^2)}{2b\omega} = \frac{m\omega_0}{b}\,\frac{\omega^2 + \omega_0^2}{2\omega\omega_0} = \frac Q2\Bigl(\frac\omega{\omega_0} + \frac{\omega_0}\omega\Bigr),
> $$
>
> and $y + 1/y \ge 2$ with equality only at $y = 1$.

> [!remark] Remark: Which Q relations are exact
> With $Q = \omega_0/\gamma = m\omega_0/b$ as the definition ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]):
>
> | Relation | Status | Where |
> |---|---|---|
> | $\omega_0/\Delta\omega$, with $\Delta\omega$ the FWHM of $\langle P\rangle$, equals $Q$ | exact | [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-3\|Theorem §A1.6.3]] |
> | $A(\omega_0) = Q\,F_0/k$ | exact | [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-2\|Theorem §A1.6.2]] |
> | $A_{\max} = Q\,F_0/k$ | approximate; exactly $Q/\sqrt{1 - 1/4Q^2}$ times $F_0/k$ | [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-1\|Theorem §B1.4.1]] |
> | slope of the phase at $\omega_0$ is $2Q/\omega_0$ | exact | [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-3\|Theorem §B1.4.3]] |
> | $Q = 2\pi E/\Delta E_{\text{cycle}}$, steady driving at $\omega_0$ | exact | [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-5\|Theorem §B1.4.5]] |
> | free decay loses the fraction $2\pi/Q$ of its energy per period | approximate; exactly $1 - e^{-2\Lambda}$ | [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-5\|Theorem §B1.2.5]] |
> | logarithmic decrement $\Lambda = \pi/Q$ | approximate; exactly $\pi/\sqrt{Q^2 - 1/4}$ | [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-4\|Theorem §B1.2.4]] |
> | FWHM of $A^2$ equals $\gamma$ | approximate; exact width $2\gamma\omega_d$ in $\omega^2$ | [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-2\|Theorem §B1.4.2]] |
> | amplitude falls by $e^{-\pi}$ in $Q$ cycles | approximate | [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^rem-a1-5-3\|Remark: Reading Q]] |
>
> The approximate ones differ from the exact ones by relative amounts of order $1/Q^2$ (or $1/Q$ for the energy lost per period), which is why they can be used interchangeably for a tuning fork but not for a shock absorber ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^ex-b1-4-1|Example §B1.4.1]]).

^rem-b1-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Narrow in frequency, long in time
> The same $\gamma$ is the width of the power resonance and the energy decay rate of the free oscillator ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^rem-a1-6-3|Remark: A sharp resonance rings long]]), and it sets the start-up time $\sim 2/\gamma$ of driven motion ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^ex-b1-3-1|Example §B1.3.1]]). A Lorentzian line in frequency and an exponentially decaying oscillation in time are two descriptions of the same oscillator; the Fourier transform makes the correspondence exact ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function|§B4.4]]).
>
> *Source: Series I §5.3 · PHY 300, Worksheet 2*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.4.1: A Low-Q Resonance, Exact versus Approximate
> Take $Q = 2$ ($\gamma = \omega_0/2$), a heavily damped oscillator. In units of $\omega_0$ and $F_0/k$:
> - *Frequencies* ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-1|Theorem §B1.4.1]]): displacement peak $\omega_m = \sqrt{1 - 1/8} = 0.935$, free oscillation $\omega_d = \sqrt{1 - 1/16} = 0.968$, velocity and power peak $1$, acceleration peak $1/0.935 = 1.069$. The peak displacement is $2/\sqrt{15/16} = 2.07$, against $A(\omega_0) = 2$.
> - *Power width* ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]]): $\omega_\pm = \tfrac12(\sqrt{4.25} \pm 0.5) = 1.281$ and $0.781$. Their difference is $0.500 = \gamma$ exactly and their product $1.000$; their midpoint is $1.031$, not $1$. The Lorentzian would put them at $0.75$ and $1.25$ (figure above). At these frequencies $\delta = 45^\circ$ and $135^\circ$ exactly.
> - *Displacement width* ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-2|Theorem §B1.4.2]]): $A^2$ is half its maximum at $\omega^2 = 0.875 \pm 0.484$, i.e. $\omega = 0.625$ and $1.166$, a width $0.541$ rather than $\gamma = 0.5$.
> - *Free decay* ([[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]], [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-5|Theorem §B1.2.5]]): $\Lambda = \pi/\sqrt{3.75} = 1.62$ (not $\pi/2 = 1.57$); successive maxima shrink by $e^{-1.62} = 0.20$, and $96\ \%$ of the energy is lost in one period, where "$2\pi/Q$" would claim $314\ \%$.
> - *Driven at $\omega_0$*, the ratio $2\pi E/\Delta E_{\text{cycle}}$ is exactly $2$ ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-5|Theorem §B1.4.5]]).
>
> A lab that measures $\gamma$ both from a resonance scan and from a free decay, and fits the scan with a Lorentzian, must expect such discrepancies at low $Q$: a symmetric curve drawn through the half-power points is centred at $\tfrac12(\omega_+ + \omega_-) = \omega_0\sqrt{1 + 1/4Q^2} = 1.031\,\omega_0$, and the wings do not match. For $Q$ in the hundreds the same comparisons agree to parts in $10^5$.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 2 (Lorentzian fit versus free decay) · Series I §5*

> [!example] Example §B1.4.2: Resonant Driving Without Damping
> What power does an undamped oscillator absorb on resonance? Formally $\langle P\rangle_{\max} = F_0^2/2b \to \infty$ as $b \to 0$, but then there is no steady state. Started from rest it moves as $x = \dfrac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}\,t\sin\omega_0t$ ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]]), with $\dot x = \dfrac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}(\sin\omega_0t + \omega_0t\cos\omega_0t)$. Its energy is
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac12m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12m\omega_0^2x^2 = \frac{F_0^2}{8m}\Bigl[t^2 + \frac{t}{\omega_0}\sin2\omega_0t + \frac{\sin^2\omega_0t}{\omega_0^2}\Bigr] \approx \frac{F_0^2t^2}{8m},
> $$
>
> and the power $P = F_0\cos\omega_0t\,\dot x = \dfrac{F_0^2}{2m}\Bigl[t\cos^2\omega_0t + \dfrac{\sin\omega_0t\cos\omega_0t}{\omega_0}\Bigr]$ averages over a cycle to $F_0^2t/4m$. The energy grows quadratically and the absorbed power linearly in time, without limit: the answer to "how much power on resonance without damping" is "more and more". With damping the same linear rise $F_0^2t/4m$ reaches the steady value $F_0^2/2b$ at $t = 2m/b = 2/\gamma$, the start-up time of [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^ex-b1-3-1|Example §B1.3.1]]. (Off resonance an undamped oscillator absorbs nothing on average in the steady state: $\chi'' = 0$ when $\gamma = 0$.)
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 3 · PHY 287, Problem Set 10 (Problem 1)*
