Level A found the steady-state response of the driven damped oscillator by real undetermined coefficients ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]]), stated that every solution is that steady state plus a decaying transient, and showed beats and the linear growth at exact resonance for the undamped oscillator started from rest ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^rem-a1-6-5|Remark: Driving without damping, and beats]]). This section answers the question "given the drive and the initial state, what is the motion?" completely: the steady state from the complex response function of [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method|§B1.1]], for one or several driving frequencies; the initial-value problem with the transient written out; and the undamped oscillator, including exact resonance, as a theorem about all solutions, with the mechanism of secular growth and the way damping cuts it off.

## Steady state

> [!definition] Definition §B1.3.1: Complex Response Function
> For the driven oscillator $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = f_0\cos\omega t$, with $f_0 = F_0/m$, the **complex response function** (transfer function) is
>
> $$
> \chi(\omega) = \frac{1}{p(i\omega)} = \frac{1}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + i\gamma\omega} ,
> $$
>
> where $p$ is the characteristic polynomial ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]). It is the complex amplitude ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]]) of the response to a unit drive $e^{i\omega t}$. Its polar form $\chi = \lvert\chi\rvert e^{-i\delta}$ defines the **phase lag** $\delta(\omega)$. The series writes $\hat D(\omega) = p(i\omega) = 1/\chi$.
>
> *Source: Series I §4.2 · PHY 287, week 11 slides · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2 §2*

^def-b1-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.1: Steady State by Complexification
> Let $\gamma > 0$ and $\omega \ge 0$, or $\gamma = 0$ and $\omega \ne \omega_0$. Then $p(i\omega) \ne 0$ and
>
> $$
> x_p(t) = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[f_0\,\chi(\omega)\,e^{i\omega t}\bigr] = A\cos(\omega t - \delta), \qquad A = \frac{f_0}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2}}, \qquad \cos\delta = \frac{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)A}{f_0}, \quad \sin\delta = \frac{\gamma\omega A}{f_0},
> $$
>
> is a solution, with $0 \le \delta < \pi$: $\delta = \pi/2$ at $\omega_0$, and $\delta$ is $\arctan\bigl(\gamma\omega/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)\bigr)$ below resonance and that value plus $\pi$ above it. For $\gamma = 0$ the response $x_p = f_0\cos\omega t/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)$ is in phase below $\omega_0$ and in antiphase ($\delta = \pi$) above. For $\gamma > 0$ it is the only solution that is periodic with the period of the drive. A drive that is a finite sum of sinusoids, $F(t) = \sum_j F_j\cos(\omega_jt + \theta_j)$, has the steady state
>
> $$
> x_p(t) = \sum_j \frac{F_j}{m}\,\bigl\lvert\chi(\omega_j)\bigr\rvert\cos\bigl(\omega_jt + \theta_j - \delta(\omega_j)\bigr) .
> $$
>
> This rederives [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]] by the complex method (with the explicit branch of $\delta$ above and below $\omega_0$), characterises the steady state as the only periodic solution, and extends it to drives with several frequencies.
>
> *Source: Series I §4.1, §4.2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2 §1–2 · PHY 287, week 11 slides and Problem Set 9 (Problems 1–2)*

^thm-b1-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $p(i\omega) = \omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + i\gamma\omega$ vanishes only if both parts do, i.e. only for $\gamma\omega = 0$ and $\omega = \omega_0$; at $\omega = 0$ it equals $\omega_0^2$. So [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] (3) gives the complex solution $f_0\chi e^{i\omega t}$ of $L[\tilde z] = f_0e^{i\omega t}$, and its real part solves the real equation ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]). Write $p(i\omega) = \lvert p\rvert e^{i\delta}$; since $\operatorname{Im}p = \gamma\omega \ge 0$, $\delta \in [0, \pi)$, with $\cos\delta = \operatorname{Re}p/\lvert p\rvert$ and $\sin\delta = \operatorname{Im}p/\lvert p\rvert$. Then $f_0\chi e^{i\omega t} = (f_0/\lvert p\rvert)e^{i(\omega t - \delta)}$, whose real part is $A\cos(\omega t - \delta)$. A second periodic solution would differ from $x_p$ by a periodic solution of the homogeneous equation; for $\gamma > 0$ every nonzero homogeneous solution decays ([[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]), so there is none. For a sum of drives apply this to each term (shifting the time origin by $\theta_j/\omega_j$ turns $\cos(\omega_jt + \theta_j)$ into $\cos\omega_jt$) and add, by [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]].

> [!remark] Remark: What the complex route saves
> The real route ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^der-a1-6-1|derivation of Theorem §A1.6.1]]) needs the two-term ansatz $a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t$, because $d/dt$ turns cosines into sines; it ends in a $2\times2$ linear system whose determinant is $(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2 = \lvert p(i\omega)\rvert^2$, and the amplitude and phase must be reassembled at the end. The complex route does all of this in one division: $e^{i\omega t}$ is an eigenfunction of $d/dt$, whereas $\{\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t\}$ only span a two-dimensional subspace that $d/dt$ maps into itself and mixes. One complex number $\chi(\omega)$ carries both the amplitude (its modulus) and the phase lag (minus its argument). For the coupled systems of [[§B2.3 Driven Coupled Oscillators and Antiresonance|§B2.3]] the saving multiplies with every mode.
>
> *Source: Series I §4.2.1*

^rem-b1-3-1

> [!caution] Caution: Conventions in the sources
> - Schwartz's Lecture 2 complexifies with $e^{-i\omega t}$ and calls the *driving* frequency $\omega_d$; his complex amplitude $1/(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2 - i\gamma\omega_d)$ is the complex conjugate of $\chi$ here. The series, like this note, uses $\omega_d$ for the frequency of *free damped* oscillation.
> - PHY 287's slides write "$\delta = \gamma\omega/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)$" for $\tan\delta$. A calculator's arctangent returns an angle in $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, which is wrong by $\pi$ above resonance; $\delta$ always lies between $0$ and $\pi$ because $\sin\delta \ge 0$.

## The initial-value problem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.2: General Solution and Initial-Value Problem
> Let $\gamma > 0$, or $\gamma = 0$ and $\omega \ne \omega_0$. The solution of $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = f_0\cos\omega t$ with $x(0) = x_0$, $\dot x(0) = v_0$ is unique and equals
>
> $$
> x(t) = \underbrace{A\cos(\omega t - \delta)}_{\text{steady state}} + \underbrace{x_{\text{free}}\bigl(t;\ x_0 - A\cos\delta,\ v_0 - A\omega\sin\delta\bigr)}_{\text{transient}},
> $$
>
> where $x_{\text{free}}(t; a, b)$ is the free solution with initial data $a$, $b$ ([[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]; for $\gamma = 0$, $a\cos\omega_0t + (b/\omega_0)\sin\omega_0t$). For $\gamma > 0$ the transient decays at the slowest rate $r(\gamma)$ of [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]], so $x(t) - x_p(t) \to 0$: after a few times $1/r$ (for an underdamped oscillator $1/r = 2/\gamma = 2Q/\omega_0$, about $Q/\pi$ cycles) the motion is the steady state whatever the initial state. For an underdamped oscillator started from rest the transient is
>
> $$
> e^{-\gamma t/2}\bigl(B_1\cos\omega_dt + B_2\sin\omega_dt\bigr), \qquad B_1 = -A\cos\delta, \qquad B_2 = -\frac{A\bigl(\omega\sin\delta + \tfrac\gamma2\cos\delta\bigr)}{\omega_d} .
> $$
>
> This makes explicit the "steady state plus transient" statement of [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]].
>
> *Source: Series I §4 (structure of the solution space), §4.2.2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2 §2.1 · PHY 287, week 11 slides and Problem Set 9 (Problem 3)*

^thm-b1-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] (3), $x - x_p$ solves the homogeneous equation, with initial data $x_0 - x_p(0)$ and $v_0 - \dot x_p(0)$; here $x_p(0) = A\cos\delta$ and $\dot x_p(0) = -A\omega\sin(-\delta) = A\omega\sin\delta$. The free solution with these data is unique ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]]) and given by [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]. From rest, $a = -A\cos\delta$ and $b = -A\omega\sin\delta$; the underdamped formula gives $B_1 = a$ and $B_2 = (b + \gamma a/2)/\omega_d$, which is the stated value.

^der-b1-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why the steady state is what one measures
> The oscillator forgets its initial state; only the imprint of the drive survives. That is why $A(\omega)$ and $\delta(\omega)$, equivalently $\chi(\omega)$, are *the* response of the system. Because the equation is linear, knowing $\chi$ at every frequency gives the long-time response to any drive that is a sum of sinusoids ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]); every periodic force is such a sum ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series|§B4.1]]), and non-periodic forces are integrals of sinusoids ([[§B4.4 Fourier Transforms and the Delta Function|§B4.4]]). While the transient lasts, the free oscillation at $\omega_d$ and the response at $\omega$ beat against each other (the figure after [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]]).
>
> *Source: Series I §4.2.2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2 §1, §2.1*

## Driving without damping

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.3.3: Undamped Driven Oscillator
> The solutions of $\ddot x + \omega_0^2x = f_0\cos\omega t$ are:
> 1. **Off resonance**, $\omega \ne \omega_0$: $\;x(t) = \dfrac{f_0}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}\cos\omega t + B_1\cos\omega_0t + B_2\sin\omega_0t$. Every solution is bounded; started from rest it beats ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^rem-a1-6-5|Remark: Driving without damping, and beats]]).
> 2. **On resonance**, $\omega = \omega_0$: $\;x(t) = \dfrac{f_0}{2\omega_0}\,t\sin\omega_0t + B_1\cos\omega_0t + B_2\sin\omega_0t$. Every solution is unbounded: the amplitude grows in proportion to $t$ (**secular growth**). The solution from rest is the first term alone.
>
> This turns the from-rest limit of [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^rem-a1-6-5|Remark: Driving without damping, and beats]] into a statement about all solutions, derived without a limit.
>
> *Source: Series I §4.1, §4.1.1–4.1.2 · PHY 287, week 11 slides and Problem Set 9 (Problems 1, 3d–e)*

^thm-b1-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Here $p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + \omega_0^2$. (1) $p(i\omega) = \omega_0^2 - \omega^2 \ne 0$, so the particular solution is $f_0\cos\omega t/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)$ ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]); add the free solutions ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]). All terms are bounded.
>
> (2) Now $i\omega_0$ is a root of $p$: $e^{i\omega_0t}$ solves the homogeneous equation, $L$ annihilates it, and no constant multiple of it can produce the drive. The root is simple, $p'(i\omega_0) = 2i\omega_0 \ne 0$, so by [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] (4)
>
> $$
> \tilde z_p = \frac{f_0}{2i\omega_0}\,t\,e^{i\omega_0t}, \qquad x_p = \operatorname{Re}\Bigl[-\frac{if_0}{2\omega_0}\,t\,(\cos\omega_0t + i\sin\omega_0t)\Bigr] = \frac{f_0}{2\omega_0}\,t\sin\omega_0t .
> $$
>
> Directly: $\ddot{\tilde z}_p = \hat c\,(2i\omega_0 - \omega_0^2t)e^{i\omega_0t}$ for $\tilde z_p = \hat c\,te^{i\omega_0t}$, so $\ddot{\tilde z}_p + \omega_0^2\tilde z_p = 2i\omega_0\hat c\,e^{i\omega_0t}$: the terms in $t$ cancel and $\hat c = f_0/2i\omega_0$. This agrees with the limit $\omega \to \omega_0$ of the from-rest solution of (1) computed at level A. Since $x_p(0) = \dot x_p(0) = 0$, it is the solution from rest. For any $B_1$, $B_2$, at $t_n = (2n + \tfrac12)\pi/\omega_0$ the solution equals $f_0t_n/2\omega_0 + B_2$, which grows without bound.

^der-b1-3-3

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]], [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Where the extra factor of t comes from
> The same identity, $L[te^{\lambda t}] = \bigl(p'(\lambda) + t\,p(\lambda)\bigr)e^{\lambda t}$, produced the critically damped solution $te^{-\gamma t/2}$ ([[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]) and produces the resonant response here: in both cases the exponent of interest is already a root of $p$. Physically, the velocity of $x_p$ at large $t$ is $\approx \tfrac12f_0\,t\cos\omega_0t$, in phase with the force $F_0\cos\omega_0t$: the force pushes along the motion during every cycle, and with nothing to dissipate the work the amplitude grows (the rate is computed in [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^ex-b1-4-2|Example §B1.4.2]]). A real system either has damping, which caps the growth ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^ex-b1-3-1|Example §B1.3.1]]), or leaves the linear regime ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^rem-b1-1-2|Remark: Where linearity comes from, and where it stops]]).
>
> *Source: Series I §4.1.2*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.3.1: Resonant Start-Up of a Damped Oscillator
> An underdamped oscillator at rest is driven at $\omega = \omega_0$ from $t = 0$. Then $\delta = \pi/2$ and $A = f_0/(\gamma\omega_0) = QF_0/k$, so $x_p = A\sin\omega_0t$, and [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]] gives $B_1 = 0$, $B_2 = -A\omega_0/\omega_d$:
>
> $$
> x(t) = A\Bigl[\sin\omega_0t - \frac{\omega_0}{\omega_d}\,e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin\omega_dt\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> - *Early times*, $\gamma t \ll 1$: $e^{-\gamma t/2} \approx 1 - \gamma t/2$ and $\omega_d = \omega_0 + O(\gamma^2/\omega_0)$, so $x \approx A\,\tfrac{\gamma t}{2}\sin\omega_0t = \dfrac{f_0}{2\omega_0}\,t\sin\omega_0t$: exactly the secular growth of the undamped oscillator ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]]).
> - *For $Q \gg 1$, all times*: $x \approx A\bigl(1 - e^{-\gamma t/2}\bigr)\sin\omega_0t$. The envelope rises like a charging capacitor and reaches 95 % of $A$ at $\gamma t/2 = 3$, i.e. after $6Q/\omega_0$, about $Q$ cycles.
> - The linear growth, continued, would reach $A$ at $t = 2/\gamma$: damping takes over on the same time scale on which the free oscillation decays. A high-$Q$ oscillator responds strongly but slowly.
>
> *Source: Series I §4.1.2, §4.2.2 · PHY 287, week 11 slides (transient at resonance)*

> [!example] Example §B1.3.2: A Complete Initial-Value Problem
> Solve $\ddot y + 5\dot y + 5y = 5\cos2t$ ($t$ in seconds) from rest, $y(0) = \dot y(0) = 0$. (Level A found only its steady state, [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^ex-a1-6-2|Example §A1.6.2]].) Here $\omega_0^2 = 5$, $\gamma = 5$, $\omega = 2$, $f_0 = 5$.
>
> *Steady state.* $p(2i) = 5 - 4 + 10i = 1 + 10i$, so $f_0\chi = 5/(1 + 10i) = 5(1 - 10i)/101 = 0.0495 - 0.495\,i$, of modulus $0.498$ and argument $-84.3^\circ$:
>
> $$
> y_p = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[(0.0495 - 0.495\,i)e^{2it}\bigr] = 0.0495\cos2t + 0.495\sin2t = 0.498\cos(2t - 1.471) .
> $$
>
> *Free motion.* $\gamma/2 = 2.5 > \omega_0 = 2.24$: overdamped ($Q = 0.45$), with $\lambda_\pm = (-5 \pm \sqrt5)/2 = -1.382$ and $-3.618\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
>
> *Transient.* Its initial data are $a = -y_p(0) = -0.0495$ and $b = -\dot y_p(0) = -2(0.495) = -0.990\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. From [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]] with $\lambda_+ - \lambda_- = \sqrt5 = 2.236$: $C_+ = (b - \lambda_-a)/2.236 = -0.523$ and $C_- = -(b - \lambda_+a)/2.236 = 0.473$. Altogether
>
> $$
> y(t) = 0.0495\cos2t + 0.495\sin2t - 0.523\,e^{-1.382\,t} + 0.473\,e^{-3.618\,t} .
> $$
>
> At $t = 0$ the terms add to zero (to rounding), and so do the slopes. The fast exponential is gone after about a second; the slow one falls below 1 % of the steady amplitude after $t = \ln\bigl(0.523/0.005\bigr)/1.382 = 3.4\ \mathrm s$. A plot of $y$ "wobbles irregularly at first and then settles into a steady oscillation", which is how the course's practice problem identifies this equation from its graph.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Final practice (Problem 4)*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The solution set of the inhomogeneous equation is a translate of the null space of $L$ ([[§11 Products and Quotients of Vector Spaces#^ladr-3-97|LADR Def. 3.97]]); [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]] picks its element by the initial data.
> - The resonant limit $\omega \to \omega_0$ of the off-resonance solution is a $0/0$ limit in $\omega$ ([[§30 L'Hospital's Rule#^thm-30-1|451 Thm. §30.1]]); [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] (4) gets the same answer without it.
> - Driving a system with several degrees of freedom: each normal mode responds with its own $\chi$, giving several resonances and an antiresonance ([[§B2.3 Driven Coupled Oscillators and Antiresonance|§B2.3]]); the electrical version is the driven RLC circuit ([[§A11.4 Resonance in AC Circuits#^thm-a11-4-1|EM Theorem §A11.4.1]]).
