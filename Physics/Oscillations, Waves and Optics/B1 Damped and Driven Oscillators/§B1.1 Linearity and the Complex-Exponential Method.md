Level A solved the oscillator equations one at a time, by a trial solution or a substitution ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]], [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]) and used complex exponentials as a shortcut whose legitimacy it only asserted ([[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^rem-a1-3-2|Remark: The reference point as a complex number]]). This section sets up the machinery that makes the shortcut a method: the oscillator equation as a linear operator, superposition as a theorem with a stated domain, uniqueness and the two-dimensional solution space, the action of the operator on exponentials, and the lemma that real parts of complex solutions are real solutions. The rest of the chapter, and the coupled systems and waves of B2–B3, compute in this language.

## Linear equations and superposition

> [!definition] Definition §B1.1.1: Linear Differential Operator; Homogeneous and Inhomogeneous Equations
> For a twice-differentiable function $x(t)$, real or complex valued, the **damped-oscillator operator** is
>
> $$
> L[x] = \ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x ,
> $$
>
> with constants $\gamma = b/m \ge 0$ and $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} > 0$ ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]). More generally $L = \sum_{n=0}^{N} a_n\,d^n/dt^n$ with constant coefficients $a_n$. An operator is **linear** if
>
> $$
> L[c_1x_1 + c_2x_2] = c_1L[x_1] + c_2L[x_2]
> $$
>
> for all functions $x_1, x_2$ and constants $c_1, c_2$; for a differential operator this means that every term contains $x$ or one of its derivatives exactly once, to the first power. The equation $L[x] = f(t)$ is **homogeneous** if $f \equiv 0$ (the free oscillator) and **inhomogeneous** otherwise; for an oscillator $f = F(t)/m$ is the driving force per unit mass. $L$ has **real coefficients** if all $a_n$ are real.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1 §5 · Series I §2.1, §4 (structure of the solution space)*

^def-b1-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.1: Superposition
> Let $L$ be linear.
> 1. If $L[x_1] = f_1$ and $L[x_2] = f_2$, then $L[c_1x_1 + c_2x_2] = c_1f_1 + c_2f_2$: the response to a sum of drives is the sum of the responses.
> 2. The solutions of the homogeneous equation $L[x] = 0$ form a vector space.
> 3. If $x_p$ is one solution of $L[x] = f$, the solutions of $L[x] = f$ are exactly the functions $x_p + x_h$ with $L[x_h] = 0$.
>
> *Domain: equations of motion linear in the displacement, i.e. a Hooke restoring force ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^mod-a1-1-1|Model §A1.1.1]]) and linear damping ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^mod-a1-5-1|Model §A1.5.1]]); for a real system this means displacements and speeds small enough that the linear terms of the force dominate ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-3|Theorem §A1.2.3]]).*
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1 §5–5.2 · Series I §2.1, §4 · PHY 287, Problem Set 9 (Problem 3)*

^thm-b1-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiation is linear, $(c_1x_1 + c_2x_2)^{(n)} = c_1x_1^{(n)} + c_2x_2^{(n)}$, and so is multiplication by a constant coefficient; summing the terms of $L$ gives (1). Taking $f_1 = f_2 = 0$ shows that linear combinations of homogeneous solutions are homogeneous solutions, and $x \equiv 0$ is one, which is (2). For (3): if $L[x] = f$ then $L[x - x_p] = f - f = 0$ by (1), so $x - x_p$ is a homogeneous solution; conversely $L[x_p + x_h] = f + 0 = f$.

^der-b1-1-1

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]], [[§8 Null Spaces and Ranges#^ladr-3-13|LADR Thm. 3.13]]

> [!remark] Remark: The layer changes
> For waves, superposition was assumed at level A ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^pr-a2-5-1|Principle §A2.5.1]]). Here it is a theorem: it follows from the linearity of the equation of motion, and it holds exactly as far as that linearity does. The same theorem gives superposition for the coupled oscillators of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators|§B2.1]] and for the wave equation of [[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation|§B3.2]]. In the language of linear algebra, (2) says the homogeneous solutions are the null space of the linear map $L$, and (3) that the solutions of $L[x] = f$ form its translate $x_p + \operatorname{null} L$.

^rem-b1-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Where linearity comes from, and where it stops
> Expand any force $F(x, \dot x)$ about an equilibrium at rest, $x = \dot x = 0$, where $F = 0$: to first order $F \approx -kx - b\dot x$ with $k = -\partial F/\partial x$ and $b = -\partial F/\partial\dot x$ at the equilibrium. That is why the damped, driven oscillator equation describes almost every system near equilibrium ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^rem-a1-2-2|Remark: Why oscillators are everywhere]]). The next terms, $x^2$, $x\dot x$, $\dot x^2$, …, are nonlinear: they are negligible only for small amplitudes, and when they are not, solutions no longer add and different driving frequencies mix ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^ex-b1-1-1|Example §B1.1.1]]). The argument needs the linear coefficient to be nonzero; at a flat minimum (for instance $U \propto x^4$) the leading behaviour is nonlinear however small the amplitude.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1 §2, §5.3*

^rem-b1-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.2: Uniqueness; the Solution Space Is Two-Dimensional
> Let $\gamma \ge 0$, $\omega_0 > 0$ and let $f$ be continuous.
> 1. Two solutions of $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = f(t)$ with the same position and velocity at one instant $t_0$ agree for all $t$, before and after $t_0$.
> 2. The real solutions of the homogeneous equation form a two-dimensional real vector space: the map $x \mapsto \bigl(x(0), \dot x(0)\bigr)$ is a linear bijection onto $\mathbf R^2$. Hence any two solutions whose initial vectors are linearly independent form a basis, and every solution is a combination of them. The complex solutions likewise form a two-dimensional complex vector space.
>
> This generalises the uniqueness argument in [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]] to damped and driven oscillators.
>
> *Source: Series I §2.1 (footnote on uniqueness), §2.2*

^thm-b1-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Uniqueness.* The difference $d = x_1 - x_2$ solves the homogeneous equation with $d(t_0) = \dot d(t_0) = 0$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]). Its "energy per unit mass" $E_d = \tfrac12\dot d^2 + \tfrac12\omega_0^2d^2 \ge 0$ satisfies
>
> $$
> \dot E_d = \dot d\,\bigl(\ddot d + \omega_0^2 d\bigr) = -\gamma\,\dot d^2 \le 0 .
> $$
>
> For $t \ge t_0$, $E_d$ does not increase from $E_d(t_0) = 0$ and is nonnegative, so it vanishes. For $t \le t_0$ use $\dot d^2 \le 2E_d$: then $\dot E_d \ge -2\gamma E_d$, so $\bigl(e^{2\gamma t}E_d\bigr)' = e^{2\gamma t}(\dot E_d + 2\gamma E_d) \ge 0$, and $e^{2\gamma t}E_d(t) \le e^{2\gamma t_0}E_d(t_0) = 0$. Thus $E_d \equiv 0$, which forces $d \equiv 0$.
>
> *Dimension.* The map $T: x \mapsto \bigl(x(0), \dot x(0)\bigr)$ is linear. By uniqueness its null space is $\{0\}$, so it is injective. It is surjective because for every pair $(x_0, v_0)$ a solution exists: [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]] exhibits, in each regime, a family with two free constants that can be fitted to any initial data (made explicit in [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]). So $T$ is an isomorphism and the space has dimension two. For complex solutions apply this to real and imaginary parts.

^der-b1-1-2

## Exponentials and the characteristic polynomial

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.3: Exponentials and the Characteristic Polynomial
> Let $L = \sum_n a_n\,d^n/dt^n$ have constant coefficients, and call $p(\lambda) = \sum_n a_n\lambda^n$ its **characteristic polynomial**; for the damped oscillator $p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + \gamma\lambda + \omega_0^2$. For every complex $\lambda$:
> 1. $L[e^{\lambda t}] = p(\lambda)\,e^{\lambda t}$ and $L[t\,e^{\lambda t}] = \bigl(p'(\lambda) + t\,p(\lambda)\bigr)e^{\lambda t}$.
> 2. $e^{\lambda t}$ solves $L[x] = 0$ if and only if $p(\lambda) = 0$ (the **characteristic equation**). If $\lambda$ is a double root, $p(\lambda) = p'(\lambda) = 0$, then $t\,e^{\lambda t}$ is a second solution.
> 3. If $p(s) \ne 0$, the equation $L[x] = g\,e^{st}$ (with $g$ a complex constant) has the particular solution $x = \dfrac{g}{p(s)}\,e^{st}$.
> 4. If $s$ is a simple root, $p(s) = 0 \ne p'(s)$, it has the particular solution $x = \dfrac{g}{p'(s)}\,t\,e^{st}$.
>
> *Source: Series I §2.4, §3.2, §3.5, §4.1.2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1 §4, §6–7*

^thm-b1-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $\lambda = \sigma + i\Omega$, $e^{\lambda t} = e^{\sigma t}(\cos\Omega t + i\sin\Omega t)$ by Euler's formula; differentiating real and imaginary parts with the product and chain rules gives $\tfrac{d}{dt}e^{\lambda t} = \lambda e^{\lambda t}$, hence $\tfrac{d^n}{dt^n}e^{\lambda t} = \lambda^n e^{\lambda t}$ and $L[e^{\lambda t}] = p(\lambda)e^{\lambda t}$. By Leibniz's rule $\tfrac{d^n}{dt^n}\bigl(t\,e^{\lambda t}\bigr) = (\lambda^n t + n\lambda^{n-1})e^{\lambda t}$, and summing with the coefficients $a_n$ gives $t\,p(\lambda) + p'(\lambda)$. For the oscillator, directly: $(te^{\lambda t})'' + \gamma(te^{\lambda t})' + \omega_0^2te^{\lambda t} = \bigl[(2\lambda + \lambda^2t) + \gamma(1 + \lambda t) + \omega_0^2t\bigr]e^{\lambda t} = \bigl[(2\lambda + \gamma) + t\,p(\lambda)\bigr]e^{\lambda t}$.
>
> (2) follows from (1) because $e^{\lambda t} \ne 0$. (3): by linearity $L\bigl[(g/p(s))e^{st}\bigr] = (g/p(s))\,p(s)e^{st} = ge^{st}$. (4): by (1) with $p(s) = 0$, $L\bigl[(g/p'(s))\,te^{st}\bigr] = (g/p'(s))\,p'(s)e^{st}$.

^der-b1-1-3

*Uses:* [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why exponentials
> Sines and cosines reproduce themselves only under an even number of derivatives, so a damping term $\gamma\dot x$ turns a cosine into a sine and spoils a trigonometric guess. The exponential is the one function that every derivative merely multiplies by a constant: it is an eigenfunction of $d/dt$, and $L$ acts on it as multiplication by $p(\lambda)$. That turns a differential equation into an algebraic one. Over the complex numbers $p$ always has $N$ roots counted with multiplicity, so $L = (d/dt - \lambda_1)\cdots(d/dt - \lambda_N)$ factors and exponential solutions always exist; exponentials play for linear differential equations the role that complex numbers play for polynomial equations. Statement (1) also unifies two situations met later: the second solution $t\,e^{-\gamma t/2}$ of the critically damped oscillator ([[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]) and the resonant response $t\sin\omega_0t$ of the undamped oscillator ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-3|Theorem §B1.3.3]]): whenever the exponent of interest is "used up" as a root of $p$, multiply by $t$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1 §4, §6.1, §7 · Series I §4.2.1 (remark)*

## Complexification

> [!definition] Definition §B1.1.2: Complexification; Complex Amplitude
> To solve a real linear equation $L[x] = f$ with a sinusoidal right side $f = f_0\cos(\omega t + \theta)$, **complexify**: replace $f$ by $\tilde f = f_0\,e^{i(\omega t + \theta)}$, whose real part is $f$, solve $L[\tilde z] = \tilde f$ for a complex function $\tilde z(t)$, and take $x = \operatorname{Re}\tilde z$. A sinusoid of angular frequency $\omega$ is written
>
> $$
> x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat x\,e^{i\omega t}\bigr], \qquad \hat x = A\,e^{i\phi},
> $$
>
> with **complex amplitude** $\hat x$: its modulus is the amplitude and its argument the phase constant. It is the phasor of [[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^def-a1-3-2|Def. §A1.3.2]] at $t = 0$. Notation: a tilde marks a complex function of time, a hat a complex constant, a star the complex conjugate. The time dependence is $e^{+i\omega t}$ throughout, and the physical quantity is always the real part.
>
> *Source: Series I §1 (Convention 1.1), §2.4 · PHY 287, week 11 slides · PHY 300, Worksheet 2*

^def-b1-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.4: Real Parts of Complex Solutions
> Let $L$ be linear with real coefficients.
> 1. If $L[\tilde z] = 0$, then $\operatorname{Re}\tilde z$ and $\operatorname{Im}\tilde z$ are real solutions of $L[x] = 0$.
> 2. If $L[\tilde z] = \tilde g$, then $L[\operatorname{Re}\tilde z] = \operatorname{Re}\tilde g$ and $L[\operatorname{Im}\tilde z] = \operatorname{Im}\tilde g$.
> 3. $L[\tilde z^*] = \bigl(L[\tilde z]\bigr)^*$; in particular the nonreal roots of $p$ and the corresponding solutions come in conjugate pairs.
>
> This is the justification that [[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^rem-a1-3-2|Remark: The reference point as a complex number]] and [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^rem-a1-6-2|Remark: The complex shortcut]] assumed.
>
> *Domain: linear operators with real coefficients; it says nothing about products of solutions.*
> *Source: Series I §2.4 (Lemma 2.3) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2 §2 · PHY 287, week 11 slides*

^thm-b1-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $\tilde z = u + iv$ with $u$, $v$ real. Differentiation acts on real and imaginary parts separately and the coefficients are real, so $L[\tilde z] = L[u] + iL[v]$ with $L[u]$ and $L[v]$ real. (1): if this vanishes, its real part $L[u]$ and imaginary part $L[v]$ vanish. (2): compare real and imaginary parts of $L[u] + iL[v] = \operatorname{Re}\tilde g + i\operatorname{Im}\tilde g$. (3): $L[\tilde z^*] = L[u] - iL[v] = (L[u] + iL[v])^*$. Since $p$ has real coefficients, $p(\lambda^*) = p(\lambda)^*$, so $\lambda^*$ is a root whenever $\lambda$ is.

^der-b1-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^def-b1-1-1|Def. §B1.1.1]], [[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-1|LADR Def. 4.1]], [[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-1b|LADR Def. 4.1b]], [[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-4|LADR Thm. 4.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.5: Real Solutions from a Conjugate Pair of Roots
> Let $L$ have real coefficients and let $\lambda = -\sigma + i\Omega$ ($\Omega \ne 0$) and $\lambda^*$ be roots of $p$. The complex solution $\tilde z = \hat c_1e^{\lambda t} + \hat c_2e^{\lambda^* t}$ is real for all $t$ if and only if
>
> $$
> \hat c_2 = \hat c_1^{\,*} \qquad\text{(reality condition)} .
> $$
>
> Then $\tilde z = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat C e^{\lambda t}\bigr]$ with $\hat C = 2\hat c_1 = Ae^{i\phi}$, that is
>
> $$
> x(t) = A\,e^{-\sigma t}\cos(\Omega t + \phi) = e^{-\sigma t}\bigl(B_1\cos\Omega t + B_2\sin\Omega t\bigr), \qquad B_1 = \operatorname{Re}\hat C, \quad B_2 = -\operatorname{Im}\hat C .
> $$
>
> The two real constants of the general real solution are packed into one complex number. For the undamped oscillator ($\sigma = 0$, $\Omega = \omega_0$) the initial data give $\hat C = x_0 - i\,v_0/\omega_0$.
>
> *Source: Series I §2.4 (reality condition, three equivalent forms) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1 §4.1*

^thm-b1-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\tilde z - \tilde z^* = (\hat c_1 - \hat c_2^{\,*})e^{\lambda t} + (\hat c_2 - \hat c_1^{\,*})e^{\lambda^* t}$. The functions $e^{\lambda t}$ and $e^{\lambda^* t}$ are linearly independent: if $ae^{\lambda t} + be^{\lambda^* t} \equiv 0$, then at $t = 0$ the function and its derivative give $a + b = 0$ and $a\lambda + b\lambda^* = 0$, so $a(\lambda - \lambda^*) = 0$ and $a = b = 0$ since $\lambda \ne \lambda^*$. So $\tilde z$ is real for all $t$ exactly when $\hat c_2 = \hat c_1^{\,*}$. Then $\tilde z = \hat c_1e^{\lambda t} + \bigl(\hat c_1e^{\lambda t}\bigr)^* = 2\operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat c_1e^{\lambda t}\bigr]$, and with $\hat C = Ae^{i\phi}$, $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[Ae^{i\phi}e^{-\sigma t}e^{i\Omega t}\bigr] = Ae^{-\sigma t}\cos(\Omega t + \phi)$; expanding the cosine gives $B_1 = A\cos\phi$ and $B_2 = -A\sin\phi$. For $\sigma = 0$: $x(0) = \operatorname{Re}\hat C = x_0$ and $\dot x(0) = \operatorname{Re}(i\omega_0\hat C) = -\omega_0\operatorname{Im}\hat C = v_0$.

^der-b1-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]], [[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-14|LADR Thm. 4.14]], [[§4 Span and Linear Independence#^ladr-2-15|LADR Def. 2.15]]

> [!remark] Remark: Three forms of one solution
> The free oscillation can be written as $B_1\cos\omega_0t + B_2\sin\omega_0t$, as $A\cos(\omega_0t + \phi)$, or as $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat Ce^{i\omega_0t}\bigr]$, with the dictionary $\hat C = B_1 - iB_2 = Ae^{i\phi}$. The first is the quickest for initial conditions, the second shows the physics, and the third is the one to compute with: the polar form of $\hat C$ *is* the amplitude–phase form, so no trigonometric identity is needed, and its argument lands in the right quadrant automatically. For the undamped oscillator $\hat C = x_0 - i\,v_0/\omega_0$ is the reference point of [[§A1.3 Simple Harmonic Motion and Circular Motion#^ex-a1-3-1|Example §A1.3.1]] at $t = 0$, read as a complex number: its polar angle is $\phi$, which is why the third start there needs no separate quadrant check ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^cau-a1-1-1|Caution: Finding the phase constant]]).
>
> *Source: Series I §2.4*

^rem-b1-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.1.6: Time Average of a Product of Sinusoids
> Let $a(t) = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat a\,e^{i\omega t}\bigr]$ and $b(t) = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat b\,e^{i\omega t}\bigr]$ with the same $\omega \ne 0$. Then
>
> $$
> a(t)\,b(t) = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\hat a\,\hat b^*\bigr) + \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\hat a\,\hat b\,e^{2i\omega t}\bigr), \qquad \langle ab\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\hat a\,\hat b^*\bigr) = \tfrac12\lvert\hat a\rvert\lvert\hat b\rvert\cos(\phi_a - \phi_b),
> $$
>
> where $\langle\cdot\rangle$ is the average over a period and $\phi_a$, $\phi_b$ are the arguments of $\hat a$, $\hat b$. In particular $\langle a^2\rangle = \tfrac12\lvert\hat a\rvert^2$.
>
> *Source: Series I §5.5 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 2 §2.3 (both in real form)*

^thm-b1-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $a = \tfrac12(\hat ae^{i\omega t} + \hat a^*e^{-i\omega t})$ and likewise $b$. Multiplying out,
>
> $$
> ab = \tfrac14\bigl(\hat a\hat b\,e^{2i\omega t} + \hat a^*\hat b^*e^{-2i\omega t}\bigr) + \tfrac14\bigl(\hat a\hat b^* + \hat a^*\hat b\bigr) = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\hat a\hat b\,e^{2i\omega t}\bigr) + \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\hat a\hat b^*\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The first term averages to zero over a period $2\pi/\omega$ (it is a sinusoid at $2\omega$); the second is constant. With $\hat a = \lvert\hat a\rvert e^{i\phi_a}$ and $\hat b = \lvert\hat b\rvert e^{i\phi_b}$, $\hat a\hat b^* = \lvert\hat a\rvert\lvert\hat b\rvert e^{i(\phi_a - \phi_b)}$.

^der-b1-1-6

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^def-b1-1-2|Def. §B1.1.2]], [[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-2|LADR Def. 4.2]], [[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-2b|LADR Def. 4.2b]]

> [!caution] Caution: Where the real-part rule fails, and sign conventions
> - **Products.** $\operatorname{Re}(\tilde a\tilde b) \ne \operatorname{Re}\tilde a\,\operatorname{Re}\tilde b$. Energies and powers are quadratic: compute them from the real quantities, or average with [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|Theorem §B1.1.6]].
> - **Complex coefficients.** The lemma needs real coefficients. The Schrödinger equation contains $i$ ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^pr-a4-1-1|QM Principle §A4.1.1]]), so the real part of a solution is not a solution; there the complex wave function itself is the state, and superposition holds with complex coefficients ([[§A4.1 The Schrödinger Equation and Stationary States#^thm-a4-1-2|QM Theorem §A4.1.2]]).
> - **$e^{+i\omega t}$ or $e^{-i\omega t}$.** Quantum mechanics writes stationary states with $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, and Schwartz's Lecture 2 complexifies with $e^{-i\omega t}$. Both are legitimate (the conjugate of a solution is a solution), but every complex amplitude, response function and phase angle then comes out as the complex conjugate of the one here. Translate before comparing formulas. The optics chapters B8–B13 use $e^{-i\omega t}$ throughout: [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-1|§B8.1, Caution: The optics sign convention, opposite to Chapters B1–B7]].
> - **$\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ or $\cos(\omega t - \phi)$.** Level A and PHY 287 write free oscillations as $A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, so $\hat C = Ae^{i\phi}$; the series writes $A\cos(\omega_0t - \phi)$ with $\hat C = Ae^{-i\phi}$. The phase constants differ in sign, and so do the formulas for them: $\tan\phi = -v_0/(\omega_0x_0)$ in the first convention, $\tan\phi = +v_0/(\omega_0x_0)$ in the second (each still fixing $\phi$ only up to $\pi$).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.1.1: Two Drives, With and Without Linearity
> An undamped oscillator is driven at two frequencies, $\ddot x + \omega_0^2x = f_1\cos\omega_1t + f_2\cos\omega_2t$ (neither equal to $\omega_0$). By [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]] the responses to the two drives simply add; by [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] (3) with $s = i\omega_j$, $p(i\omega_j) = \omega_0^2 - \omega_j^2$,
>
> $$
> x_p = \frac{f_1}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_1^2}\cos\omega_1t + \frac{f_2}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_2^2}\cos\omega_2t .
> $$
>
> Each response sits at its own frequency, unaffected by the other: two radio stations at nearby frequencies do not disturb each other, and a receiver can pick out one coefficient.
>
> Add a small nonlinear term, $\ddot x + \omega_0^2x + \varepsilon x^2 = f_1\cos\omega_1t + f_2\cos\omega_2t$. Write $x = x^{(0)} + \varepsilon x^{(1)} + \dots$ with $x^{(0)} = a_1\cos\omega_1t + a_2\cos\omega_2t$ the linear response above. To first order in $\varepsilon$, $\ddot x^{(1)} + \omega_0^2x^{(1)} = -\bigl(x^{(0)}\bigr)^2$, and
>
> $$
> \bigl(x^{(0)}\bigr)^2 = \tfrac12(a_1^2 + a_2^2) + \tfrac12a_1^2\cos2\omega_1t + \tfrac12a_2^2\cos2\omega_2t + a_1a_2\bigl[\cos(\omega_1 + \omega_2)t + \cos(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t\bigr] .
> $$
>
> The correction is driven at $0$, $2\omega_1$, $2\omega_2$ and $\omega_1 \pm \omega_2$: the drives now interact and new frequencies appear (mixing). The effect is of relative size $\varepsilon a/\omega_0^2$ for amplitude $a$, which is why superposition is a small-amplitude statement.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1 §5.2*
