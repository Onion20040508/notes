*Sources: Series Part I (the user's notes), §3 · Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lecture 1 · PHY 287 (Kandula, Fall 2023) · PHY 300 (Mihaly, Fall 2024).*

Level A found the three kinds of motion of the oscillator with linear damping ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]]), its energy loss $dE/dt = -bv^2$ and, for light damping, the decay $E \approx E_0e^{-\gamma t}$ ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]]) and the quality factor $Q = \omega_0/\gamma$ ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]). Here the same results come out of the characteristic equation of [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], now with the geometry of the roots, the initial-value problem solved explicitly in every regime, a precise sense in which critical damping is "fastest", and exact (not cycle-averaged) formulas for the decay per period and the energy.

> [!remark] Remark: Notation across the sources
> The equation is $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0$ with $\gamma = b/m$, as at level A, in PHY 287 and in Schwartz. The series writes $2\beta = b/m$, so its $\beta$ is $\gamma/2$: its $e^{-\beta t}$ is $e^{-\gamma t/2}$ and its $Q = \omega_0/2\beta$ is the same $Q = \omega_0/\gamma$. Electromagnetism's $\gamma = R/2L$ also corresponds to $\gamma/2$ here ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^rem-a1-5-4|Remark: The electrical twin]]). The damped frequency is $\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$ (level A's $\omega'$, Schwartz's $\omega_u$, PHY 287's $\omega$ or $\omega_1$); in the overdamped case $\kappa = \sqrt{\gamma^2/4 - \omega_0^2}$ (the series calls this $\gamma$, a clash to watch for).

^rem-b1-2-1

## The characteristic roots

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.1: Characteristic Roots of the Damped Oscillator
> The damped oscillator ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^mod-a1-5-1|Model §A1.5.1]]) has characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + \gamma\lambda + \omega_0^2$, with roots
>
> $$
> \lambda_\pm = -\frac\gamma2 \pm \sqrt{\frac{\gamma^2}{4} - \omega_0^2}, \qquad \lambda_+ + \lambda_- = -\gamma, \qquad \lambda_+\lambda_- = \omega_0^2 .
> $$
>
> - **Underdamped**, $\gamma < 2\omega_0$ ($Q > \tfrac12$): a complex-conjugate pair $\lambda_\pm = -\gamma/2 \pm i\omega_d$ on the circle $\lvert\lambda\rvert = \omega_0$.
> - **Critically damped**, $\gamma = 2\omega_0$ ($Q = \tfrac12$): the double root $\lambda = -\omega_0$.
> - **Overdamped**, $\gamma > 2\omega_0$ ($Q < \tfrac12$): two real roots $\lambda_\pm = -\gamma/2 \pm \kappa$ with $\lambda_- < -\omega_0 < \lambda_+ < 0$.
>
> For every $\gamma > 0$ both roots have negative real part; at $\gamma = 0$ they are $\pm i\omega_0$.
>
> Level A found these roots with the exponential trial solution ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^rem-a1-5-2|Remark: The exponential shortcut]]); new here are their geometry (the circle $\lvert\lambda\rvert = \omega_0$, the ordering of the overdamped roots) and the sum and product rules used below.
>
> *Source: Series I §3.2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1 §4 · PHY 287, week 10 slides*

^thm-b1-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The roots follow from the quadratic formula, and $p(\lambda) = (\lambda - \lambda_+)(\lambda - \lambda_-)$ gives the sum and product. Underdamped: $\lvert\lambda_\pm\rvert^2 = \gamma^2/4 + \omega_d^2 = \omega_0^2$, and the real part is $-\gamma/2$. Overdamped: both roots are real, their sum is negative and their product $\omega_0^2$ positive, so both are negative; since $\lambda_- < \lambda_+$ and $\lambda_+\lambda_- = \omega_0^2$, $\lvert\lambda_-\rvert > \omega_0 > \lvert\lambda_+\rvert$.

^der-b1-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-15|LADR Thm. 4.15]]

![[ph-wo-b1-2-1.svg]]
*The roots $\lambda_\pm$ in the complex plane as the damping grows from zero (arrows). They start at $\pm i\omega_0$ and slide along the circle $\lvert\lambda\rvert = \omega_0$ (blue; points for $Q = 5$ and $Q = 1$), meet at $-\omega_0$ at critical damping, and then split along the negative real axis (red): $\lambda_-$ runs off to $-\infty$ while $\lambda_+$ creeps back toward $0$. The real part of a root is a decay rate, the imaginary part an oscillation frequency.*

## General solution and initial-value problem

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.2: General Solution of the Damped Oscillator
> The solution of $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0$ with $x(0) = x_0$, $\dot x(0) = v_0$ is, in the three regimes,
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \text{underdamped:}\quad & x(t) = e^{-\gamma t/2}\Bigl[x_0\cos\omega_dt + \frac{v_0 + \gamma x_0/2}{\omega_d}\,\sin\omega_dt\Bigr] = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat C\,e^{\lambda_+t}\bigr], \quad \hat C = x_0 - i\,\frac{v_0 + \gamma x_0/2}{\omega_d},\\
> \text{critically damped:}\quad & x(t) = \bigl[x_0 + (v_0 + \gamma x_0/2)\,t\bigr]\,e^{-\gamma t/2},\\
> \text{overdamped:}\quad & x(t) = \frac{(v_0 - \lambda_-x_0)\,e^{\lambda_+t} - (v_0 - \lambda_+x_0)\,e^{\lambda_-t}}{\lambda_+ - \lambda_-}, \qquad \lambda_+ - \lambda_- = 2\kappa ,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> and there are no other solutions. In amplitude–phase form the underdamped solution is $Ae^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega_dt + \phi)$ with $Ae^{i\phi} = \hat C$.
>
> This completes [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-2|Theorem §A1.5.2]], which found the three forms (and that there are no others) by stripping off the factor $e^{-\gamma t/2}$: here they follow from the characteristic roots, and the constants are given explicitly in terms of $x_0$ and $v_0$.
>
> *Source: Series I §3.3–3.5 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1 §4 · PHY 287, week 10 slides and Problem Set 8 (Problem 3)*

^thm-b1-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] (2), $e^{\lambda_\pm t}$ are solutions.
>
> *Underdamped.* The general complex solution is $\hat c_1e^{\lambda_+t} + \hat c_2e^{\lambda_-t}$ with $\lambda_- = \lambda_+^*$; it is real exactly when $\hat c_2 = \hat c_1^{\,*}$, and then equals $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat Ce^{\lambda_+t}\bigr] = e^{-\gamma t/2}(B_1\cos\omega_dt + B_2\sin\omega_dt)$ with $B_1 = \operatorname{Re}\hat C$, $B_2 = -\operatorname{Im}\hat C$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]]). Initial data: $x(0) = B_1 = x_0$ and $\dot x(0) = -\tfrac\gamma2B_1 + \omega_dB_2 = v_0$, so $B_2 = (v_0 + \gamma x_0/2)/\omega_d$.
>
> *Overdamped.* $x = C_+e^{\lambda_+t} + C_-e^{\lambda_-t}$ with $C_+ + C_- = x_0$ and $\lambda_+C_+ + \lambda_-C_- = v_0$. Multiplying the first equation by $\lambda_-$ or $\lambda_+$ and subtracting gives $C_+ = (v_0 - \lambda_-x_0)/(\lambda_+ - \lambda_-)$ and $C_- = -(v_0 - \lambda_+x_0)/(\lambda_+ - \lambda_-)$.
>
> *Critically damped.* The ansatz gives only $e^{-\gamma t/2}$. Put $x = u(t)\,e^{-\gamma t/2}$ (reduction of order). Then $\dot x = (\dot u - \tfrac\gamma2u)e^{-\gamma t/2}$, $\ddot x = (\ddot u - \gamma\dot u + \tfrac{\gamma^2}4u)e^{-\gamma t/2}$, and with $\omega_0^2 = \gamma^2/4$,
>
> $$
> \ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = \Bigl[\ddot u + (-\gamma + \gamma)\dot u + \Bigl(\tfrac{\gamma^2}4 - \tfrac{\gamma^2}2 + \tfrac{\gamma^2}4\Bigr)u\Bigr]e^{-\gamma t/2} = \ddot u\,e^{-\gamma t/2} .
> $$
>
> The $u$ and $\dot u$ terms cancel because $-\gamma/2$ is a double root, so $\ddot u = 0$ and $u = C_1 + C_2t$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]] (2) says the same). Initial data: $C_1 = x_0$ and $C_2 - \tfrac\gamma2C_1 = v_0$.
>
> *Completeness.* In each regime the two basic solutions have linearly independent initial vectors, $(1, \lambda_+)$ and $(1, \lambda_-)$, or $(1, -\gamma/2)$ and $(0, 1)$, so by [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]] they span all solutions; the formulas above are the unique solutions with the given data.

^der-b1-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-1|Theorem §B1.2.1]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-5|Theorem §B1.1.5]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-2|Theorem §B1.1.2]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^prop-29-6|451 Prop. §29.6]]

> [!remark] Remark: One formula for all three regimes
> The three solutions are one function of the parameters. With $S(t) = \sin(\omega_dt)/\omega_d$ (underdamped), $S(t) = t$ (critical) or $S(t) = \sinh(\kappa t)/\kappa$ (overdamped), and $C = \dot S$,
>
> $$
> x(t) = e^{-\gamma t/2}\bigl[x_0\,C(t) + (v_0 + \gamma x_0/2)\,S(t)\bigr] .
> $$
>
> Setting $\kappa = i\omega_d$ turns $\sinh(\kappa t)/\kappa$ into $\sin(\omega_dt)/\omega_d$, and both tend to $t$ as $\omega_d$ or $\kappa \to 0$: the motion depends continuously on the damping, and nothing singular happens at $\gamma = 2\omega_0$. The critical solution $t\,e^{-\gamma t/2}$ is the limit of $\bigl(e^{\lambda_+t} - e^{\lambda_-t}\bigr)/(\lambda_+ - \lambda_-)$, a difference quotient in the root itself, as the two roots merge.
>
> *Source: Series I §3.5*

## Return to equilibrium

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.3: Return Without Oscillation; Critical Damping Is Fastest
> 1. For $\gamma \ge 2\omega_0$ a solution that is not identically zero vanishes at most once.
> 2. Every solution decays at least as fast as $(1 + t)\,e^{-rt}$ and a generic one no faster, where the **slowest decay rate** is
>
> $$
> r(\gamma) = \begin{cases} \gamma/2, & \gamma \le 2\omega_0,\\[2pt] \gamma/2 - \kappa = \dfrac{\omega_0^2}{\gamma/2 + \kappa}, & \gamma \ge 2\omega_0 \end{cases}
> $$
>
> (the factor $1 + t$ is needed only at $\gamma = 2\omega_0$). $r$ increases with $\gamma$ up to $r = \omega_0$ at critical damping and decreases after it, with $r \approx \omega_0^2/\gamma = k/b$ for $\gamma \gg 2\omega_0$.
>
> So at fixed $\omega_0$, critical damping gives the fastest eventual return to equilibrium, and it is the only regime that combines this with at most one passage through $x = 0$. This makes precise the statement "as fast as possible without overshoot" of [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^rem-a1-5-1|Remark: The three regimes]].
>
> *Source: Series I §3.4–3.5 (remarks) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1 §4.3*

^thm-b1-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) Overdamped: $x = 0$ requires $C_+e^{\lambda_+t} = -C_-e^{\lambda_-t}$, i.e. $e^{(\lambda_+ - \lambda_-)t} = -C_-/C_+$; the left side is strictly increasing in $t$, so this has at most one solution. Critical: $x = 0$ requires $x_0 + (v_0 + \gamma x_0/2)t = 0$, a linear equation in $t$ with at most one root unless both coefficients vanish, in which case $x \equiv 0$.
>
> (2) Read the rates off [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]: the underdamped envelope decays as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$; the critical solution as $(C_1 + C_2t)e^{-\omega_0t}$; the overdamped one is dominated at late times by the slower exponential $e^{\lambda_+t}$, rate $\lvert\lambda_+\rvert = \gamma/2 - \kappa$, unless $C_+ = 0$. Rationalising, $\gamma/2 - \kappa = (\gamma^2/4 - \kappa^2)/(\gamma/2 + \kappa) = \omega_0^2/(\gamma/2 + \kappa)$, which decreases as $\gamma$ grows (the denominator grows), equals $\omega_0$ at $\gamma = 2\omega_0$, and tends to $\omega_0^2/\gamma$. Below critical damping $r = \gamma/2 < \omega_0$.

^der-b1-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-7|451 Cor. §29.7]]

> [!caution] Caution: "Fastest" means the eventual rate
> The statement compares late-time exponential rates for generic initial data. An underdamped oscillator released from rest reaches $x = 0$ sooner than a critically damped one, but overshoots; an overdamped one started with the special velocity $v_0 = \lambda_-x_0$ (so $C_+ = 0$) decays at the fast rate $\lvert\lambda_-\rvert$. For large damping the slow rate $k/b$ has a simple meaning: inertia is negligible, $b\dot x \approx -kx$, and the mass creeps back as if through syrup.

## Decay per cycle and energy

> [!definition] Definition §B1.2.1: Logarithmic Decrement
> For an underdamped oscillator with damped period $T_d = 2\pi/\omega_d$, the **logarithmic decrement** is
>
> $$
> \Lambda = \ln\frac{x(t)}{x(t + T_d)} ,
> $$
>
> measured in practice between successive maxima on the same side.
>
> *Source: Series I §3.3*

^def-b1-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.4: Logarithmic Decrement and Q
> For the underdamped oscillator $x(t + T_d) = e^{-\gamma T_d/2}\,x(t)$ for every $t$, and successive maxima are $T_d$ apart. Hence, exactly,
>
> $$
> \Lambda = \frac{\gamma T_d}{2} = \frac{\pi\gamma}{\omega_d} = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{Q^2 - \tfrac14}}, \qquad Q = \sqrt{\frac{\pi^2}{\Lambda^2} + \frac14} .
> $$
>
> Level A noted the constant factor $e^{-\gamma T_d/2}$ per cycle ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^rem-a1-5-1|Remark: The three regimes]]); new is its exact relation to $Q$. For $Q \gg 1$, $\Lambda \approx \pi/Q$: in $Q$ cycles the amplitude falls by about $e^{-\pi} \approx 1/23$ ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^rem-a1-5-3|Remark: Reading Q]]).
>
> *Source: Series I §3.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 1 §4.1*

^thm-b1-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $x = Ae^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega_dt + \phi)$ ([[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]]) and the cosine has period $T_d$, so $x(t + T_d) = e^{-\gamma T_d/2}x(t)$. The velocity is $e^{-\gamma t/2}$ times a $T_d$-periodic function too, so its zeros, the extrema of $x$, are spaced by $T_d$ (they are not exactly at the maxima of the cosine). Then $\Lambda = \gamma T_d/2 = \pi\gamma/\omega_d$, and with $\gamma = \omega_0/Q$, $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - 1/4Q^2}$: $\gamma/\omega_d = 1/\sqrt{Q^2 - 1/4}$. Solving for $Q$ gives the inverse.

^der-b1-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^def-b1-2-1|Def. §B1.2.1]], [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B1.2.5: Energy of the Underdamped Oscillator
> For $x = Ae^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega_dt + \phi)$ the mechanical energy $E = \tfrac12m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12kx^2$ is exactly
>
> $$
> E(t) = \tfrac12kA^2\,e^{-\gamma t}\Bigl[1 + \frac{1}{2Q}\sin\bigl(2\omega_dt + 2\phi + \chi\bigr)\Bigr], \qquad \sin\chi = \frac{1}{2Q}, \quad \cos\chi = \frac{\omega_d}{\omega_0} .
> $$
>
> So the energy decays as $e^{-\gamma t}$ with a ripple of relative size $1/2Q$ at $2\omega_d$; $E(t + T_d) = e^{-\gamma T_d}E(t)$ exactly, and the fraction of energy lost per damped period is $1 - e^{-2\Lambda}$, which is $2\pi/Q$ to leading order in $1/Q$.
>
> This makes exact [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^thm-a1-5-3|Theorem §A1.5.3]], which averaged over a cycle.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 10 slides and Problem Set 8 (Problem 3c) · Series I §3.6*

^thm-b1-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\theta = \omega_dt + \phi$: $\dot x = Ae^{-\gamma t/2}\bigl(-\tfrac\gamma2\cos\theta - \omega_d\sin\theta\bigr)$, so
>
> $$
> E = \tfrac12mA^2e^{-\gamma t}\Bigl[\tfrac{\gamma^2}4\cos^2\theta + \gamma\omega_d\sin\theta\cos\theta + \omega_d^2\sin^2\theta + \omega_0^2\cos^2\theta\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Using $\omega_d^2 = \omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4$, the bracket is $\omega_0^2 + \tfrac{\gamma^2}4\cos2\theta + \tfrac{\gamma\omega_d}2\sin2\theta = \omega_0^2\bigl[1 + \tfrac{\gamma}{2\omega_0}\bigl(\tfrac{\omega_d}{\omega_0}\sin2\theta + \tfrac{\gamma}{2\omega_0}\cos2\theta\bigr)\bigr]$. With $\gamma/2\omega_0 = 1/2Q = \sin\chi$ and $\omega_d/\omega_0 = \cos\chi$ (their squares add to one) the inner bracket is $\sin(2\theta + \chi)$, and $m\omega_0^2 = k$. The ripple has period $T_d/2$, so it is the same at $t$ and $t + T_d$, and $E(t + T_d)/E(t) = e^{-\gamma T_d} = e^{-2\Lambda}$ ([[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]]). For $Q \gg 1$, $1 - e^{-2\Lambda} \approx 2\Lambda \approx 2\pi/Q$. Differentiating recovers $\dot E = -b\dot x^2$: the loss rate vanishes twice per cycle, at the turning points, which is the ripple.

^der-b1-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]], [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-4|Theorem §B1.2.4]], [[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^def-a1-5-2|Def. §A1.5.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The electrical twin
> The free series RLC circuit, $L\ddot q + R\dot q + q/C = 0$ ([[§A10.6 RLC Circuits#^thm-a10-6-1|EM Theorem §A10.6.1]]), has the characteristic equation $L\lambda^2 + R\lambda + 1/C = 0$, so everything above carries over with $\gamma = R/L$ and $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$; its three regimes are [[§A10.6 RLC Circuits#^thm-a10-6-2|EM Theorem §A10.6.2]]. The ripple in the energy is the alternation between the capacitor's $q^2/2C$, which the resistor cannot touch at the instant $i = 0$, and the inductor's $\tfrac12Li^2$.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B1.2.1: Drag Without a Spring
> Remove the spring: $\omega_0 = 0$, and $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x = 0$. The characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda) = \lambda(\lambda + \gamma)$ has roots $\lambda_+ = 0$ and $\lambda_- = -\gamma$, and the overdamped formula of [[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-2|Theorem §B1.2.2]] gives
>
> $$
> x(t) = \frac{(v_0 + \gamma x_0)\cdot1 - v_0\,e^{-\gamma t}}{\gamma} = x_0 + \frac{v_0}{\gamma}\bigl(1 - e^{-\gamma t}\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The body coasts to rest at $x_0 + v_0/\gamma$, as for linear drag in mechanics ([[§A3.10 Drag and Terminal Speed|CM §A3.10]]), and does not return: the root $\lambda_+ = 0$ means no restoring force. A weak spring moves this root to $\lambda_+ \approx -\omega_0^2/\gamma = -k/b$ ([[§B1.2 The Damped Oscillator#^thm-b1-2-3|Theorem §B1.2.3]]): the body first coasts on the time scale $1/\gamma = m/b$, then creeps back on the much longer scale $b/k$. (At critical damping, by contrast, both $e^{-\gamma t/2}$ and $t\,e^{-\gamma t/2}$ are needed; $Ae^{-\gamma t/2}$ alone fits only special initial data.)
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 2*

^ex-b1-2-1

> [!example] Example §B1.2.2: When Does a Non-Oscillating Oscillator Overshoot?
> Take $x_0 > 0$. An overdamped oscillator crosses zero at some $t > 0$ exactly when $e^{(\lambda_+ - \lambda_-)t} = (v_0 - \lambda_+x_0)/(v_0 - \lambda_-x_0)$ has a solution greater than $1$. If $v_0 > \lambda_-x_0$ the right side is less than $1$ (its numerator is the smaller); if $v_0 < \lambda_-x_0$ both are negative and the ratio exceeds $1$. So
>
> $$
> \text{overshoot} \iff v_0 < \lambda_-x_0 = -\lvert\lambda_-\rvert\,x_0 ,
> $$
>
> a push toward equilibrium faster than $\lvert\lambda_-\rvert x_0$; at critical damping, $\lambda_- = -\omega_0$, the condition is $v_0 < -\omega_0x_0$ (directly: $x_0 + (v_0 + \omega_0x_0)t$ must change sign).
>
> A critically damped mechanism with $\omega_0 = 4.0\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ starts at $x_0 = 0.10\ \mathrm m$. Released from rest it creeps back without crossing. Pushed with $v_0 = -0.60\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (beyond the threshold $-0.40\ \mathrm{m/s}$):
>
> $$
> x(t) = \bigl[0.10 + (-0.60 + 0.40)\,t\bigr]\,e^{-4.0t}\ \mathrm m = (0.10 - 0.20\,t)\,e^{-4.0t}\ \mathrm m \qquad (t\text{ in s}) .
> $$
>
> It crosses at $t = 0.50\ \mathrm s$; $\dot x = (-0.60 + 0.80\,t)e^{-4.0t}$ vanishes at $t = 0.75\ \mathrm s$, where $x = -0.05\,e^{-3}\ \mathrm m = -2.5\ \mathrm{mm}$, the single overshoot, after which it returns from below.
>
> *Source: Series I §3.4–3.5 (zero crossings; critical initial-value problem) · PHY 287, Problem Set 8 (Problem 3a)*

^ex-b1-2-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Reduction of order ends in $\ddot u = 0$, whose solutions are the linear functions ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^prop-29-6|451 Prop. §29.6]]); the critical solution as a limit of the others is a derivative in the root, computable by L'Hospital's rule ([[§30 L'Hospital's Rule#^thm-30-1|451 Thm. §30.1]]).
> - The roots of $\lambda^2 + \gamma\lambda + \omega_0^2$ are the factorisation of a real quadratic ([[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-15|LADR Thm. 4.15]]); $e^{\lambda t}$ and $te^{\lambda t}$ are linearly independent ([[§4 Span and Linear Independence#^ladr-2-15|LADR Def. 2.15]]).
> - With several degrees of freedom the characteristic equation becomes a determinant condition and the roots become normal-mode frequencies: [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators|§B2.1]].
