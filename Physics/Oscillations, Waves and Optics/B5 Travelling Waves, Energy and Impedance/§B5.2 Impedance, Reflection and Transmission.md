What happens to a wave that reaches the end of its medium, or a place where the medium changes? Level A answered for a fixed and a free end ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-3|Theorem §A2.5.3]]) and for two strings of equal tension carrying a sinusoidal wave ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-4|Theorem §A2.5.4]]), and noticed in passing that the combination $\sqrt{\mu F_T}$ governs the energy balance ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^rem-a2-5-3|Remark: Reading the junction formulas]]). This section makes that combination the central concept. Impedance, force over velocity, is defined for lumped elements and for a continuous medium; a travelling wave turns out to be a medium "seen" through its characteristic impedance; and the reflection and transmission of a pulse of any shape, at any junction or termination, depend only on the impedances on the two sides. Strings, rods, sound in air and water, colliding blocks and electrical circuits then obey the same formulas.

## Impedance

> [!definition] Definition §B5.2.1: Mechanical Impedance
> Let a force $F(t) = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat F e^{i\omega t}\bigr]$ act on a linear mechanical system at some point, which moves in the steady state with velocity $u(t) = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat u\,e^{i\omega t}\bigr]$ along the force. The **impedance** of the system at that point is
>
> $$
> Z(\omega) = \frac{\hat F}{\hat u}, \qquad Z = R + iX ,
> $$
>
> with **resistance** $R = \operatorname{Re}Z$ and **reactance** $X = \operatorname{Im}Z$; its unit is $\mathrm{N\,s/m} = \mathrm{kg/s}$. It tells how much force it takes to impose a given velocity: a large impedance is a system that is hard to set moving. When $Z$ does not depend on $\omega$ and is real, $F = Zu$ holds for any time dependence.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §2 and §5*

^def-b5-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.1: Impedance of Lumped Elements
> With the convention $e^{i\omega t}$:
>
> $$
> Z_{\text{dashpot}} = b, \qquad Z_{\text{mass}} = i\omega m, \qquad Z_{\text{spring}} = \frac{k}{i\omega} = -\frac{ik}{\omega} .
> $$
>
> Elements attached to one moving point share its velocity, and their impedances add. A mass on a spring with a dashpot, driven at the mass, therefore has
>
> $$
> Z(\omega) = b + i\Bigl(\omega m - \frac{k}{\omega}\Bigr), \qquad \hat u = \frac{\hat F}{Z(\omega)} .
> $$
>
> At low frequency the spring dominates (stiffness-dominated, $X < 0$), at high frequency the mass (mass-dominated, $X > 0$); the reactance vanishes at $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$, where only the resistance $b$ limits the motion. This is the velocity resonance, which peaks exactly at $\omega_0$ ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^cau-a1-6-2|Caution: Which resonance frequency]]; [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power|§B1.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §5*

^thm-b5-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each element obeys a linear law between the applied force and its motion; with $u = \operatorname{Re}[\hat ue^{i\omega t}]$ a time derivative multiplies the complex amplitude by $i\omega$ and a time integral divides by it ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]). Dashpot: $F = bu$, so $\hat F = b\hat u$. Mass: $F = m\,du/dt$, so $\hat F = i\omega m\,\hat u$. Spring: $F = k\xi$ with $u = d\xi/dt$, so $\hat\xi = \hat u/(i\omega)$ and $\hat F = (k/i\omega)\hat u$. For the oscillator, the driving force must supply all three: $F = m\dot u + bu + k\xi$ (the equation of [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]]), so $\hat F = (i\omega m + b + k/i\omega)\hat u$. The modulus $|\hat u| = |\hat F|/\sqrt{b^2 + (\omega m - k/\omega)^2}$ is largest where $\omega m = k/\omega$.

*Uses:* [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^def-b5-2-1|Def. §B5.2.1]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]], [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The electrical analogue
> A series RLC circuit obeys $V = L\ddot Q + R\dot Q + Q/C$, the oscillator equation with $V \leftrightarrow F$, $I = \dot Q \leftrightarrow u$, $L \leftrightarrow m$, $R \leftrightarrow b$, $1/C \leftrightarrow k$. The impedance $Z = \hat V/\hat I$ of a resistor, inductor and capacitor is $R$, $i\omega L$ and $1/(i\omega C)$, and in series $Z = R + i(\omega L - 1/\omega C)$, whose modulus is the impedance of [[§A11.2 RLC Series Circuits with AC#^def-a11-2-2|EM Def. §A11.2.2]]. Complex impedances combine in series and parallel like resistances; in the mechanical picture, elements sharing one velocity are "in series" (their impedances add), elements sharing one force (a spring and a dashpot connected end to end) are "in parallel" (their inverses add). Matching impedances is a daily concern of electrical engineering: a transmitter delivers the most power to an antenna matched to it, and power reflected from a mismatched antenna can damage the amplifier.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §6*

^rem-b5-2-1

## Characteristic impedance

> [!definition] Definition §B5.2.2: Characteristic Impedance
> The **characteristic impedance** of a linear one-dimensional continuum with stiffness $K$, inertia $\mu$ and wave speed $c = \sqrt{K/\mu}$ ([[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^mod-b5-1-1|Model §B5.1.1]]) is
>
> $$
> Z_0 = \sqrt{K\mu} = \mu c = \frac{K}{c} .
> $$
>
> For a string $Z_0 = \sqrt{T\mu} = T/c$; for a rod or a fluid column of cross-section $S$, $Z_0 = \rho cS$. The impedance per unit area of a fluid or solid, $z = \rho c$, is its **specific acoustic impedance** ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^rem-a3-2-1|Remark: Same structure as the string, and the role of ρv]]): about $420\ \mathrm{kg/(m^2\,s)}$ for air, $1.48 \times 10^6$ for water, $4.8 \times 10^7$ for steel.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §2 and §7, Lecture 10 §1*

^def-b5-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.2: Force and Velocity in a Travelling Wave
> In a linear one-dimensional continuum, a wave moving toward $+x$ has $F = Z_0u$ and a wave moving toward $-x$ has $F = -Z_0u$, at every point and instant and for any shape. For a general solution, split into right- and left-moving parts $u = u_+ + u_-$ ([[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution|§B3.3]]),
>
> $$
> F = Z_0\,(u_+ - u_-) .
> $$
>
> Consequently a semi-infinite medium driven at its end, with no wave coming back, presents to the driver the real, frequency-independent impedance $Z_0$: the driver feels exactly the drag of a dashpot with $b = Z_0$, although nothing in the medium dissipates energy. The energy is carried away by the wave ([[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves|§B5.3]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §2 and Lecture 10 §2*

^thm-b5-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $\xi = f(x - ct)$: $u = \partial_t\xi = -cf'$ and $F = -K\,\partial_x\xi = -Kf' = (K/c)\,u = Z_0u$. For $\xi = g(x + ct)$: $u = cg'$ and $F = -Kg' = -Z_0u$. A general solution is $f(x - ct) + g(x + ct)$ ([[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution|§B3.3]]); add the two results. A driver at the left end $x = 0$ of a medium on $x > 0$ plays the role of "the medium to the left" in [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^def-b5-1-1|Def. §B5.1.1]]: it exerts $F(0,t)$. With only the outgoing wave present, $F(0,t) = Z_0u(0,t)$, and by Newton's third law the medium pushes back on the driver with $-Z_0u$, the law of a dashpot.

## Reflection and transmission

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.3: Reflection and Transmission at a Junction
> Two linear continua with impedances $Z_1$ (on $x < 0$, speed $c_1$) and $Z_2$ (on $x > 0$, speed $c_2$) are joined at $x = 0$ as in [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]. An incident wave $\xi_i(t - x/c_1)$, of any shape, produces a reflected and a transmitted wave of the **same shape**,
>
> $$
> \xi = \xi_i\Bigl(t - \frac{x}{c_1}\Bigr) + r\,\xi_i\Bigl(t + \frac{x}{c_1}\Bigr) \ \ (x < 0), \qquad \xi = t_{12}\,\xi_i\Bigl(t - \frac{x}{c_2}\Bigr) \ \ (x > 0),
> $$
>
> with the displacement (and velocity) coefficients
>
> $$
> r = \frac{Z_1 - Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2}, \qquad t_{12} = \frac{2Z_1}{Z_1 + Z_2} = 1 + r .
> $$
>
> The internal force (or pressure) is reflected with $-r = (Z_2 - Z_1)/(Z_1 + Z_2)$ and transmitted with $2Z_2/(Z_1 + Z_2)$. Only the ratio $Z_2/Z_1$ matters. $Z_2 \to \infty$ is a fixed end ($r = -1$), $Z_2 \to 0$ a free end ($r = 1$, the end moving with twice the incident amplitude), and $Z_2 = Z_1$ gives no reflection even if the two media differ in $K$ and $\mu$ separately.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §2–3 · PHY 287, week 13 slides (waves at the boundary)*

^thm-b5-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Writing the waves as functions of $t \mp x/c$ instead of $x \mp ct$ (the same information, rescaled) makes them agree at $x = 0$. On the left the solution is $f(t - x/c_1) + g(t + x/c_1)$, with $f = \xi_i$ given and $g$ unknown; on the right it is $h(t - x/c_2)$ only, because before the incident wave arrives the right side is at rest, so no wave comes in from $+\infty$. The junction conditions of [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]] at $x = 0$:
> - continuity of $\xi$: $f(t) + g(t) = h(t)$;
> - continuity of $F = -K\,\partial_x\xi$: on the left $\partial_x\xi = (-f' + g')/c_1$, so $F = (K_1/c_1)(f' - g') = Z_1(f' - g')$; on the right $F = Z_2h'$. Hence $Z_1(f' - g') = Z_2h'$.
>
> A function with zero derivative is constant ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]), so $Z_1(f - g) = Z_2h + C$; before the wave arrives all three vanish, so $C = 0$. Substituting $h = f + g$: $(Z_1 + Z_2)\,g = (Z_1 - Z_2)\,f$ and $h = f + g = \dfrac{2Z_1}{Z_1 + Z_2}\,f$. The force in each outgoing wave is $\pm Z$ times its velocity ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]): $F_r = -Z_1\,r\,u_i = -r\,F_i$ and $F_t = Z_2\,t_{12}\,u_i = (2Z_2/(Z_1 + Z_2))\,F_i$.
>
> For two strings of equal tension, $Z = \sqrt{T\mu} \propto \sqrt\mu$, and $r$, $t_{12}$ become the coefficients of [[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-4|Theorem §A2.5.4]]; the present derivation needs neither equal tensions nor a sinusoidal wave.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.4: Power Reflection and Transmission
> At the junction of [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]] the fractions of the incident power that are reflected and transmitted are
>
> $$
> \mathcal R = r^2 = \Bigl(\frac{Z_1 - Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2}\Bigr)^2, \qquad \mathcal T = \frac{Z_2}{Z_1}\,t_{12}^2 = \frac{4Z_1Z_2}{(Z_1 + Z_2)^2}, \qquad \mathcal R + \mathcal T = 1 ,
> $$
>
> at every instant, not only on average. Both are symmetric under $Z_1 \leftrightarrow Z_2$: a junction passes the same fraction of power in either direction.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10 §2 · PHY 287, week 13 slides (waves at the boundary)*

^thm-b5-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The power carried across a point is force times velocity, $P = Fu$ ([[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]]), here the work per unit time done by the medium on the left on the medium on the right. For a single travelling wave $F = \pm Z u$ ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]), so it carries $Zu^2$ in its direction of motion. At $x = 0$ the incident, reflected and transmitted velocities are $u_i$, $ru_i$ and $t_{12}u_i$, so
>
> $$
> \frac{P_r}{P_i} = \frac{Z_1r^2u_i^2}{Z_1u_i^2} = r^2, \qquad \frac{P_t}{P_i} = \frac{Z_2t_{12}^2}{Z_1} = \frac{4Z_1Z_2}{(Z_1 + Z_2)^2},
> $$
>
> and $(Z_1 - Z_2)^2 + 4Z_1Z_2 = (Z_1 + Z_2)^2$ gives $\mathcal R + \mathcal T = 1$. The junction has no mass and no friction, so it can neither store nor absorb energy; the balance holds instant by instant.

> [!caution] Caution: Which amplitude is reflected with which sign
> - $r$ and $t_{12}$ refer to displacement and velocity. The force (for sound, the pressure) has reflection coefficient $-r$: a wave in air meeting water is reflected with its displacement inverted and its pressure upright, and the pressure at the surface nearly doubles.
> - $t_{12} > 1$ when the wave passes into a medium of **lower** impedance ($Z_2 < Z_1$), and $t_{12} < 1$ into a higher one. A larger amplitude does not mean more power: the softer medium needs less power for the same motion, and $\mathcal T \le 1$ always.
> - Level A states the junction rule in terms of wave speeds ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-4|Theorem §A2.5.4]]). That form holds only for equal tensions; with different tensions the impedance, not the speed, decides. A heavy string under high tension can match a light one under low tension: Schwartz's choice $T_2 = (\mu_1/\mu_2)\,T_1$ gives $Z_2 = Z_1$.

^cau-b5-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Pipes
> In a pipe whose cross-section jumps from $S_1$ to $S_2$, the conditions at the step are continuity of the **pressure** and of the **volume flow** $U = Su$, not of the force $S\,\Delta p$, which jumps (the step itself takes up the difference). The pair that matches is then $(\Delta p, U)$, whose product is still the power, and the relevant impedance is the **acoustic impedance** $Z_A = \Delta p/U = \rho c/S$. With $Z_A$ in place of $Z$ the formulas of [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]] hold for $U$, giving
>
> $$
> r_U = \frac{1/S_1 - 1/S_2}{1/S_1 + 1/S_2} = \frac{S_2 - S_1}{S_1 + S_2} .
> $$
>
> This is the opposite sign to a rod of changing cross-section, where $\xi$ and $F$ are continuous and $Z = \rho cS$. Both are consistent with the end conditions: a pipe closed by a wall ($S_2 \to 0$) has $r_U = -1$, a fixed end; a pipe opening into free air ($S_2 \to \infty$) has $r_U = 1$, a free end; a rod that simply stops ($S_2 \to 0$) has a free end. The description assumes plane waves, wavelengths large compared with the width of the pipe.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §7*

^cau-b5-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.2.5: Reflection from a Terminating Load
> A linear continuum of impedance $Z_0$ on $x < 0$ ends at $x = 0$ on a load of impedance $Z_L(\omega)$ ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^def-b5-2-1|Def. §B5.2.1]]). A sinusoidal incident wave of angular frequency $\omega$ is reflected with the complex coefficient (for velocity and displacement)
>
> $$
> r(\omega) = \frac{Z_0 - Z_L(\omega)}{Z_0 + Z_L(\omega)} .
> $$
>
> $Z_L = \infty$ is a fixed end, $Z_L = 0$ a free end, a second semi-infinite medium is the load $Z_L = Z_2$ ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]]), and $Z_L = Z_0$ reflects nothing. A purely reactive load ($Z_L = iX$) reflects totally, $|r| = 1$, with a frequency-dependent phase shift; a load with $\operatorname{Re}Z_L > 0$ absorbs power and has $|r| < 1$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §2 and §5 (combined here)*

^thm-b5-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> On $x < 0$ write the velocity as $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat u_ie^{i\omega(t - x/c)} + \hat u_re^{i\omega(t + x/c)}\bigr]$. By [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]] the force is $\operatorname{Re}\bigl[Z_0\hat u_ie^{i\omega(t - x/c)} - Z_0\hat u_re^{i\omega(t + x/c)}\bigr]$. At $x = 0$ the medium exerts $F(0,t)$ on the load, which moves with $u(0,t)$, and by the definition of $Z_L$ the two complex amplitudes are in the ratio $Z_L$:
>
> $$
> Z_0(\hat u_i - \hat u_r) = Z_L(\hat u_i + \hat u_r) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\hat u_r}{\hat u_i} = \frac{Z_0 - Z_L}{Z_0 + Z_L} .
> $$
>
> Displacements are $\hat u/(i\omega)$, with the same ratio. If $Z_L = iX$, numerator and denominator are $Z_0 \mp iX$, complex conjugates of equal modulus, so $|r| = 1$. In general $|Z_0 + Z_L|^2 - |Z_0 - Z_L|^2 = 4Z_0\operatorname{Re}Z_L$, so $|r| < 1$ exactly when $\operatorname{Re}Z_L > 0$.

> [!model] Model §B5.2.6: Impedance-Matched Termination
> A finite medium of real characteristic impedance $Z_0$ ends on a dashpot with $b = Z_0$, attached through a massless end, with no other force on the end. By [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-5|Theorem §B5.2.5]], $r = 0$ at every frequency, so a wave of any shape is absorbed without reflection: to the waves the medium appears to continue to infinity ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]). It idealises an absorbing end: a string run into a damping medium, an anechoic wedge in a sound lab, a transmission line terminated in its characteristic resistance. Real terminations are matched only over a band of frequencies.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §2 and §5 (combined here)*

^mod-b5-2-6

## Impedance matching

> [!remark] Remark: Getting energy across a mismatch
> When $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ differ by a large factor, almost all the power is reflected ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]]); it does not matter which is larger. Three ways around it, all in Schwartz:
> - **Choose the parameters**, as with the tensions of two strings ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^cau-b5-2-1|Caution: Which amplitude]]).
> - **Insert an intermediate stage** of intermediate impedance ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^ex-b5-2-3|Example §B5.2.3]]): two moderate mismatches lose less than one large one.
> - **Change the impedance gradually**. A megaphone widens slowly from the mouth to a large opening, so the acoustic impedance $\rho c/S$ ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^cau-b5-2-2|Caution: Pipes]]) falls in many small steps instead of one jump.
>
> A loudspeaker cone and the air it drives are badly mismatched, which is one reason a typical speaker turns only about $10^{-5}$ of its electrical power into sound ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^ex-a3-2-2|Example §A3.2.2]]). Mismatch can also amplify motion: an earthquake wave passing from rock ($z \approx 16\ \mathrm{MPa\,s/m}$) into loose fill ($0.15\ \mathrm{MPa\,s/m}$) has $t_{12} = 2(16)/(16.15) = 1.98$, nearly doubling the ground's displacement, even though only $\mathcal T = 3.7\%$ of the power enters the fill. This is why building on landfill in an earthquake zone is unwise.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §2, §4, §7 and Lecture 10 §3*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B5.2.1: Shouting at Someone under Water
> Sound in air ($z_1 = \rho c = 420\ \mathrm{kg/(m^2\,s)}$) meets the surface of water ($z_2 = 1.48 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{kg/(m^2\,s)}$) at normal incidence. Per unit area the junction is that of [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]]:
>
> $$
> r = \frac{z_1 - z_2}{z_1 + z_2} = -0.99943, \qquad t_{12} = \frac{2z_1}{z_1 + z_2} = 5.7 \times 10^{-4}, \qquad \text{pressure: } -r = 0.99943,\ \ \frac{2z_2}{z_1 + z_2} = 1.99943 .
> $$
>
> The water surface barely moves (its velocity amplitude is $5.7 \times 10^{-4}$ of that in the incident wave), while the pressure just below the surface is twice the incident pressure amplitude. The transmitted power fraction is
>
> $$
> \mathcal T = \frac{4z_1z_2}{(z_1 + z_2)^2} = 1.13 \times 10^{-3}, \qquad 10\log_{10}\mathcal T = -29.5\ \mathrm{dB}
> $$
>
> ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]): an $80\ \mathrm{dB}$ shout arrives in the water at about $50\ \mathrm{dB}$ (on the intensity scale of the water side). The same factor applies to sound going the other way, from water into air.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §7*

^ex-b5-2-1

> [!example] Example §B5.2.2: A String Ending on a Mass
> A string ($T = 10\ \mathrm N$, $\mu = 0.020\ \mathrm{kg/m}$, so $Z_0 = \sqrt{T\mu} = 0.447\ \mathrm{kg/s}$) ends on a ring of mass $M = 10\ \mathrm g$ that slides without friction on a vertical rod. The ring is a load $Z_L = i\omega M$ (end condition 3 of [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]]), so by [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-5|Theorem §B5.2.5]]
>
> $$
> r(\omega) = \frac{Z_0 - i\omega M}{Z_0 + i\omega M} = e^{-2i\varphi}, \qquad \tan\varphi = \frac{\omega M}{Z_0} .
> $$
>
> All the energy comes back ($|r| = 1$), but the reflected wave lags by $2\varphi$. At low frequency ($\omega M \ll Z_0$) the ring is too light to matter and the end is free ($r \to 1$); at high frequency it is too heavy to move and the end is fixed ($r \to -1$). The crossover is at $\omega = Z_0/M = 44.7\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ ($7.1\ \mathrm{Hz}$), where $r = -i$, a quarter-period lag. Unlike a junction of two strings, this load reflects different frequencies with different phases, so a pulse comes back distorted: its sharp, high-frequency features inverted, its slow parts upright.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §2 and §5 (combined here); string data from PHY 300, Midterm 2 practice*

> [!example] Example §B5.2.3: Impedance Matching with a Middle Block
> A block $m$ moving at $v$ collides elastically with a block $M$ at rest. By [[§A5.3 Collisions in One and Two Dimensions#^thm-a5-3-2|CM Theorem §A5.3.2]],
>
> $$
> v_m = \frac{m - M}{m + M}\,v, \qquad v_M = \frac{2m}{m + M}\,v ,
> $$
>
> exactly $r$ and $t_{12}$ of [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-3|Theorem §B5.2.3]] with $Z_1 = m$, $Z_2 = M$: for an impulsive push the mass plays the role of the impedance (the heavier the block, the less velocity a given push imparts). The fraction of the kinetic energy handed on is $4mM/(m + M)^2 = \mathcal T$. For $m = 1$, $M = 3$ (any unit): $v_M = v/2$, and $75\%$ of the energy is transferred.
>
> Put a block $m_2$ between them. Two transfers give $\mathcal T(m, m_2)\,\mathcal T(m_2, M) = \dfrac{16\,m\,m_2^2\,M}{(m + m_2)^2(m_2 + M)^2}$. Its logarithmic derivative in $m_2$ vanishes where $(m + m_2)(m_2 + M) = m_2(2m_2 + m + M)$, i.e. at the **geometric mean** $m_2 = \sqrt{mM}$, where both steps have the same ratio $\sqrt{M/m}$. With $m_2 = 2$ the middle block leaves at $\tfrac{2m}{m + m_2}\,v = \tfrac23v$ and hands on $v_M = \tfrac{2m_2}{m_2 + M}\cdot\tfrac23v = \tfrac{8}{15}v = 0.533\,v$, so $3(8/15)^2 = 85.3\%$ of the energy arrives; with $m_2 = \sqrt3$: $v_M = 0.536\,v$ and $86.2\%$. More blocks in geometric progression bring the transfer as close to $100\%$ as desired, the discrete version of a gradual taper.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9 §4*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - Impedance and the complex response of the driven oscillator ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator|§B1.3]]) carry the same information: the displacement per unit force is $\hat\xi/\hat F = 1/(i\omega Z) = 1/(k - m\omega^2 + i\omega b)$.
> - The step "zero derivative implies constant" ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]) fixes the shapes of the reflected and transmitted waves; the conditions at $x = 0$ of [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]] are the only physics input.
> - For light at normal incidence the same algebra with $Z$ replaced by the refractive index $n$ gives the amplitude reflection coefficient $(n_1 - n_2)/(n_1 + n_2)$ of the electric field, negative on reflection from a denser medium: the phase change of [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference#^law-a6-3-1|Law §A6.3.1]]. Derivation from Maxwell's equations: Chapter B9 (planned).
