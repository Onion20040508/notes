Level A derived a wave equation three times, each time from Newton's second law on a small piece of the medium: for a stretched string ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]), for a fluid ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]]) and for a thin rod ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-3|Theorem §A3.1.3]]); [[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation|§B3.2]] derived it once more as the limit of a bead chain. This section shows that the four derivations are one: each medium is described by a displacement field and an internal force tied to it by a linear stiffness law, and Newton's law plus that law give the wave equation as a pair of first-order equations for the force and the velocity. The same pair fixes what happens at an end or at a junction, and it is the starting point of impedance ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission|§B5.2]]) and of energy transport ([[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves|§B5.3]]).

## One description for three media

> [!definition] Definition §B5.1.1: Displacement Field, Velocity Field, Internal Force
> Label each element of a one-dimensional medium by its equilibrium position $x$. Its **displacement** $\xi(x,t)$ is measured from equilibrium, along $x$ for a longitudinal wave and perpendicular to it for a transverse wave; its **velocity** is $u = \partial\xi/\partial t$. The **internal force** $F(x,t)$ is the component, along the displacement, of the force that the medium to the left of a cut at $x$ exerts on the medium to the right of it, minus its value at equilibrium. By Newton's third law the right part exerts $-F(x,t)$ on the left part.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lectures 6 and 9*

^def-b5-1-1

> [!model] Model §B5.1.1: Linear One-Dimensional Continuum
> A **linear one-dimensional continuum** is a medium described by $\xi(x,t)$ ([[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^def-b5-1-1|Def. §B5.1.1]]) with
> - **inertia** $\mu$, the mass per unit length, uniform;
> - a **linear stiffness law**: the internal force is proportional to the local deformation,
>
> $$
> F(x,t) = -K\,\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}(x,t),
> $$
>
>   with a constant **stiffness** $K > 0$ (a force);
> - small displacements and deformations ($|\partial\xi/\partial x| \ll 1$), no dissipation, no external forces along $\xi$.
>
> The four standard media are instances:
>
> | medium | $\xi$ | $K$ | $\mu$ | level-A home |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | stretched string, tension $T$ | transverse $y$ | $T$ | $\mu$ | [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^mod-a2-3-1\|Model §A2.3.1]] |
> | thin rod, Young's modulus $Y$, cross-section $S$ | longitudinal | $YS$ | $\rho S$ | [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-3\|Theorem §A3.1.3]] |
> | fluid in a tube of cross-section $S$, bulk modulus $B$ | longitudinal $s$ | $BS$ | $\rho S$ | [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2\|Theorem §A3.1.2]] |
> | spring chain, springs $k$, masses $m$, spacing $a$, for wavelengths $\gg a$ | longitudinal | $ka$ | $m/a$ | [[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation\|§B3.2]] |
>
> For a gas, $B$ is the adiabatic bulk modulus $\gamma p$ ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^mod-a3-1-4|Model §A3.1.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4 §5 and Lecture 6 §2–3 · Series I §9.1–9.2*

^mod-b5-1-1

![[ph-wo-b5-1-1.svg]]
*The internal force at a cut. Left: on a string, the left part pulls the right part along the string; for small slopes the transverse component is $F = -T\,\partial y/\partial x$. The element between $x$ and $x + \Delta x$ feels $F(x)$ at its left end and $-F(x + \Delta x)$ at its right end; here both point down, and the element, near a crest, accelerates down. Right: in a rod or a gas column the element feels $F(x)$ on its left face and $-F(x + \Delta x)$ on its right face; $F > 0$ is a push (compression), $F < 0$ a pull (tension).*

> [!remark] Remark: Where each stiffness law comes from
> - **String.** The tension is along the tangent; its transverse component is $-T\sin\theta \approx -T\,\partial y/\partial x$ ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^der-a2-3-2|derivation of Theorem §A2.3.2]]). The stiffness is the *pre-tension* $T$, not an elastic modulus: a slack string carries no transverse waves.
> - **Rod.** A stretched slab has strain $\partial\xi/\partial x$ and carries the tension $YS\,\partial\xi/\partial x$ ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-2|CM Def. §A7.2.2]]); the left part pulls the right part back with $F = -YS\,\partial\xi/\partial x$.
> - **Fluid.** The fractional volume change is $\partial s/\partial x$, so the excess pressure is $\Delta p = -B\,\partial s/\partial x$ ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]]) and the left fluid pushes the right fluid with $F = S\,\Delta p$.
> - **Spring chain.** Between neighbours the spring force is $-k(\xi_{j+1} - \xi_j) \approx -ka\,\partial\xi/\partial x$. Schwartz calls $ka$ an "elastic modulus"; it is a force, the $YS$ of a rod made of such springs, since a rod segment of length $a$ is a spring of constant $YS/a$ ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^rem-a7-2-2|CM Remark: A rod is a spring]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4 §5*

## The wave equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.2: Wave Equation of a Linear Continuum
> In a linear one-dimensional continuum ([[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^mod-b5-1-1|Model §B5.1.1]]) the velocity and the internal force obey the first-order pair
>
> $$
> \mu\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}, \qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial t} = -K\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} ,
> $$
>
> and the displacement, the velocity and the force each obey the wave equation ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-4|Def. §A2.2.4]])
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2\xi}{\partial t^2} = c^2\,\frac{\partial^2\xi}{\partial x^2}, \qquad c = \sqrt{\frac{K}{\mu}} .
> $$
>
> With the table of [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^mod-b5-1-1|Model §B5.1.1]], $c = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ for a string, $\sqrt{Y/\rho}$ for a rod, $\sqrt{B/\rho}$ for a fluid and $a\sqrt{k/m}$ for a spring chain. (Level A writes the tension $F_T$ and the speed $v$; here $T$ and $c$, as in the series.)
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4 §5 and Lecture 6 · Series I §9.2*

^thm-b5-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Newton's law.* The element between $x$ and $x + \Delta x$ has mass $\mu\,\Delta x$. The medium on its left exerts $F(x,t)$ on it; the medium on its right exerts $-F(x + \Delta x, t)$, by Newton's third law applied at the cut $x + \Delta x$. So
>
> $$
> \mu\,\Delta x\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = F(x,t) - F(x + \Delta x,t) .
> $$
>
> Divide by $\Delta x$ and let $\Delta x \to 0$: $\mu\,\partial_t u = -\partial_x F$.
>
> *Stiffness law.* Differentiate $F = -K\,\partial_x\xi$ with respect to $t$ and exchange the order of the derivatives (for twice continuously differentiable $\xi$): $\partial_t F = -K\,\partial_x\partial_t\xi = -K\,\partial_x u$.
>
> *Elimination.* Put $F = -K\,\partial_x\xi$ and $u = \partial_t\xi$ into Newton's law: $\mu\,\partial_t^2\xi = K\,\partial_x^2\xi$, the wave equation with $c^2 = K/\mu$. Differentiating the first equation of the pair by $t$ and the second by $x$ and eliminating $\partial_x\partial_t F$ gives $\mu\,\partial_t^2u = K\,\partial_x^2u$; the other way round, $\mu\,\partial_t^2F = K\,\partial_x^2F$.
>
> For the spring chain the stiffness law holds only for wavelengths long compared with $a$; the exact chain equation is the difference equation of [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain|§B3.1]], and the wave equation is its long-wavelength limit ([[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation|§B3.2]]).

> [!remark] Remark: What is new compared with level A
> The level-A results [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]], [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]] and [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-3|Theorem §A3.1.3]] are this one theorem read through three rows of the table; "speed = $\sqrt{\text{elastic}/\text{inertial}}$" ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^rem-a3-1-2|Remark: Stiff and light means fast]]) becomes the precise statement $c^2 = K/\mu$, with $K$ and $\mu$ both per unit length, so that the cross-section cancels for rods and fluids. The first-order form is new and is what the rest of the chapter uses: it pairs a **force** with a **velocity**, whose ratio is the impedance ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission|§B5.2]]) and whose product is the power ([[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves|§B5.3]]). Every solution is a sum of a right-moving and a left-moving shape ([[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution|§B3.3]]), whatever the medium.

^rem-b5-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Which bulk modulus for a gas
> Schwartz obtains the speed of sound by dividing the string equation by the area: "tension per area" becomes the pressure $p$, giving $c = \sqrt{p/\rho}$. That identification is a heuristic. The stiffness of a gas column is $BS$, and for an ideal gas the bulk modulus happens to equal $p$ only at constant temperature; $\sqrt{p/\rho}$ is the isothermal speed, about 15% too low for air ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^rem-a3-1-3|Remark: The isothermal value]]). Sound is adiabatic, $B = \gamma p$ ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-5|Theorem §A3.1.5]]), with $\gamma = (f + 2)/f$ for $f$ active degrees of freedom ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-3|TH Theorem §A3.5.3]]): $f = 3$ for a monatomic gas and $f = 5$ (three translations, two rotations) for N$_2$ and O$_2$ at room temperature.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 6 §3.1*

## Ends and junctions

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.1.3: Conditions at Ends and Junctions
> For a linear one-dimensional continuum ([[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^mod-b5-1-1|Model §B5.1.1]]) ending at $x = L$:
> 1. **Fixed end** (held in place): $\xi(L,t) = 0$, equivalently $u(L,t) = 0$.
> 2. **Free end** (no external force along $\xi$ on a massless end): $F(L,t) = 0$, equivalently $\partial\xi/\partial x\,(L,t) = 0$.
> 3. **Loaded end** (attached to a body of mass $M$, displacement $\xi(L,t)$, acted on by external forces $F_{\text{ext}}$ along $\xi$):
>
> $$
> M\,\frac{\partial^2\xi}{\partial t^2}(L,t) = F(L,t) + F_{\text{ext}}(t) .
> $$
>
> Two media joined at $x = 0$ (string knotted to string, rod bonded to rod), with stiffnesses $K_1$, $K_2$ and no mass concentrated at the joint, satisfy
>
> $$
> \xi(0^-,t) = \xi(0^+,t), \qquad F(0^-,t) = F(0^+,t), \quad\text{i.e.}\quad K_1\,\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}(0^-,t) = K_2\,\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}(0^+,t) .
> $$
>
> The displacement is continuous; the slope jumps unless $K_1 = K_2$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 6 §4 and Lecture 9 §1*

^thm-b5-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Loaded end.* The body at $x = L$ feels $F(L,t)$ from the medium on its left and $F_{\text{ext}}$ from outside; Newton's second law gives condition 3. *Free end.* With $M = 0$ and $F_{\text{ext}} = 0$, condition 3 leaves $F(L,t) = 0$, and $K \neq 0$ gives $\partial_x\xi = 0$. Equivalently, apply Newton's law to the last element of length $\Delta x$, which feels only $F(L - \Delta x)$: $\mu\,\Delta x\,\partial_t^2\xi = F(L - \Delta x, t)$; as $\Delta x \to 0$ the left side vanishes, so $F(L,t) = 0$. *Fixed end:* the definition.
>
> *Junction.* Continuity of $\xi$: the medium does not tear. For the force, take the element from $-\Delta x$ to $\Delta x$, of mass $(\mu_1 + \mu_2)\Delta x$:
>
> $$
> (\mu_1 + \mu_2)\,\Delta x\,\frac{\partial^2\xi}{\partial t^2} = F(-\Delta x, t) - F(\Delta x, t) .
> $$
>
> The forces stay finite as $\Delta x \to 0$ while the mass goes to zero, so the right side must tend to zero: $F(0^-) = F(0^+)$. (Otherwise a finite force would accelerate a vanishing mass without bound, Schwartz's argument.) With $F = -K_j\,\partial_x\xi$ on each side this is the stated slope condition.

^der-b5-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^def-b5-1-1|Def. §B5.1.1]], [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^mod-b5-1-1|Model §B5.1.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|CM Principle §A3.5.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The conditions in each medium
> - **String.** Fixed end: tied to a wall. Free end: a massless ring sliding on a frictionless rod ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-3|Theorem §A2.5.3]]). Junction with different tensions: Schwartz's ring on a rod, which takes up the difference of the longitudinal tension components while transmitting the transverse ones; then $T_1\,\partial_xy(0^-) = T_2\,\partial_xy(0^+)$. Level A treated equal tensions only ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-4|Theorem §A2.5.4]]), where the slope is continuous.
> - **Rod.** A clamped end is fixed; an end in air is free, since no stress acts on it ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^rem-a3-3-5|Remark: Longitudinal standing waves in a rod]]).
> - **Air column.** A closed end is fixed ($s = 0$); an open end is free to first approximation, because the atmosphere holds $\Delta p = 0$, so $F = S\,\Delta p = 0$ ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^mod-a3-3-1|Model §A3.3.1]]). A change of cross-section in a pipe is not a junction of the type above, since the force $S\,\Delta p$ jumps there while the pressure stays continuous: [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^cau-b5-2-2|Caution: Pipes]].
>
> Fixed and free ends are the Dirichlet and Neumann conditions of Schwartz. Two fixed or two free ends give the frequencies $\omega_n = n\pi c/L$; one of each gives $\omega_n = (n - \tfrac12)\pi c/L$, odd harmonics only ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^rem-a2-6-2|Remark: Other boundary conditions]], [[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]). Two free ends also allow $n = 0$, the rigid translation $\xi = \text{const}$ (or uniform motion $\xi = c_0 + c_1t$), which is not a vibration. The modes themselves are worked out for the string in [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String|§B4.2]]; by [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]] they carry over to every row of the table.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 6 §4 and Lecture 9 §1 · PHY 300, Worksheet 6*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B5.1.1: Two Waves on One Steel String
> The high E string of a guitar is steel, with $\mu = 3.09 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{kg/m}$ under $T = 56.4\ \mathrm N$ (the data of [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^ex-a2-3-1|Example §A2.3.1]]). It carries transverse waves (row 1 of the table) and longitudinal waves (row 2), with the same $\mu$ but different stiffnesses. Take $Y = 2.0 \times 10^{11}\ \mathrm{Pa}$ and $\rho = 7860\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$ ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^rem-a7-2-1|CM Remark: Some values]], [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^rem-a9-1-1|CM Remark: Typical densities]]). The cross-section is $S = \mu/\rho = 3.93 \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ (diameter $0.22\ \mathrm{mm}$), so
>
> $$
> K_{\text{trans}} = T = 56.4\ \mathrm N, \qquad K_{\text{long}} = YS = 7.9 \times 10^3\ \mathrm N ,
> $$
>
> $$
> c_{\text{trans}} = \sqrt{T/\mu} = 427\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad c_{\text{long}} = \sqrt{Y/\rho} = 5.0 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> The longitudinal waves are $\sqrt{YS/T} = 11.8$ times faster. The ratio has a meaning: $T/(YS) = (c_{\text{trans}}/c_{\text{long}})^2 = 0.72\%$ is the strain of the tuned string, how much it has been stretched. A string is a good transverse medium precisely because it is stretched only slightly: the tension that gives it transverse stiffness is a small fraction of the longitudinal stiffness $YS$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lectures 4 and 6 (the two wave equations); data from UP1 Example 16.5 and CM §A7.2, §A9.1*

^ex-b5-1-1

> [!example] Example §B5.1.2: The Helmholtz Resonator
> A jar of volume $V$ has a neck of length $L$ and cross-section $S$. When the wavelength is much longer than the jar, the air does not form a wave: the air in the neck moves as one mass $\rho SL$, and the air in the jar, compressed as a whole, is the spring. Push the plug of air in by $x$: the jar's volume shrinks by $Sx$, the pressure rises adiabatically by $\Delta p = B\,Sx/V$ with $B = \gamma p = \rho c^2$, and the force on the plug is $-S\,\Delta p$. So the spring constant and the frequency ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]) are
>
> $$
> k = \frac{\rho c^2S^2}{V}, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{\rho SL}} = c\,\sqrt{\frac{S}{VL}} .
> $$
>
> For $S = 1.0\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, $L = 1.0\ \mathrm{cm}$, $V = 1.0\ \mathrm L$ and $c = 343\ \mathrm{m/s}$: $\omega = 343\sqrt{10}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} = 1.08 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $f = 173\ \mathrm{Hz}$, with wavelength $c/f = 2.0\ \mathrm m$, twenty times the jar's size, which justifies the lumped picture. A $10\ \mathrm{cm}$ neck would give $55\ \mathrm{Hz}$. The resonator has a single resonance and no overtones, in contrast with an air column of the same air, whose stiffness and inertia are spread out along it. Its $Q$ is low when the neck is narrow and lossy, which the body of a violin uses to broaden its Helmholtz resonance near $300\ \mathrm{Hz}$. (The end correction of the neck, [[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^rem-a3-3-4|Remark: Real pipes]], is neglected.)
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 6 §5*

^ex-b5-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The step from $F(x) - F(x + \Delta x)$ to $-\partial F/\partial x$ and the exchange $\partial_t\partial_x\xi = \partial_x\partial_t\xi$ use partial derivatives ([[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]]) and the equality of mixed partials for twice continuously differentiable functions (Multivariable Analysis).
> - The first-order pair is a linear hyperbolic system; its characteristic combinations $F \pm \sqrt{K\mu}\,u$ are carried unchanged along $x \mp ct$ ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]). Mathematical Methods (planned).
> - In three dimensions a fluid obeys the same pair with $\partial_x$ replaced by the gradient and divergence: [[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions|§B7.1]].
