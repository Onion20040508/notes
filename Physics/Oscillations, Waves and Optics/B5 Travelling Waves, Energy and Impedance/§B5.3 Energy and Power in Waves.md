*Sources: Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lectures 9 and 10 · PHY 300 (Mihaly, Fall 2024).*

Level A computed the kinetic and stretching energy per unit length of a wave on a string ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]]), the average power of a sinusoidal wave as force times velocity ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]]), and intensity, its inverse-square fall-off and the decibel scale ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^def-a2-4-1|Def. §A2.4.1]], [[§A3.2 Sound Intensity|§A3.2]]). This section treats energy as a quantity that flows: in any linear continuum ([[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^mod-b5-1-1|Model §B5.1.1]]) energy density and energy flux obey a local conservation law, the flux of a travelling wave is the characteristic impedance times the velocity squared ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^def-b5-2-2|Def. §B5.2.2]]), waves running in opposite directions carry their energies independently, and a standing wave carries none on balance. Complex amplitudes give the average power into any load in one line.

## Energy density and energy flux

> [!definition] Definition §B5.3.1: Energy Density; Energy Flux
> In a linear one-dimensional continuum with displacement $\xi$, velocity $u = \partial_t\xi$, internal force $F = -K\,\partial_x\xi$ and inertia $\mu$ ([[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^mod-b5-1-1|Model §B5.1.1]]), the **energy density** (energy per unit length) and the **energy flux** (power transmitted across the point $x$ toward $+x$) are
>
> $$
> \varepsilon(x,t) = \frac12\,\mu u^2 + \frac12\,K\Bigl(\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}\Bigr)^2, \qquad P(x,t) = F\,u = -K\,\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}\,\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10 §1–2*

^def-b5-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why these are the energy and the power
> - The first term is the kinetic energy of the mass $\mu\,dx$. The second is the work stored by deforming the element: a slab of length $dx$ obeying the stiffness law is a spring of constant $K/dx$, stretched by $\partial_x\xi\,dx$, so it stores $\tfrac12(K/dx)(\partial_x\xi\,dx)^2 = \tfrac12K(\partial_x\xi)^2\,dx$. For a string this is the stretching energy of [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]], which depends on the slope, not on the displacement ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^rem-a2-4-1|Remark: University Physics' route to the potential energy]]); for a rod it is the elastic energy, for a gas column the work of adiabatic compression.
> - The flux is the power that the medium on the left delivers to the medium on the right: force $F$ ([[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^def-b5-1-1|Def. §B5.1.1]]) times the velocity of its point of application ([[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]]). Schwartz points out that one must take the force from the left only, not the net force on the element. For sound in a tube, $P = S\,\Delta p\,u$, and per unit area $\Delta p\,u$, whose average is the intensity ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]).
> - For the string level A derived both expressions ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]] and the derivation of [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]]). For a general continuum they are taken here as definitions, justified by the two points above and confirmed by [[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^thm-b5-3-1|Theorem §B5.3.1]]: with them, energy is locally conserved.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10 §1–2*

^rem-b5-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.1: Local Conservation of Energy
> For every solution of the wave equation of a linear continuum,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} = 0, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\int_a^b\varepsilon\,dx = P(a,t) - P(b,t) .
> $$
>
> The energy in any stretch changes only by what flows in through its ends. At a fixed or a free end $P = 0$, so a finite medium with such ends conserves its total energy; for a string fixed at both ends this is [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-2|Theorem §B4.2.2]].
>
> *Source: added here, from the densities of Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10*

^thm-b5-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Differentiate $\varepsilon$ and use the first-order pair of [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]], $\mu\,\partial_tu = -\partial_xF$ and $\partial_t(\partial_x\xi) = \partial_xu$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial t} = \mu u\,\partial_tu + K\,\partial_x\xi\,\partial_xu = -u\,\partial_xF - F\,\partial_xu = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(Fu) = -\frac{\partial P}{\partial x},
> $$
>
> using $K\,\partial_x\xi = -F$. Integrate from $a$ to $b$ and exchange $d/dt$ with the integral (the integrand is continuously differentiable); the fundamental theorem of calculus gives $-[P]_a^b$. At a fixed end $u = 0$, at a free end $F = 0$; either way $P = Fu = 0$.

^der-b5-3-1

*Uses:* [[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^def-b5-3-1|Def. §B5.3.1]], [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]], [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-3|Theorem §B5.1.3]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.2: Energy of Counter-Propagating Waves
> Split a solution into right- and left-moving parts, $\xi = f(x - ct) + g(x + ct)$, with velocities $u_+ = -cf'$ and $u_- = cg'$. Then
>
> $$
> \varepsilon = \mu\,u_+^2 + \mu\,u_-^2, \qquad P = Z_0\,u_+^2 - Z_0\,u_-^2 = c\,(\mu u_+^2) - c\,(\mu u_-^2) .
> $$
>
> Each part carries its own energy density $\mu u_\pm^2$ along with it at the wave speed, and there are **no cross terms**: waves running in opposite directions do not interfere in energy, although their displacements do. For a single travelling wave the kinetic and potential densities are equal ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]]), $\varepsilon = \mu u^2$ and $P = c\,\varepsilon = Z_0u^2$: energy moves at the speed $c$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10 §1–2 (single travelling wave); the decomposition is added here*

^thm-b5-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $f(x - ct)$: $\partial_x\xi = f' = -u_+/c$; for $g(x + ct)$: $\partial_x\xi = g' = u_-/c$. So $u = u_+ + u_-$ and $\partial_x\xi = (u_- - u_+)/c$. With $K/c^2 = \mu$,
>
> $$
> \varepsilon = \tfrac12\mu(u_+ + u_-)^2 + \tfrac12\mu(u_+ - u_-)^2 = \mu(u_+^2 + u_-^2) :
> $$
>
> the cross terms $\pm\mu u_+u_-$ of the kinetic and the potential energy cancel. The force is $F = Z_0(u_+ - u_-)$ ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]), so $P = Fu = Z_0(u_+ - u_-)(u_+ + u_-) = Z_0(u_+^2 - u_-^2)$, and $Z_0 = \mu c$. For $g = 0$, $\tfrac12\mu u^2 = \tfrac12K(\partial_x\xi)^2$.

^der-b5-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^def-b5-3-1|Def. §B5.3.1]], [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]], [[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]

## Average power

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.3: Average Power in Terms of Impedance
> Let $F = \operatorname{Re}[\hat Fe^{i\omega t}]$ and $u = \operatorname{Re}[\hat ue^{i\omega t}]$ at a point. The power averaged over a period is
>
> $$
> \langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\hat F\,\hat u^*\bigr) .
> $$
>
> 1. A sinusoidal **travelling wave** of displacement amplitude $A$ carries
>
> $$
> \langle P\rangle = \tfrac12 Z_0\,|\hat u|^2 = \tfrac12 Z_0\,\omega^2A^2 = \frac{|\hat F|^2}{2Z_0} .
> $$
>
> 2. A **load** of impedance $Z_L$ moving with velocity amplitude $|\hat u|$ absorbs $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(Z_L)\,|\hat u|^2$; the reactance absorbs nothing on average.
> 3. An incident wave and its reflection, with velocity amplitudes $\hat u_i$ and $r\hat u_i$, carry the net power $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12Z_0|\hat u_i|^2\bigl(1 - |r|^2\bigr)$ toward $+x$.
>
> For a string, $Z_0 = \sqrt{\mu T}$ makes 1 the formula $\tfrac12\sqrt{\mu F_T}\,\omega^2A^2$ of [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]]; per unit area of a sound wave, with $z = \rho c$, it is $I = \tfrac12\rho c\,\omega^2s_{\max}^2 = \Delta p_{\max}^2/(2\rho c)$ ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]]); for the driven oscillator, 2 with $\operatorname{Re}Z = b$ is $\tfrac12b\,\omega^2A^2$ ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-3|Theorem §A1.6.3]], [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-4|Theorem §B1.4.4]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10 §2 and §4*

^thm-b5-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $P = Fu$ is a product of two sinusoids of the same frequency, whose average is $\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\hat F\hat u^*)$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|Theorem §B1.1.6]]). (1) In a wave toward $+x$, $\hat F = Z_0\hat u$ with $Z_0$ real ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]), so $\langle P\rangle = \tfrac12Z_0|\hat u|^2$; $|\hat u| = \omega A$, and $|\hat u| = |\hat F|/Z_0$. (2) $\hat F = Z_L\hat u$ gives $\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(Z_L\hat u\hat u^*) = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(Z_L)|\hat u|^2$. (3) By [[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]] the flux is $Z_0(u_+^2 - u_-^2)$ with no cross term; average each square with $\langle u_\pm^2\rangle = \tfrac12|\hat u_\pm|^2$.

^der-b5-3-3

*Uses:* [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|Theorem §B1.1.6]], [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]], [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^def-b5-2-1|Def. §B5.2.1]], [[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: What the impedance says about power
> At a fixed velocity amplitude, a high-impedance medium carries more power ($\tfrac12Z_0|\hat u|^2$); at a fixed force amplitude, a low-impedance one does ($|\hat F|^2/2Z_0$). The first is why a heavier, tauter string or a denser medium needs more power for the same motion; the second is why soft ground shakes violently in an earthquake ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^rem-b5-2-2|Remark: Getting energy across a mismatch]]). Part 3 restates [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-4|Theorem §B5.2.4]] from the incident side: whatever is not reflected is transmitted or absorbed. A dashpot with $b = Z_0$ at the end of a string absorbs everything ($r = 0$, [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^mod-b5-2-6|Model §B5.2.6]]); a dashpot with $b = 3Z_0$ or $b = Z_0/3$ has $|r| = \tfrac12$ and absorbs $1 - \tfrac14 = 75\%$.

^rem-b5-3-2

## Standing waves

> [!theorem] Theorem §B5.3.4: Energy in a Standing Wave
> The standing wave $y = A\sin kx\,\cos\omega t$ on a string ($\omega = ck$) has
>
> $$
> \varepsilon = \tfrac14\mu\omega^2A^2\bigl(1 + \cos 2kx\,\cos 2\omega t\bigr), \qquad P = \tfrac14 Z_0\,\omega^2A^2\,\sin 2kx\,\sin 2\omega t .
> $$
>
> The average flux is zero everywhere. The flux vanishes at all times at the nodes and at the antinodes, so the energy between a node and the next antinode, $\mu\omega^2A^2\lambda/16$, stays there: it flows back and forth within each quarter wavelength, sitting as stretching energy near the nodes when the string is at its extreme and as kinetic energy near the antinodes when it is straight. The same holds for every standing wave of a linear continuum.
>
> *Source: added here, from the densities of Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10 §1–2*

^thm-b5-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Here $u = -A\omega\sin kx\sin\omega t$ and $\partial_xy = Ak\cos kx\cos\omega t$. With $Tk^2 = \mu\omega^2$,
>
> $$
> \varepsilon = \tfrac12\mu\omega^2A^2\bigl(\sin^2kx\,\sin^2\omega t + \cos^2kx\,\cos^2\omega t\bigr) .
> $$
>
> Insert $\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 - \cos2\theta)$ and $\cos^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2\theta)$; the products of constants and the mixed terms combine to $\tfrac14(1 + \cos 2kx\cos 2\omega t)$. The flux is $P = -T\,\partial_xy\,u = TkA^2\omega\,\sin kx\cos kx\,\sin\omega t\cos\omega t = \tfrac14Tk\omega A^2\sin 2kx\sin 2\omega t$, and $Tk = T\omega/c = Z_0\omega$. $\sin 2kx = 0$ at $x = n\lambda/4$, the nodes and antinodes. The energy in $[0, \lambda/4]$ is $\tfrac14\mu\omega^2A^2\bigl(\lambda/4 + \cos 2\omega t\int_0^{\lambda/4}\cos 2kx\,dx\bigr) = \mu\omega^2A^2\lambda/16$, since the integral is $\sin(\pi)/2k = 0$. Alternatively: a standing wave is two equal waves in opposite directions, and by [[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]] their fluxes cancel at every point on average.

^der-b5-3-4

## Plane waves and coherent sources

> [!remark] Remark: Plane sound waves and intensity
> A plane sound wave in three dimensions, $s = s_0\cos(\omega t - \vec k\cdot\vec r)$, is the wave of a gas column of unit cross-section moving along $\vec k$ ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]); far from its source any wave is locally plane. Per unit area of the wave front, [[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]] gives the intensity $I = \tfrac12\rho c\,\omega^2s_0^2$. In the wave $\Delta p = \rho c\,u$ ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]], per unit area), so the instantaneous flux $\Delta p\,u = \rho c\,u^2$ is never negative and swings between $0$ and twice its mean $I$ at the frequency $2\omega$. The decibel scale and the inverse-square law are level A ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]], [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-5|Theorem §A2.4.5]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10 §3–4*

^rem-b5-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Coherent and incoherent sources
> Two waves moving in the **same** direction do interfere in energy. Two plane waves of equal amplitude and frequency with phase difference $\Delta\varphi$ add to amplitude $2A\cos(\Delta\varphi/2)$ ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-2|Theorem §A2.5.2]]), so their intensity is $4I_1\cos^2(\Delta\varphi/2)$: four times one source in phase, zero in antiphase. If the phase difference wanders randomly (independent sources, or many frequencies), $\cos^2$ averages to $\tfrac12$ and the intensities simply add, $2I_1$. Where does the extra power of two coherent sources come from? From the sources: each source pushes against the pressure wave of the other, so in phase each does more work per cycle than alone. Schwartz calls this **source loading**.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10 §5*

^rem-b5-3-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B5.3.1: Driving a Long String
> A very long string ($T = 10\ \mathrm N$, $\mu = 0.020\ \mathrm{kg/m}$) is driven at its left end by $y(0,t) = (0.020\ \mathrm m)\cos(10\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\,t)$ and carries a travelling wave $y = A\sin\bigl[\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}(x - ct)\bigr]$ away from it. Find $A$, $c$, $\lambda$, and the force and power the driver must supply.
>
> The end moves with the string, so $A = 0.020\ \mathrm m$. Then $c = \sqrt{T/\mu} = 22.4\ \mathrm{m/s}$ ([[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]]) and $\lambda = 2\pi c/\omega = 14.0\ \mathrm m$. (The wave that actually matches the drive is $y = A\cos(\omega t - kx)$; the stated sine form fits it only up to a phase, which does not affect $A$, $c$ or $\lambda$.) The string presents the driver with the impedance $Z_0 = \sqrt{T\mu} = 0.447\ \mathrm{kg/s}$ ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-2|Theorem §B5.2.2]]): the driver feels a pure drag $-Z_0u$, in phase with the end's velocity, of amplitude $Z_0\omega A = 0.089\ \mathrm N$. By [[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^thm-b5-3-3|Theorem §B5.3.3]],
>
> $$
> \langle P\rangle = \tfrac12 Z_0\,\omega^2A^2 = \tfrac12(0.447\ \mathrm{kg/s})(10\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})^2(0.020\ \mathrm m)^2 = 8.9\ \mathrm{mW} .
> $$
>
> Check through the energy density: the string ahead fills with $\langle\varepsilon\rangle = \tfrac12\mu\omega^2A^2 = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{J/m}$ at the rate $c$ metres per second, $c\langle\varepsilon\rangle = 8.9\ \mathrm{mW}$ ([[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Midterm 2 practice*

^ex-b5-3-1

> [!example] Example §B5.3.2: Energy of a Pulled String
> A string of length $L = 1.00\ \mathrm m$ and mass $0.010\ \mathrm{kg}$, under tension $T = 5\ \mathrm N$, fixed at both ends, is pulled into a zigzag: displacement $+h$ at $x = L/4$ and $-h$ at $3L/4$, straight in between, with $h = 1.00\ \mathrm{cm}$. Find its energy and the lowest frequency at which it vibrates when released.
>
> Every straight piece has slope $\pm 4h/L$ (rising $h$ over $L/4$, or falling $2h$ over $L/2$), and the string is at rest, so by [[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^def-b5-3-1|Def. §B5.3.1]]
>
> $$
> E = \int_0^L\tfrac12T\Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Bigr)^2dx = \tfrac12T\,\frac{16h^2}{L^2}\,L = \frac{8Th^2}{L} = 4.0\ \mathrm{mJ} .
> $$
>
> The course solution reaches the same value by treating each kink as a spring of constant $8T/L$. After release the energy stays $4.0\ \mathrm{mJ}$ ([[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^thm-b5-3-1|Theorem §B5.3.1]]), but it changes form: the shape splits into two half-height copies running in opposite directions ([[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]]), each carrying $2.0\ \mathrm{mJ}$, half kinetic and half stretching ([[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^thm-b5-3-2|Theorem §B5.3.2]]). The initial shape is odd about $x = L/2$, so only the even modes $\sin(n\pi x/L)$, $n = 2, 4, \ldots$, are present ([[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]]); the lowest is $n = 2$, with $\lambda = L$ and
>
> $$
> f_2 = \frac{c}{L} = \frac1L\sqrt{\frac{TL}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{5\ \mathrm N}{(0.010\ \mathrm{kg})(1.00\ \mathrm m)}} = 22.4\ \mathrm{Hz},
> $$
>
> twice the fundamental $11.2\ \mathrm{Hz}$ of the string, which this shape does not excite.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Midterm 2 and solutions*

^ex-b5-3-2

> [!example] Example §B5.3.3: A Loudspeaker near a Wall
> A small loudspeaker sits a distance $d$ in front of a rigid wall; a microphone is on the same normal, at distance $L \gg d$ from the wall. The wall has infinite impedance, so it reflects the sound totally, with the pressure upright ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^cau-b5-2-1|Caution: Which amplitude]]); the reflection is the wave of an image source a distance $d$ behind the wall. Far away both are plane waves, with paths $L - d$ and $L + d$, so they differ in phase by $\Delta\varphi = 2kd = 4\pi d/\lambda$.
> - **Low frequencies**, $d \ll \lambda$: $\Delta\varphi \approx 0$, and the intensity at the microphone is $4\cos^2(\Delta\varphi/2) \approx 4$ times that of the same speaker in open space ([[§B5.3 Energy and Power in Waves#^rem-b5-3-4|Remark: Coherent and incoherent sources]]). Energy is conserved: the sound fills only the half-space in front of the wall, which accounts for a factor $2$; the other factor $2$ is source loading, since the image pushes on the speaker cone in phase with its motion and the speaker, moving as before, does twice the work. Total radiated power doubles; intensity quadruples.
> - **At $d = \lambda/4$**, $\Delta\varphi = \pi$ and the two cancel on the axis. For $d = 0.50\ \mathrm m$ and $c = 343\ \mathrm{m/s}$ this happens at $f = c/4d = 172\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
> - **High frequencies**, $d \gtrsim \lambda$: the phase difference varies rapidly with direction and frequency, and on average the enhancement is only the factor $2$ from confinement.
>
> Schwartz calls the low-frequency gain a proximity resonance. A corner or a narrow wedge adds more images and more gain, which is why bass sounds louder from a speaker in a corner.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10 §5*

^ex-b5-3-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The local conservation law $\partial_t\varepsilon + \partial_xP = 0$ is the one-dimensional form of a continuity equation, like the conservation of charge or of mass in a fluid ([[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^thm-a9-4-2|CM Theorem §A9.4.2]]); for electromagnetic waves the flux is the Poynting vector ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-1|EM Def. §A12.3.1]]).
> - Passing from $\int_a^b\partial_xP\,dx$ to $P(b) - P(a)$ is the fundamental theorem of calculus ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]); the averages use [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|Theorem §B1.1.6]].
> - How the energy of a wave packet moves when the medium is dispersive: with its envelope, at the group velocity ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^thm-b6-2-3|Theorem §B6.2.3]]).
