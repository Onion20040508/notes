Chapter A4 set up the ray model of light and the laws of reflection and refraction ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light|§A4.1]], [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction|§A4.2]]). This chapter asks what an optical element does to *all* the rays leaving one point of an object: when they meet again, or seem to, the element has formed an image. Mirrors come first, because only the law of reflection is involved; this section also fixes the sign convention used for every mirror, surface and lens in the chapter.

## Images

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.1: Image; Real and Virtual Images
> Let rays leave a point $P$ of an object and pass through an optical element (mirror, refracting surface, lens). If after the element they all pass through one point $Q$, or all travel as if they came from one point $Q$, then $Q$ is the **image** of $P$; the images of all the points of an object make up its image.
> - The image is **real** if the rays actually pass through $Q$. A real image can be projected onto a screen placed there.
> - The image is **virtual** if the rays only appear to diverge from $Q$ (their backward extensions meet there). A virtual image cannot be projected.
>
> Both kinds can be seen: the eye only needs rays that diverge as if from a point.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.1*

^def-a5-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.2: Object and Image Distances; Heights; Magnification
> Distances are measured along the optical axis from the vertex of the mirror or surface, or from the centre of the lens: the **object distance** $d_o$ and the **image distance** $d_i$. Heights are measured perpendicular to the axis: the **object height** $h_o$ and the **image height** $h_i$. The (linear) **magnification** is
>
> $$
> m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} .
> $$
>
> $m > 0$ means an upright image, $m < 0$ an inverted one; $|m| > 1$ an enlarged image, $|m| < 1$ a reduced one. The signs of $d_o$, $d_i$, $h_o$, $h_i$ follow [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]].
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.1, §2.2, eq. (2.8)*

^def-a5-1-2

## Plane mirrors

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.1: Image in a Plane Mirror
> A plane mirror forms a virtual, upright image of the same size as the object, as far behind the mirror as the object is in front of it. In the sign convention of [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]],
>
> $$
> d_i = -d_o, \qquad m = +1 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.1, eq. (2.1)*

^thm-a5-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Let the mirror be the plane $x = 0$ and the point $P$ sit at distance $d_o$ in front of it. A ray from $P$ meets the mirror at $B$; by the law of reflection ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction|§A4.2]]) it leaves at the same angle to the normal as it arrived. Extend the reflected ray backward behind the mirror until it meets the normal through $P$ at $Q$. The two right triangles with hypotenuses $PB$ and $QB$ share the side along the mirror and have equal angles at $B$, so they are congruent: $Q$ lies on the normal through $P$ at the same distance $d_o$ behind the mirror. This holds for every ray from $P$, whatever its direction, so all reflected rays diverge from the single point $Q$: an image, and a virtual one, since no light goes behind the mirror. Doing this for the top and the bottom of an object gives an image of the same height and orientation. The image lies on the side opposite the one the reflected light travels into, so $d_i = -d_o$.

^der-a5-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction|§A4.2]] (law of reflection), [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-1|Def. §A5.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Front and back; several mirrors
> - A plane mirror reverses front and back, not left and right: the image of a right hand is a left hand, as when a glove is turned inside out.
> - With two mirrors the image in one acts as an object for the other. Two parallel mirrors give, in principle, an endless row of images (each one reversing front and back again); two mirrors at right angles give three images, one of them formed by rays that reflect off both mirrors.

^rem-a5-1-1

## Spherical mirrors

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.3: Spherical Mirror; Focal Point and Focal Length
> A **spherical mirror** is a piece of a sphere of radius $R$, silvered on one side. It is **concave** if the inner surface reflects and **convex** if the outer surface reflects. Its **optical axis** is the symmetry axis, through the **centre of curvature** $C$ and the **vertex** (the point where the axis meets the mirror).
>
> Rays arriving parallel to the axis leave a concave mirror converging through one point of the axis, the **focal point** $F$; from a convex mirror they leave diverging as if from a point $F$ behind the mirror (a virtual focal point). The distance from the vertex to $F$ is the **focal length** $f$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.2*

^def-a5-1-3

> [!model] Model §A5.1.2: Paraxial Rays
> All rays are **paraxial**: they make small angles with the optical axis and stay at distances from it much smaller than the radius of curvature. Then every angle $\theta$ that occurs is small, and
>
> $$
> \sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta, \qquad \cos\theta \approx 1 ,
> $$
>
> with errors of relative order $\theta^2$. Equivalently, the mirror (or lens) is small compared with its radius of curvature. This chapter assumes the model throughout; it is also called the small-angle approximation.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.2*

^mod-a5-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.3: Focal Length of a Spherical Mirror
> For paraxial rays a spherical mirror has a well-defined focal point, half-way between the vertex and the centre of curvature:
>
> $$
> f = \frac{R}{2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.2, eq. (2.2)*

^thm-a5-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take a concave mirror and a ray parallel to the axis at height $y$, striking the mirror at $X$. The radius $CX$ is the normal there; call the angle of incidence $\theta$, so $\sin\theta = y/R$. The reflected ray makes the same angle $\theta$ with $CX$ (law of reflection), and the incident ray, being parallel to the axis, makes the angle $\theta$ with the axis at $C$ (alternate angles). So the triangle $C X F$, where $F$ is the point where the reflected ray crosses the axis, has equal angles $\theta$ at $C$ and $X$: it is isosceles, $CF = FX$, and its base $CX = R$ gives $CF = R/(2\cos\theta)$. The distance from the vertex is
>
> $$
> f(\theta) = R - \frac{R}{2\cos\theta} .
> $$
>
> For paraxial rays $\cos\theta = 1 + O(\theta^2)$, so $f = R/2$ for all of them: they all cross the axis at the same point. For a convex mirror the same construction, applied to the backward extensions, puts the virtual focal point at $R/2$ behind the vertex.

^der-a5-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction|§A4.2]] (law of reflection), [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-3|Def. §A5.1.3]], [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^mod-a5-1-2|Model §A5.1.2]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Aberrations
> - **Spherical aberration.** The exact formula in the derivation shows that $f(\theta)$ decreases as $\theta$ grows: rays far from the axis cross it closer to the mirror than paraxial ones. A broad parallel beam therefore has no sharp focus, and the image of an extended object is blurred. A *parabolic* mirror focuses every ray parallel to its axis at one point, and a spherical mirror that is small compared with $R$ approximates its central part well.
> - **Coma.** Parallel rays that are *not* parallel to the axis are focused at different heights and distances, giving a comet-shaped smear. Coma also affects parabolic mirrors; it is not a failure of the small-angle approximation.

^rem-a5-1-2

## The sign convention

> [!definition] Definition §A5.1.4: Sign Convention for Mirrors, Refracting Surfaces and Thin Lenses
> University Physics measures distances along the axis from the vertex (mirror, refracting surface) or the centre (thin lens), with light arriving from the object's side, and assigns signs as follows:
>
> | Quantity | Positive | Negative |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | object distance $d_o$ | real object: on the side the light comes from | virtual object: the incoming rays converge toward a point beyond the element (in systems of several elements) |
> | image distance $d_i$ | real image: in front of a mirror; on the far side of a refracting surface or lens | virtual image: behind a mirror; on the object's side of a refracting surface or lens |
> | focal length $f$ | concave mirror; converging lens | convex mirror; diverging lens |
> | radius $R$ | concave mirror; surface convex toward the object | convex mirror; surface concave toward the object |
> | heights $h_o$, $h_i$ | above the optical axis | below the optical axis |
> | magnification $m$ | upright image | inverted image |
>
> With these signs the mirror equation, the refracting-surface equation, the thin-lens equation and the lensmaker's equation each hold in one form for all cases.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.2, §2.3, §2.4, and the summary table of ch. 2*

^def-a5-1-4

> [!remark] Remark: One rule behind the table
> Every sign says whether a point lies where the light really is. An object distance is positive on the incoming side, where the light comes from. An image distance is positive on the outgoing side, where the light goes after the element: in front of a mirror (the light turns back), behind a lens or refracting surface (the light goes on). A radius is positive when the centre of curvature lies on the outgoing side, and a focal length is positive when the focal point is real. So $R = 2f$ has the same sign for both kinds of mirror, and real images always have $d_i > 0$.

^rem-a5-1-3

> [!caution] Caution: Signs in practice
> - Insert every known quantity with its sign, and read the sign of every result: a negative $d_i$ is information (virtual image), not an error.
> - The convention is not universal. Upper-level optics texts often measure all distances in the direction of the light (a "Cartesian" convention), and then the same physics is written with different signs. Check the convention before combining formulas from different books.

^cau-a5-1-1

## Ray tracing and the mirror equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.4: Principal Rays of a Spherical Mirror
> For paraxial rays:
> 1. A ray parallel to the axis is reflected through $F$ (concave) or as if from $F$ (convex).
> 2. A ray through $F$ (concave), or heading toward $F$ behind the mirror (convex), is reflected parallel to the axis.
> 3. A ray through $C$ (concave), or heading toward $C$ (convex), is reflected back along itself.
> 4. A ray striking the vertex is reflected symmetrically about the axis.
>
> Any two of them, traced from the tip of an object, locate the tip of its image; the image of a point on the axis lies on the axis, so an upright object gives an image perpendicular to the axis.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.2*

^thm-a5-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Ray 1 is the definition of $F$ ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-3|Def. §A5.1.3]]), which exists for paraxial rays by [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-3|Theorem §A5.1.3]]. Ray 2 is ray 1 run backwards: light paths are reversible, because the law of reflection is symmetric in the incident and reflected rays. A ray along a radius meets the mirror along the normal, so the angle of incidence is zero and the ray returns along itself (ray 3). At the vertex the normal is the axis, so the angles above and below the axis are equal (ray 4). Because the mirror is symmetric about the axis, it maps the axis to itself.

^der-a5-1-4

*Uses:* [[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction|§A4.2]] (law of reflection), [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-3|Def. §A5.1.3]], [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-3|Theorem §A5.1.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.1.5: Mirror Equation and Magnification
> For a spherical mirror and paraxial rays, with the signs of [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]],
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{2}{R}, \qquad m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = -\frac{d_i}{d_o} .
> $$
>
> For a plane mirror ($R \to \infty$, $1/f = 0$) this gives back $d_i = -d_o$ and $m = 1$ ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-1|Theorem §A5.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.2, eqs. (2.4)–(2.9)*

^thm-a5-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take a concave mirror and an object beyond $F$, so that the image is real and inverted ($d_o, d_i, f, h_o > 0$, $h_i < 0$); in the paraxial model the mirror can be replaced by its vertex plane when measuring heights.
>
> *Magnification* (ray 4). The incident and reflected rays at the vertex make equal angles with the axis, so the right triangles with legs $(d_o, h_o)$ and $(d_i, |h_i|)$ are similar: $|h_i|/h_o = d_i/d_o$. With $h_i < 0$ this is $m = h_i/h_o = -d_i/d_o$.
>
> *Mirror equation* (ray 1). The reflected ray leaves the mirror at height $h_o$, crosses the axis at $F$ (distance $f$ from the vertex) and reaches the image tip at distance $d_i$, height $h_i$. Similar triangles on either side of $F$ give $|h_i|/h_o = (d_i - f)/f$. Equating the two expressions for $|h_i|/h_o$,
>
> $$
> \frac{d_i}{d_o} = \frac{d_i - f}{f} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{d_o} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{d_i} ,
> $$
>
> after dividing by $d_i$. With $f = R/2$ ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-3|Theorem §A5.1.3]]) the right side is $2/R$. For a convex mirror, or an object inside $F$, the same similar triangles appear with some quantities on the other side of the mirror or axis; the sign convention is built so that the signed equations come out identical (University Physics leaves the convex case as an exercise). University Physics instead uses ray 3 through $C$ for the second triangle; that route gives $1/d_o + 1/d_i = 2/R$ directly, again only for paraxial rays, since it treats the mirror as flat at the vertex.

^der-a5-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: Where the image lands
> Solving the mirror equation for $d_i = d_o f/(d_o - f)$ sorts all cases.
> - Concave mirror ($f > 0$): object beyond $C$ ($d_o > 2f$): real, inverted, reduced image between $F$ and $C$. At $C$: real, inverted, same size ($m = -1$), at $C$. Between $C$ and $F$: real, inverted, enlarged, beyond $C$. At $F$: the reflected rays are parallel; no image (it is "at infinity"). Inside $F$: virtual, upright, enlarged, behind the mirror.
> - Convex mirror ($f < 0$): for every real object, a virtual, upright, reduced image between the vertex and $F$, which is why convex mirrors give a wide field of view.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.1.1: A Makeup Mirror
> A mirror shows a face $12.0$ cm away upright and magnified $1.50$ times. Which mirror is it? Upright means $m = +1.50$, so $d_i = -m\,d_o = -18.0$ cm: a virtual image behind the mirror. The mirror equation gives
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{12.0\ \mathrm{cm}} - \frac{1}{18.0\ \mathrm{cm}} = \frac{1}{36.0\ \mathrm{cm}}, \qquad f = +36.0\ \mathrm{cm}, \quad R = 72.0\ \mathrm{cm} .
> $$
>
> A concave mirror, with the face inside its focal length: the one case in which a single mirror magnifies an upright image.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.2, Problem 35*

^ex-a5-1-1

> [!example] Example §A5.1.2: Measuring the Cornea
> A keratometer measures the curvature of the cornea, which reflects like a convex mirror. A light source $12$ cm from the cornea gives an image of magnification $0.032$. From $m = -d_i/d_o$,
>
> $$
> d_i = -m\,d_o = -(0.032)(12\ \mathrm{cm}) = -0.384\ \mathrm{cm},
> $$
>
> a virtual image behind the surface. The mirror equation gives
>
> $$
> f = \Bigl(\frac{1}{12\ \mathrm{cm}} + \frac{1}{-0.384\ \mathrm{cm}}\Bigr)^{-1} = -0.40\ \mathrm{cm}, \qquad R = 2f = -0.79\ \mathrm{cm} .
> $$
>
> The negative signs say "convex", as they must; a radius of curvature of about $0.8$ cm is typical of a cornea. The smaller the radius, the smaller the magnification at a given distance, which is what the instrument exploits.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 2.2*

^ex-a5-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The small-angle approximations of the paraxial model are first-order Taylor expansions: $\sin\theta = \theta + O(\theta^3)$, $\cos\theta = 1 + O(\theta^2)$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).
> - Rays are the normals to the wave fronts of a light wave, such as the [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^mod-a12-2-1|EM Model §A12.2.1]]; geometric optics is the limit in which the wavelength is much smaller than every mirror and aperture ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light|§A4.1]]).
> - At the upper level, paraxial optics is written with ray matrices (Chapter B12, planned).
