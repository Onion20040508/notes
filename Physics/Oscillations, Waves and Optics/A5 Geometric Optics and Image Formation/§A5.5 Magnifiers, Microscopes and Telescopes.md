How large an object looks is set by the angle it subtends at the eye, since that fixes the size of its image on the retina ([[§A5.4 The Eye and the Camera|§A5.4]]). Optical instruments enlarge that angle: a magnifier lets the eye get closer than its near point, a microscope adds a lens that first makes an enlarged real image, and a telescope brings a distant object's image close to the eye. Each is analysed one lens at a time ([[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^rem-a5-3-2|§A5.3]]), with the signs of [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]].

## Angular magnification and the magnifier

> [!definition] Definition §A5.5.1: Angular Magnification
> The **angular magnification** of an instrument is
>
> $$
> M = \frac{\theta_{\text{image}}}{\theta_{\text{object}}},
> $$
>
> where $\theta_{\text{image}}$ is the angle subtended at the eye by the image the instrument forms, and $\theta_{\text{object}}$ the angle the object subtends when viewed without it under the best conditions: at the near point, $25$ cm, for a small nearby object (magnifier, microscope); at its actual distance for a distant one (telescope). A negative $M$ means an inverted final image.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.7, eq. (2.26); §2.8, eq. (2.39)*

^def-a5-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.5.1: Simple Magnifier
> A converging lens of focal length $f$ held a distance $\ell$ in front of the eye, with the object inside its focal length so that it forms a virtual image a distance $L$ from the eye, has angular magnification
>
> $$
> M = \frac{25\ \mathrm{cm}}{L}\Bigl(1 + \frac{L - \ell}{f}\Bigr) \qquad (\text{all lengths in cm}).
> $$
>
> With the lens at the eye ($\ell = 0$): image at the near point ($L = 25$ cm) gives the largest value, $M = 1 + (25\ \mathrm{cm})/f$; image at infinity (relaxed eye) gives $M = (25\ \mathrm{cm})/f$. So
>
> $$
> \frac{25\ \mathrm{cm}}{f} \le M \le 1 + \frac{25\ \mathrm{cm}}{f} ,
> $$
>
> and a shorter focal length magnifies more.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.7, eqs. (2.27)–(2.33)*

^thm-a5-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For small angles the angle subtended by an object of height $h$ at distance $D$ is $h/D$ in radians. The image, of height $h_i$ at distance $L$ from the eye, subtends $\theta_{\text{image}} = h_i/L$; the object viewed at the near point subtends $\theta_{\text{object}} = h_o/(25\ \mathrm{cm})$. So
>
> $$
> M = \frac{h_i}{h_o}\,\frac{25\ \mathrm{cm}}{L} = \Bigl(-\frac{d_i}{d_o}\Bigr)\frac{25\ \mathrm{cm}}{L} = \Bigl(1 - \frac{d_i}{f}\Bigr)\frac{25\ \mathrm{cm}}{L},
> $$
>
> using $m = -d_i/d_o$ and $1/d_o = 1/f - 1/d_i$ from [[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^thm-a5-3-3|Theorem §A5.3.3]]. The image is virtual, on the object's side of the lens, so $d_i < 0$ and $|d_i| = L - \ell$, that is $-d_i = L - \ell$. Substituting gives the formula. For $\ell = 0$, $L = 25$ cm it is $1 + 25/f$; as $L \to \infty$, $(25/L)(1 + L/f) \to 25/f$. Between the two the expression is monotonic in $L$, which gives the range.

The emerging rays (red) diverge as if from a virtual image (dashed) at the near point, six times the object's height, so the image subtends six times the angle the object would subtend at 25 cm: $M = 1 + 25/5.0 = 6.0$.*

> [!caution] Caution: Angular versus linear magnification
> $M$ compares angles, $m$ compares heights. They coincide only for a magnifier at the eye with the image at the near point, where both the image and the comparison object are $25$ cm away. A telescope's image is far smaller than the object, $|m| \ll 1$, yet $|M| > 1$: what matters to the eye is the angle. With the image at infinity, $m$ is not even defined, while $M = 25\ \mathrm{cm}/f$ is finite.

^cau-a5-5-1

> [!example] Example §A5.5.1: Inspecting a Diamond
> A jeweller holds a magnifier at the eye and wants a $3.0$ mm diamond to appear as large as a $15$ mm object at the near point. With the image at the near point, $M = m = 15/3.0 = 5.0$, and [[§A5.5 Magnifiers, Microscopes and Telescopes#^thm-a5-5-1|Theorem §A5.5.1]] gives
>
> $$
> f = \frac{25\ \mathrm{cm}}{M - 1} = \frac{25\ \mathrm{cm}}{4.0} = 6.3\ \mathrm{cm} .
> $$
>
> For $10\times$ the lens would need $f = 25\ \mathrm{cm}/9 = 2.8$ cm: strongly curved, and to be held very close to the eye. Beyond about $5\times$ to $10\times$ a single lens becomes impractical, which is what the compound microscope solves.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 2.10*

## The compound microscope

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.5.2: Magnification of a Compound Microscope
> An objective of short focal length $f_{\text{obj}}$, with the object just beyond its focal point, forms a real, inverted, enlarged image at $d_i^{\text{obj}}$; an eyepiece of focal length $f_{\text{eye}}$ views this image as a magnifier. The net angular magnification is
>
> $$
> M_{\text{net}} = m_{\text{obj}}\,M_{\text{eye}}, \qquad m_{\text{obj}} = -\frac{d_i^{\text{obj}}}{d_o^{\text{obj}}} = -\frac{d_i^{\text{obj}} - f_{\text{obj}}}{f_{\text{obj}}} .
> $$
>
> - Final image at the near point: $M_{\text{eye}} = 1 + (25\ \mathrm{cm})/f_{\text{eye}}$.
> - Final image at infinity: the intermediate image is at the focal point of the eyepiece, $M_{\text{eye}} = (25\ \mathrm{cm})/f_{\text{eye}}$, and with the **tube length** $L = d_i^{\text{obj}} - f_{\text{obj}}$, the distance between the facing focal points of the two lenses (standardised at $16$ cm),
>
> $$
> M_{\text{net}} = -\frac{L}{f_{\text{obj}}}\,\frac{25\ \mathrm{cm}}{f_{\text{eye}}} = -\frac{(16\ \mathrm{cm})(25\ \mathrm{cm})}{f_{\text{obj}}\,f_{\text{eye}}} .
> $$
>
> The final image is virtual and inverted, which no single lens can produce.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.8, eqs. (2.34)–(2.38)*

^thm-a5-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Objective.* By the thin-lens equation, $1/d_o^{\text{obj}} = 1/f_{\text{obj}} - 1/d_i^{\text{obj}}$, so $m_{\text{obj}} = -d_i^{\text{obj}}/d_o^{\text{obj}} = 1 - d_i^{\text{obj}}/f_{\text{obj}}$, the stated form.
>
> *Eyepiece.* Its object is the intermediate image, of height $h_i^{\text{obj}} = m_{\text{obj}}h_o$. As a magnifier at the eye ([[§A5.5 Magnifiers, Microscopes and Telescopes#^thm-a5-5-1|Theorem §A5.5.1]]) it makes the final image subtend $\theta_{\text{image}} = M_{\text{eye}}\,h_i^{\text{obj}}/(25\ \mathrm{cm})$. The unaided eye would see the object at the near point under $\theta_{\text{object}} = h_o/(25\ \mathrm{cm})$. Dividing, $M_{\text{net}} = M_{\text{eye}}\,h_i^{\text{obj}}/h_o = m_{\text{obj}}M_{\text{eye}}$.
>
> *Image at infinity.* Rays from a point in the eyepiece's focal plane leave it parallel, so for a relaxed eye the intermediate image must lie in that plane, and $M_{\text{eye}} = 25\ \mathrm{cm}/f_{\text{eye}}$. The tube length is the distance from the objective's focal point to its image, $L = d_i^{\text{obj}} - f_{\text{obj}}$, so $m_{\text{obj}} = -L/f_{\text{obj}}$.
>
> University Physics writes $m_{\text{obj}} \approx -d_i^{\text{obj}}/f_{\text{obj}}$, using $d_o^{\text{obj}} \approx f_{\text{obj}}$; that approximation drops the $f_{\text{obj}}$ next to $d_i^{\text{obj}}$, which is good when $d_i^{\text{obj}} \gg f_{\text{obj}}$.

*Uses:* [[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^thm-a5-3-3|Theorem §A5.3.3]], [[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^rem-a5-3-2|§A5.3]] (systems of several elements), [[§A5.5 Magnifiers, Microscopes and Telescopes#^thm-a5-5-1|Theorem §A5.5.1]], [[§A5.5 Magnifiers, Microscopes and Telescopes#^def-a5-5-1|Def. §A5.5.1]]

> [!example] Example §A5.5.2: A Microscope
> An object is $6.20$ mm from an objective with $f_{\text{obj}} = 6.00$ mm; the eyepiece has $f_{\text{eye}} = 50.0$ mm and is placed so that the final image is at the near point. The objective images the object at
>
> $$
> d_i^{\text{obj}} = \Bigl(\frac{1}{6.00\ \mathrm{mm}} - \frac{1}{6.20\ \mathrm{mm}}\Bigr)^{-1} = 186\ \mathrm{mm}, \qquad m_{\text{obj}} = -\frac{186}{6.20} = -30.0 .
> $$
>
> The eyepiece gives $M_{\text{eye}} = 1 + 25/5.00 = 6.00$, so $M_{\text{net}} = (-30.0)(6.00) = -180$: the final image is inverted and subtends 180 times the angle the object would at the near point. For this the eyepiece must stand $4.17$ cm beyond the intermediate image (its object distance for an image at $-25$ cm), so the lenses are $22.8$ cm apart. University Physics quotes $-186$, from the approximation $m_{\text{obj}} \approx -d_i^{\text{obj}}/f_{\text{obj}}$; with the final image at infinity instead, the same lenses give $M_{\text{net}} = (-30.0)(5.00) = -150$.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 2.11*

## Telescopes

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.5.3: Angular Magnification of a Telescope
> A refracting telescope whose objective (focal length $f_{\text{obj}}$) and eyepiece (focal length $f_{\text{eye}}$) share a focal plane, viewing a distant object, produces a final image at infinity with angular magnification
>
> $$
> M = -\frac{f_{\text{obj}}}{f_{\text{eye}}} .
> $$
>
> The image is inverted; a long objective focal length and a short eyepiece focal length give a large magnification. The same holds for a reflecting telescope, with $f_{\text{obj}} = R/2$ of its mirror.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.8, eq. (2.40)*

^thm-a5-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The object is effectively at infinity: rays from one of its points arrive parallel, at the small angle $\theta_{\text{object}}$ to the axis, which is also the angle the object subtends at the unaided eye. The objective focuses them to a point of its focal plane, where the undeviated ray through its centre arrives: at the distance $|h| = f_{\text{obj}}\,\theta_{\text{object}}$ from the axis, on the opposite side from the object (inverted). That point lies in the focal plane of the eyepiece too, so the rays leave the eyepiece parallel, along the ray through the eyepiece's centre, at the angle $\theta_{\text{image}} = |h|/f_{\text{eye}}$. Hence $|M| = \theta_{\text{image}}/\theta_{\text{object}} = f_{\text{obj}}/f_{\text{eye}}$, and the minus sign records the inversion. For a mirror objective, $f = R/2$ by [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-3|Theorem §A5.1.3]].

> [!remark] Remark: Kinds of telescopes
> - Microscope versus telescope: both have an objective and an eyepiece, but in a microscope the object is close and the intermediate image larger than the object, while in a telescope the object is far away, the intermediate image is tiny, and all the magnification of the angle comes from the eyepiece.
> - Galileo's telescope uses a diverging eyepiece and gives an upright image; a terrestrial telescope adds a third converging lens that inverts the image once more.
> - Large refracting telescopes suffer from chromatic aberration ([[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^rem-a5-3-3|§A5.3]]), absorption in thick glass, and lenses sagging under their own weight (the largest, at Yerkes, has a 40-inch objective). Newton's reflecting telescope replaced the objective lens by a concave mirror, which has no chromatic aberration; in the **Newtonian** design a small flat mirror sends the light to an eyepiece at the side of the tube, in the **Cassegrain** design a convex mirror sends it back through a hole in the primary mirror. Most research telescopes, including the Hubble (a $2.4$ m mirror; its resolution: [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^ex-a7-4-1|Example §A7.4.1]]) and the two $10$ m Keck telescopes, are reflectors. A large mirror gathers more light and, by [[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution#^thm-a7-4-1|Theorem §A7.4.1]], resolves finer detail.

^rem-a5-5-1

> [!example] Example §A5.5.3: A Small Reflecting Telescope
> A telescope has a concave objective mirror of radius $2.00$ m and an eyepiece of focal length $4.00$ cm. Its objective focal length is $R/2 = 1.00$ m, so
>
> $$
> M = -\frac{100\ \mathrm{cm}}{4.00\ \mathrm{cm}} = -25.0 .
> $$
>
> A sunspot $25\,000$ km across, at the Sun's distance of $1.50 \times 10^8$ km, subtends $\theta_{\text{object}} = (2.5 \times 10^4)/(1.50 \times 10^8) = 1.7 \times 10^{-4}$ rad to the naked eye, and its image in the telescope $25 \times 1.7 \times 10^{-4} = 4.2 \times 10^{-3}$ rad $= 0.24^\circ$.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.8, Problem 104*
