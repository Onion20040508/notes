A lens is two refracting surfaces ([[§A5.2 Images Formed by Refraction|§A5.2]]) close together. When it is thin, the two surfaces act as one element with a single focal length, and image formation obeys the same equation as for mirrors ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-5|Theorem §A5.1.5]]). This section derives that thin-lens equation and the lensmaker's equation, which gives the focal length from the shape and the glass. Signs follow [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]].

## Converging and diverging lenses

> [!definition] Definition §A5.3.1: Converging and Diverging Lens; Focal Point; Focal Plane
> A **converging** (convex) lens bends rays toward its optical axis: rays entering parallel to the axis cross the axis at one point on the far side, the **focal point** $F$. A **diverging** (concave) lens bends rays away from the axis: rays entering parallel to the axis leave as if they came from a point $F$ on the incoming side (a virtual focal point). The distance from the centre of the lens to $F$ is the **focal length** $f$, positive for a converging and negative for a diverging lens. The **focal plane** is the plane through $F$ perpendicular to the axis. A converging lens is thicker at the centre than at the edge, a diverging lens thinner.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.4*

^def-a5-3-1

> [!model] Model §A5.3.1: Thin Lens
> A lens is **thin** if its thickness is much smaller than the radii of curvature of its surfaces and than the object and image distances. In this model, together with paraxial rays ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^mod-a5-1-2|Model §A5.1.2]]):
> - a ray bends once, at the central plane of the lens;
> - a ray through the centre of the lens goes on undeviated;
> - the lens has the same focal length on both sides.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.4*

^mod-a5-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.3.2: Ray-Tracing Rules for a Thin Lens
> 1. A ray entering parallel to the axis leaves through the focal point on the far side (converging lens), or along the line from the focal point on the near side (diverging lens).
> 2. A ray through the centre of the lens is not deviated.
> 3. A ray through the near focal point (converging lens), or heading toward the far focal point (diverging lens), leaves parallel to the axis.
>
> Two of these rays from the tip of an object locate the tip of the image; the base of an object on the axis is imaged on the axis.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.4*

^thm-a5-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Rule 1 is [[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^def-a5-3-1|Def. §A5.3.1]]. Rule 3 is rule 1 for light running backwards: Snell's law reads the same in both directions, so every light path can be reversed ([[§A4.2 Reflection and Refraction#^thm-a4-2-5|Theorem §A4.2.5]]), and the focal length is the same on both sides ([[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^mod-a5-3-1|Model §A5.3.1]]). Near its centre the lens is a thin slab with parallel faces: a ray crossing it is refracted at the first face and back to its original direction at the second, with a sideways shift proportional to the thickness, which the model neglects. That is rule 2.

^der-a5-3-2

## The thin-lens equation

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.3.3: Thin-Lens Equation and Lensmaker's Equation
> A thin lens of index $n_2$ in a medium of index $n_1$, with surface radii $R_1$ (the surface met first) and $R_2$, forms images according to
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{f}, \qquad m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = -\frac{d_i}{d_o},
> $$
>
> with focal length given by the **lensmaker's equation**
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{f} = \Bigl(\frac{n_2}{n_1} - 1\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\Bigr), \qquad \text{in air:}\quad \frac{1}{f} = (n - 1)\Bigl(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> All quantities carry the signs of [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]]. The focal length depends only on the two radii and the two indices.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.4, eqs. (2.18)–(2.22)*

^thm-a5-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Two surfaces in a row.* Let the lens have thickness $t$. The first surface (from $n_1$ into $n_2$, radius $R_1$) images the object at a distance $d_i'$ from it ([[§A5.2 Images Formed by Refraction#^thm-a5-2-2|Theorem §A5.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> \frac{n_1}{d_o} + \frac{n_2}{d_i'} = \frac{n_2 - n_1}{R_1} .
> $$
>
> This image is the object for the second surface (from $n_2$ back into $n_1$, radius $R_2$), at the distance $d_o' = t - d_i'$ in front of it; the formula holds whatever the sign of $d_i'$. The second surface forms the final image at $d_i$:
>
> $$
> \frac{n_2}{t - d_i'} + \frac{n_1}{d_i} = \frac{n_1 - n_2}{R_2} .
> $$
>
> Adding the two equations,
>
> $$
> \frac{n_1}{d_o} + \frac{n_1}{d_i} + \frac{n_2}{d_i'} + \frac{n_2}{t - d_i'} = (n_2 - n_1)\Bigl(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> In the thin-lens limit $t \to 0$ the third and fourth terms cancel. Dividing by $n_1$,
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} = \Bigl(\frac{n_2}{n_1} - 1\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{1}{R_1} - \frac{1}{R_2}\Bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Reading off $f$.* The right side does not depend on where the object is. For an object at infinity the image is at the focal point, $d_i = f$ ([[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^def-a5-3-1|Def. §A5.3.1]]), so the right side equals $1/f$: this is the lensmaker's equation, and the left side then gives the thin-lens equation.
>
> *Magnification.* The ray through the centre goes straight on ([[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^thm-a5-3-2|Theorem §A5.3.2]]), so the triangles it forms with the axis on the two sides are similar: $h_o/d_o = -h_i/d_i$ (a real image lies below the axis), so $m = -d_i/d_o$.
>
> *A second route*, from ray tracing alone. The ray that enters parallel at height $h_o$ crosses the axis at $F$ and reaches the image tip; similar triangles on either side of $F$ give $-h_i/h_o = (d_i - f)/f$. Equating with $-h_i/h_o = d_i/d_o$ and dividing by $d_i$ gives $1/d_o = 1/f - 1/d_i$, exactly as for the mirror ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^thm-a5-1-5|Theorem §A5.1.5]]).

> [!caution] Caution: Signs of the two radii
> $R_1$ belongs to the surface the light meets first. For a biconvex lens the first surface is convex toward the object ($R_1 > 0$) and the second is concave toward it ($R_2 < 0$), so both terms of $1/R_1 - 1/R_2$ are positive and $f > 0$. For a biconcave lens both terms are negative and $f < 0$. Turning a thin lens around swaps the surfaces and flips both signs, which leaves $f$ unchanged: a thin lens has the same focal length from either side.

^cau-a5-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Where the image lands
> Solving for $d_i = (1/f - 1/d_o)^{-1}$ gives the same sorting as for a concave mirror ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^rem-a5-1-4|§A5.1]]), with "in front of the mirror" replaced by "on the far side".
> - Converging lens: an object far away gives an image near the focal plane ($d_i \to f$). As the object approaches $F$, the real, inverted image recedes to infinity; at $2f$ it is at $2f$ and of the same size. Inside $F$ the image becomes virtual, upright and enlarged, on the object's side.
> - Diverging lens: every real object gives a virtual, upright, reduced image between $F$ and the lens.
>
> A single lens or mirror cannot form a virtual image of a real object that is inverted; that takes two elements ([[§A5.5 Magnifiers, Microscopes and Telescopes|§A5.5]]).

^rem-a5-3-1

![[ph-wo-a5-3-2.svg]]

The converging lens gives real images ($d_i > 0$) for $d_o > f$, approaching the focal plane for distant objects, and virtual images for $d_o < f$; the diverging lens gives virtual images, $f < d_i < 0$ (between its focal point and the lens), for every object. Dots: the three cases of Example §A5.3.2.*

> [!remark] Remark: Systems of several elements
> When light passes through several mirrors or lenses, apply the image equations one element at a time: the image formed by one element is the object for the next, with its distance measured from the next element. If the light reaches the next element before converging to that intermediate image, it is a virtual object, $d_o < 0$ ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]]). The total magnification is the product of the individual ones. The derivation above is the simplest case, and microscopes and telescopes are built this way ([[§A5.5 Magnifiers, Microscopes and Telescopes|§A5.5]]).

^rem-a5-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Oblique parallel rays; chromatic aberration
> - Parallel rays that are not parallel to the axis also come to a point in the focal plane, namely where the undeviated ray through the centre crosses it. This is why a distant scene forms a whole image in the focal plane of a camera or telescope objective.
> - The index of a real glass depends on the wavelength (dispersion, [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]]), so by the lensmaker's equation so does $f$: colours focus at different points and the edges of a white object's image come out coloured and blurred. This **chromatic aberration** is reduced by a *doublet*, a converging and a diverging lens of different glasses cemented together. Mirrors have none (the law of reflection does not involve $n$), which is one reason large telescopes use mirrors.

^rem-a5-3-3

> [!remark] Remark: Ray matrices
> In the paraxial model a ray is fixed, at any plane along the axis, by its distance $\rho$ from the axis and its angle $\theta$ to it, and every element acts *linearly* on the pair $(\rho, \theta)$. So each element is a $2\times 2$ matrix and a system of elements is the product of their matrices: free travel over a distance $t$ is $\begin{pmatrix}1 & t\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ and a thin lens is $\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ -1/f & 1\end{pmatrix}$. Requiring that the position at the image plane not depend on the starting angle gives back the thin-lens equation. This is the method of PHY 300 and of Chapter B12: the imaging condition [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-4|Theorem §B12.1.4]], and the thin-lens equation from it, [[§B12.1 Ray Matrices for Refraction, Translation and Lenses#^thm-b12-1-5|Theorem §B12.1.5]].
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 12*

^rem-a5-3-4

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A5.3.1: Covering Half the Lens
> A converging lens projects the image of an object onto a screen. What happens when the left half of the lens is covered? The whole image remains, in the same place and at the same size, but dimmer. Every point of the lens receives rays from every point of the object and sends them to the corresponding image point ([[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-1|Def. §A5.1.1]]); covering part of the lens removes some of the rays to *each* image point, not the image points themselves. The principal rays are just convenient members of that bundle; the image does not need them to pass through the lens.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 12*

^ex-a5-3-1

> [!example] Example §A5.3.2: One Lens, Three Object Distances
> A $3.0$ cm tall object stands in front of a converging lens with $f = +10.0$ cm. From $d_i = (1/f - 1/d_o)^{-1}$ and $m = -d_i/d_o$:
>
> | $d_o$ | $d_i$ | $m$ | $h_i$ | image |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | $50.0$ cm | $+12.5$ cm | $-0.250$ | $-0.75$ cm | real, inverted, reduced |
> | $20.0$ cm | $+20.0$ cm | $-1.00$ | $-3.0$ cm | real, inverted, same size |
> | $5.00$ cm | $-10.0$ cm | $+2.00$ | $+6.0$ cm | virtual, upright, enlarged |
>
> The first case is a camera (distant object, image near the focal plane, reduced), the last a magnifying glass (object inside $F$).
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 2.4*

^ex-a5-3-2

> [!example] Example §A5.3.3: Grinding a Diverging Lens
> What radius of curvature must a symmetric biconcave lens of glass with $n = 1.55$ have for a focal length of $20$ cm in air? A diverging lens has $f = -20$ cm. Its first surface is concave toward the object, $R_1 = -R$, and its second is convex toward it, $R_2 = +R$, with $R > 0$ the common radius. The lensmaker's equation gives
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{f} = (n - 1)\Bigl(-\frac{1}{R} - \frac{1}{R}\Bigr) = -\frac{2(n-1)}{R} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R = -2(n-1)f = -2(0.55)(-20\ \mathrm{cm}) = 22\ \mathrm{cm} .
> $$
>
> The signs work out to a positive radius, as they must for the magnitude $R$.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 2.3*

^ex-a5-3-3
