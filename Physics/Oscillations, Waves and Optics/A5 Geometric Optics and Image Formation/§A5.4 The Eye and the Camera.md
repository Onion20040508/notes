The eye and the camera are both a converging lens that throws a real, inverted image onto a light-sensitive surface. The thin-lens equation of [[§A5.3 Thin Lenses|§A5.3]] describes both; they differ in how they focus objects at different distances: the eye changes its focal length, a camera its lens-to-sensor distance, or neither if the focal length is short enough. This section also treats the commonest application of lenses, correcting vision. Signs follow [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]].

## The eye

> [!model] Model §A5.4.1: The Eye as a Thin Lens
> The cornea and the crystalline lens together act as a single thin converging lens at a fixed distance (about $2$ cm) from the retina. For clear vision the image distance must equal this lens-to-retina distance, for objects near and far; the ciliary muscles change the shape, hence the focal length, of the lens to achieve this. The layered structure of the eye and its several refracting interfaces are ignored.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.5*

^mod-a5-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §A5.4.1: Accommodation; Near Point and Far Point
> **Accommodation** is the adjustment of the eye's focal length to focus objects at different distances. The **near point** is the closest distance at which the eye can focus, the **far point** the farthest. For normal vision they are taken as $25$ cm and infinity. The near point grows with age.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.5*

^def-a5-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §A5.4.2: Optical Power
> The **optical power** of a lens of focal length $f$ is
>
> $$
> P = \frac{1}{f},
> $$
>
> measured in **diopters**, $1\ \mathrm D = 1\ \mathrm m^{-1}$ (with $f$ in metres). It is positive for converging and negative for diverging lenses. Eyeglass and contact-lens prescriptions are given as powers.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.5, eq. (2.23)*

^def-a5-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A5.4.2: Power Form of the Thin-Lens Equation; Lenses in Contact
> In terms of power the thin-lens equation reads
>
> $$
> P = \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} .
> $$
>
> Thin lenses in contact act as one thin lens whose power is the sum of theirs:
>
> $$
> P_{\text{total}} = P_1 + P_2 + P_3 + \cdots
> $$
>
> For thin lenses close together but not touching, this holds approximately.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.5, eqs. (2.24)–(2.25)*

^thm-a5-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The first equation is [[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^thm-a5-3-3|Theorem §A5.3.3]] with $1/f = P$. For two thin lenses in contact, apply it one lens at a time ([[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^rem-a5-3-2|Remark: Systems of several elements]]). Lens 1 images the object at $d_i'$: $P_1 = 1/d_o + 1/d_i'$. With no gap, that image lies at the same distance $d_i'$ beyond lens 2, on its outgoing side, so for lens 2 it is an object at $d_o'' = -d_i'$ (a virtual object if $d_i' > 0$; [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]]), and lens 2 forms the final image at $d_i$: $P_2 = -1/d_i' + 1/d_i$. Adding, the intermediate image drops out:
>
> $$
> P_1 + P_2 = \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} ,
> $$
>
> which is the thin-lens equation of a single lens of power $P_1 + P_2$. Repeat for more lenses.

^der-a5-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^thm-a5-3-3|Theorem §A5.3.3]], [[§A5.4 The Eye and the Camera#^def-a5-4-2|Def. §A5.4.2]], [[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^rem-a5-3-2|§A5.3]] (systems of several elements), [[§A5.1 Images Formed by Mirrors#^def-a5-1-4|Def. §A5.1.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Lenses with a gap
> For two thin lenses a distance $t$ apart, the effective focal length is $1/f = 1/f_1 + 1/f_2 - t/(f_1 f_2)$, which reduces to the sum of powers when $t \ll |f_1|, |f_2|$. In PHY 300 this follows from multiplying ray matrices ([[§A5.3 Thin Lenses#^rem-a5-3-4|§A5.3]]). For such a separated pair, object and image distances are no longer measured from a single lens plane but from the system's principal planes ([[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^thm-b12-2-1|Theorem §B12.2.1]]; the separated pair is [[§B12.2 Optical Systems, Lens Waveguides and Resonators#^ex-b12-2-1|Example §B12.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Final practice exam*

^rem-a5-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: The cornea does most of the focusing
> The refractive indices in the eye are close to each other: cornea $1.38$, aqueous humor $1.34$, lens $1.41$ on average (largest at its centre), vitreous humor $1.34$. The big jump is at the front, from air ($1.00$) into the cornea, so the cornea, with a focal length of about $2.3$ cm, does most of the focusing, and the lens ($f \approx 6.4$ cm) supplies the adjustable fine focus. Under water the jump at the cornea nearly disappears ($1.33 \to 1.38$), the eye loses most of its power and vision blurs; a face mask restores the air in front of the cornea. The retinal image is inverted; the brain turns it upright.

^rem-a5-4-2

> [!definition] Definition §A5.4.3: Nearsightedness and Farsightedness
> A **nearsighted** (myopic) eye converges too strongly: distant objects are imaged in front of the retina and look blurred, while near objects are seen clearly; the far point is closer than infinity. A **farsighted** (hyperopic) eye converges too weakly: near objects are imaged behind the retina, and the near point is farther than $25$ cm.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.5*

^def-a5-4-3

> [!remark] Remark: How a corrective lens works
> A corrective lens does not repair the eye's focusing. It forms an image of the object at a place the eye *can* focus on, and the eye looks at that image as its object.
> - Myopia: a diverging lens ($P < 0$) makes the parallel rays from a distant object diverge as if they came from the far point.
> - Hyperopia: a converging lens ($P > 0$) takes an object at $25$ cm and forms a virtual image of it at the near point.
>
> In both cases the image distance is negative (virtual image, on the object's side), and distances must be measured from the lens, not from the eye, when glasses sit in front of the eye.

## Examples: the eye

> [!example] Example §A5.4.1: Power of the Eye
> The cornea and the eye lens have focal lengths of $2.3$ cm and $6.4$ cm. Treated as thin lenses in contact ([[§A5.4 The Eye and the Camera#^thm-a5-4-2|Theorem §A5.4.2]]),
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{f_{\text{eye}}} = \frac{1}{2.3\ \mathrm{cm}} + \frac{1}{6.4\ \mathrm{cm}}, \qquad f_{\text{eye}} = 1.69\ \mathrm{cm}, \qquad P_{\text{eye}} = \frac{1}{0.0169\ \mathrm m} = 59\ \mathrm D .
> $$
>
> For an object at the near point, an eye of focal length $1.7$ cm forms the image at $d_i = (1/1.7 - 1/25)^{-1}\ \mathrm{cm} = 1.8$ cm behind the lens, with $m = -1.8/25 = -0.073$: inverted and only 7 % of the object's size. The image distance hardly changes between $25$ cm and infinity, which is why a small change of focal length suffices for accommodation.
>
> *Source: UP3 Examples 2.6–2.7*

^ex-a5-4-1

> [!example] Example §A5.4.2: Correcting Nearsightedness
> A nearsighted person has a far point of $30.0$ cm; the glasses sit $1.50$ cm in front of the eye. For an object at infinity the lens must form a virtual image at the far point, $30.0 - 1.50 = 28.5$ cm in front of the lens: $d_o = \infty$, $d_i = -0.285$ m. Then
>
> $$
> P = \frac{1}{\infty} + \frac{1}{-0.285\ \mathrm m} = -3.51\ \mathrm D ,
> $$
>
> a diverging lens, thinnest at its centre, as on every prescription for nearsighted eyes.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 2.8*

^ex-a5-4-2

> [!example] Example §A5.4.3: Correcting Farsightedness
> A farsighted person has a near point of $1.00$ m and wants to read at $25.0$ cm; the glasses sit $1.5$ cm in front of the eye. The lens must image the page, at $d_o = 25.0 - 1.5 = 23.5$ cm, to the near point, $d_i = -(100 - 1.5)\ \mathrm{cm} = -98.5$ cm:
>
> $$
> P = \frac{1}{0.235\ \mathrm m} + \frac{1}{-0.985\ \mathrm m} = +3.24\ \mathrm D ,
> $$
>
> a converging lens, thickest at its centre.
>
> *Source: UP3 Example 2.9*

## The camera

> [!remark] Remark: A camera is a single lens
> However many elements a camera lens has, its optics is essentially that of one converging lens with the object much farther away than the focal length, so the real, inverted image lies just beyond the focal plane, where the sensor is. In a digital camera the sensor is a charge-coupled device (CCD), a chip of light-sensitive pixels behind red, green and blue filters. Simple cameras focus by moving the lens relative to the sensor; a phone camera cannot, and does not need to ([[§A5.4 The Eye and the Camera#^ex-a5-4-4|Example §A5.4.4]]).

^rem-a5-4-4

> [!remark] Remark: Aperture and depth of focus
> The rays forming one image point fill a cone whose base is the lens aperture, of diameter $D$, and whose tip is the image point, at $d_i$. If the screen or sensor is displaced by $\delta$ from the image plane, the point is spread into a disc of diameter $D\,\delta/d_i$ (similar triangles). For a given acceptable blur $b$ the screen may be anywhere within $\delta = b\,d_i/D$ of the image: a smaller aperture gives a proportionally larger **depth of focus**, at the price of less light. In PHY 300 this is measured by comparing a small and a large aperture in front of the same lens.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 12*

^rem-a5-4-5

> [!example] Example §A5.4.4: Why a Phone Camera Needs No Focusing
> A phone camera has a fixed lens of focal length about $5$ mm, at a fixed distance from the sensor. For a selfie at arm's length, $d_o = 500$ mm:
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{d_i} = \frac{1}{5\ \mathrm{mm}} - \frac{1}{500\ \mathrm{mm}}, \qquad d_i = 5.05\ \mathrm{mm} ;
> $$
>
> for a person $5$ m away, $d_i = 5.005$ mm, and for a very distant object $d_i = f = 5.000$ mm. Because every object distance is at least a hundred times the focal length, all images lie within about $50\ \mu\mathrm m$ of the focal plane, less than the thickness of a sheet of paper, and a sensor fixed at the focal plane records them all sharply enough.
>
> *Source: UP3 §2.6*
