Level A stated that a chain of $N$ beads has exactly $N$ normal modes, whose frequencies approach those of a string ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^rem-a2-6-3|Remark: Resonance and general motion]], [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^rem-a2-3-1|Remark: From beads to a string]]). This section proves it. The method of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators|§B2.1]] applies unchanged, an eigenvalue problem for a symmetric matrix, but an $N \times N$ determinant cannot be expanded by hand. The way out is the exponential ansatz used for oscillators in time, now applied *in space*: it gives the dispersion relation $\omega = 2\omega_0\sin(ka/2)$, and the fixed ends then select the $N$ modes, which turn out to be sampled standing waves.

## The chain and its equations

> [!model] Model §B3.1.1: The Bead Chain
> $N$ identical point masses $m$ have equilibrium positions $x_j = ja$, $j = 1, \dots, N$, between fixed walls at $x_0 = 0$ and $x_{N+1} = (N + 1)a = L$; $a$ is the **lattice spacing**. Two versions:
> - **longitudinal chain:** neighbouring masses, and the end masses and the walls, are joined by $N + 1$ identical ideal springs of constant $k$; $y_j$ is the displacement of mass $j$ along the line;
> - **beaded string:** the masses are threaded on a massless string under tension $T$; $y_j$ is the transverse displacement of mass $j$, small enough that every segment makes a small angle with the axis, the tension keeps its value $T$, and the masses do not move along the axis (as for the ideal string, [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^mod-a2-3-1|Model §A2.3.1]]).
>
> No damping, no gravity.
>
> *Source: Series I §8 and §9.1 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4 · PHY 287, week 12 N-coupled-oscillator slides*

^mod-b3-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.2: Equations of Motion of the Chain
> Both versions of the chain obey
>
> $$
> \ddot y_j = \omega_0^2\bigl(y_{j-1} - 2y_j + y_{j+1}\bigr), \qquad j = 1, \dots, N, \qquad y_0(t) = y_{N+1}(t) = 0,
> $$
>
> with
>
> $$
> \omega_0^2 = \frac km \quad\text{(longitudinal)}, \qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{T}{ma} \quad\text{(beaded string)} .
> $$
>
> The **phantom sites** $j = 0$ and $j = N + 1$, held at zero, encode the fixed walls. In matrix form $\ddot{\vec y} = -\mathsf A_N\vec y$, where $\mathsf A_N = \omega_0^2\,\mathsf T_N$ and $\mathsf T_N$ is the tridiagonal matrix with $2$ on the diagonal and $-1$ on the two neighbouring diagonals. $\mathsf A_N$ is real, symmetric and positive definite, so by [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]] the chain has $N$ normal modes with positive frequencies and orthogonal mode shapes.
>
> *Source: Series I §8.1–8.2, §9.1 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §4.1 · PHY 287, week 12 N-coupled-oscillator slides*

^thm-b3-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Longitudinal.* The spring between masses $j$ and $j + 1$ has extension $y_{j+1} - y_j$; it pulls mass $j$ with $+k(y_{j+1} - y_j)$ and mass $j + 1$ with $-k(y_{j+1} - y_j)$, exactly as the coupling spring of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]. So mass $j$ feels $k(y_{j+1} - y_j) + k(y_{j-1} - y_j)$. The wall spring on mass 1 has extension $y_1$ and exerts $-ky_1 = k(y_0 - y_1)$ with $y_0 = 0$, and likewise at the far end: with the phantom sites, one formula covers every row. Divide Newton's second law by $m$.
>
> *Beaded string.* The segment from bead $j$ to bead $j + 1$ makes an angle $\varphi$ with the axis, $\tan\varphi = (y_{j+1} - y_j)/a$, and pulls bead $j$ along itself with tension $T$. Its transverse component is $T\sin\varphi \approx T\tan\varphi = (T/a)(y_{j+1} - y_j)$, with an error of relative order $\varphi^2$, and to the same order the longitudinal components $T\cos\varphi \approx T$ of the two segments cancel. With the left segment, the transverse force on bead $j$ is $(T/a)(y_{j-1} - 2y_j + y_{j+1})$: the longitudinal result with $k \to T/a$.
>
> The matrix has $K_{ij} = K_{ji}$ for a chain of equal springs, and every mass is linked to a wall through the chain, so [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]] applies. For $N = 2$ the equations are those of the coupled pair with $k_c = k$, $\omega_c = \omega_0$.

> [!caution] Caution: Letters
> - From here on $k$ without subscript is a **wavenumber** ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-2|Def. §A2.2.2]]), as in the dispersion relation; the spring constant enters only through $\omega_0^2 = k/m$, which is used instead. Schwartz flags the same clash.
> - The sources name things differently: the series writes the spacing $a$ and the phase step per site $\theta = ka$; PHY 287 writes the spacing $l$ and labels the masses by $p$; Schwartz writes the spacing $\Delta x$, labels the masses by $n$ and calls the dimensionless phase step $p$ the "wavenumber".
> - $\omega_0$ is the frequency of one mass on one spring, not a frequency of the chain: the chain's frequencies run from about $\pi\omega_0/(N + 1)$ up to almost $2\omega_0$.

## The dispersion relation

> [!definition] Definition §B3.1.1: Dispersion Relation
> In a medium whose equations of motion are the same at every site (or every point), look for sinusoidal travelling waves $y = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat A\,e^{i(\omega t - kx)}\bigr]$, with wavenumber $k$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-2|Def. §A2.2.2]]) and complex amplitude $\hat A$; on a lattice $x$ takes only the values $x_j = ja$. The equations of motion allow such a wave only if $\omega$ and $k$ satisfy a relation $\omega = \omega(k)$, the **dispersion relation** of the medium. The medium is **nondispersive** if $\omega/k$ is the same for all $k$, and **dispersive** otherwise; what $\omega/k$ and $d\omega/dk$ mean physically is the subject of [[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^def-b6-2-1|Def. §B6.2.1]].
>
> *Source: Series I §8.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §4.3*

^def-b3-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.3: Dispersion Relation of the Chain
> $y_j = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat A\,e^{i(\omega t - kja)}\bigr]$ with $\hat A \ne 0$ solves the interior equation $\ddot y_j = \omega_0^2(y_{j-1} - 2y_j + y_{j+1})$ for every $j$ if and only if
>
> $$
> \omega^2 = 4\omega_0^2\sin^2\frac{ka}{2}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \omega = 2\omega_0\Bigl|\sin\frac{ka}{2}\Bigr| .
> $$
>
> - $\omega$ depends on $k$ only through $\cos ka$: $k$ and $-k$, and $k$ and $k + 2\pi/a$, give the same frequency (and $k$, $k + 2\pi/a$ give the same displacements at the sites).
> - There is a ceiling: $\omega \le 2\omega_0$, reached at $ka = \pi$, where neighbours move in antiphase.
> - For long waves, $ka \ll 1$: $\omega \approx \omega_0a\,|k|$, linear in $k$. The chain is dispersive, but long waves hardly notice.
>
> *Source: Series I §8.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §4.3 · PHY 287, week 12 N-coupled-oscillator slides*

^thm-b3-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Work with the complex solution $\tilde y_j = \hat Ae^{i(\omega t - kja)}$; the real part of a complex solution of a real linear equation is a solution ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]). Substituting, $\ddot{\tilde y}_j = -\omega^2\tilde y_j$ and $\tilde y_{j\pm1} = e^{\mp ika}\tilde y_j$, so the equation becomes, after dividing by $\tilde y_j \ne 0$,
>
> $$
> -\omega^2 = \omega_0^2\bigl(e^{ika} - 2 + e^{-ika}\bigr) = -2\omega_0^2\,(1 - \cos ka) = -4\omega_0^2\sin^2\frac{ka}{2},
> $$
>
> by $e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta} = 2\cos\theta$ and $1 - \cos\theta = 2\sin^2(\theta/2)$. The listed properties follow from the formula; for small $ka$, $\sin(ka/2) = ka/2 + O\bigl((ka)^3\bigr)$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).

^der-b3-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]], [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: An exponential ansatz in space
> The interior equation of motion, read for a single frequency, is a linear recursion in the site index $j$ with constant coefficients, the discrete analogue of a linear differential equation with constant coefficients in $t$. The same move works: try a geometric sequence $e^{-ijka}$, as one tries $e^{\lambda t}$ in time. *Every* $k$ solves the interior equation, with its own $\omega$, just as every root of the characteristic equation gives a solution in time. What selects the allowed values is the boundary, here the walls, exactly as initial conditions select the constants in time. Only a translation-invariant system, the same at every site, admits this ansatz; that is why the bulk equations had to be identical and the ends were rewritten with phantom sites.
>
> *Source: Series I §8.3*

## Normal modes of the fixed-end chain

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.4: Normal Modes of the Fixed-End Chain
> The chain of [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]] has exactly $N$ normal modes, $n = 1, \dots, N$:
>
> $$
> v_j^{(n)} = \sin\frac{jn\pi}{N + 1} = \sin(k_nx_j), \qquad k_n = \frac{n\pi}{(N + 1)a} = \frac{n\pi}{L}, \qquad \omega_n = 2\omega_0\sin\frac{n\pi}{2(N + 1)} .
> $$
>
> The frequencies are distinct and increase with $n$, all below $2\omega_0$. Mode $n$ samples, at the sites, the standing wave $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ that fits $n$ half-wavelengths between the walls ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]]). The mode shapes are orthogonal, with
>
> $$
> \sum_{j=1}^{N}\sin\frac{jn\pi}{N + 1}\,\sin\frac{jn'\pi}{N + 1} = \frac{N + 1}{2}\,\delta_{nn'} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Series I §8.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §4.2–4.4 · PHY 287, week 12 N-coupled-oscillator slides; week 13 N-very-large slides · PHY 300, Worksheet 4a*

^thm-b3-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A normal mode $\vec y = \vec v\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ needs $\mathsf A_N\vec v = \omega^2\vec v$ ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]]), which reads row by row
>
> $$
> \omega_0^2\bigl(2v_j - v_{j-1} - v_{j+1}\bigr) = \omega^2v_j, \qquad j = 1, \dots, N, \qquad v_0 = v_{N+1} = 0 .
> $$
>
> *Interior.* By [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]], with $\theta = ka$, both $e^{ij\theta}$ and $e^{-ij\theta}$ solve this recursion with the same $\omega^2 = 4\omega_0^2\sin^2(\theta/2)$, so does any combination, in particular $v_j = (e^{ij\theta} - e^{-ij\theta})/2i = \sin j\theta$, which has $v_0 = 0$.
>
> *Far wall.* $v_{N+1} = \sin\bigl((N + 1)\theta\bigr) = 0$ forces $\theta = \theta_n = n\pi/(N + 1)$ with $n$ an integer. $n = 0$ and $n = N + 1$ give $\vec v = \vec 0$; $n' = 2(N + 1) - n$ gives $\sin j\theta_{n'} = -\sin j\theta_n$, the same mode; and $n \mapsto n + 2(N + 1)$ changes nothing. So $n = 1, \dots, N$ remain. On this range $\theta_n/2 \in (0, \pi/2)$, where $\sin$ is increasing, so the $N$ frequencies $\omega_n$ are distinct. An $N \times N$ matrix has at most $N$ eigenvalues ([[§14 Invariant Subspaces#^ladr-5-12|LADR Thm. 5.12]]), so these are all of them, each with a one-dimensional space of mode shapes: no mode has been missed, and no determinant was expanded. The phantom sites make the end rows part of the recursion, so the end equations hold too.
>
> *Orthogonality.* Eigenvectors of the symmetric $\mathsf A_N$ with distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]]). For the norm, $\sum_j\sin^2 j\theta_n = \tfrac12\sum_{j=1}^N(1 - \cos 2j\theta_n)$. With $\varphi = 2\theta_n$, the geometric sum $\sum_{j=0}^{N}e^{ij\varphi} = (1 - e^{i(N+1)\varphi})/(1 - e^{i\varphi}) = 0$, since $e^{i(N+1)\varphi} = e^{2\pi in} = 1$ and $e^{i\varphi} \ne 1$ for $1 \le n \le N$. Taking real parts, $\sum_{j=1}^N\cos j\varphi = -1$, so $\sum_j\sin^2j\theta_n = \tfrac12(N + 1)$.

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the modes
> - **Checks.** For $N = 2$, $\theta_n = \pi/3, 2\pi/3$ give $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2, 3\omega_0^2$ with shapes $\propto(1, 1), (1, -1)$: the coupled pair with $k_c = k$ ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]). For $N = 3$, $\omega^2 = (2 - \sqrt2)\,\omega_0^2,\ 2\omega_0^2,\ (2 + \sqrt2)\,\omega_0^2$ with shapes $(1, \sqrt2, 1)$, $(1, 0, -1)$, $(1, -\sqrt2, 1)$, which Schwartz finds numerically.
> - **Low modes** ($n \ll N$) are long, smooth waves that hardly feel the graininess, and $\omega_n \approx \omega_0\,n\pi/(N + 1)$: equally spaced frequencies, the harmonic series of a string.
> - **High modes** alternate in sign from mass to mass. Since $\sin\bigl(j(\pi - \theta)\bigr) = (-1)^{j+1}\sin j\theta$, mode $N + 1 - n$ is mode $n$ with every other mass flipped. Near the top every spring is worked almost maximally, the frequencies crowd together below $2\omega_0$, and nothing faster exists: the ceiling is the memory of the graininess, which a continuous string does not have.
>
> *Source: Series I §8.3 (Remark: reading the mode shapes) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §2, §4.3 · PHY 287, week 13 N-very-large slides*

^rem-b3-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.1.5: General Motion of the Chain
> Every motion of the fixed-end chain is a superposition of its $N$ modes,
>
> $$
> y_j(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\sin\frac{jn\pi}{N + 1}\,\bigl(a_n\cos\omega_nt + b_n\sin\omega_nt\bigr),
> $$
>
> with the coefficients given by the initial displacements and velocities:
>
> $$
> a_n = \frac{2}{N + 1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}y_j(0)\,\sin\frac{jn\pi}{N + 1}, \qquad b_n = \frac{2}{(N + 1)\,\omega_n}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\dot y_j(0)\,\sin\frac{jn\pi}{N + 1} .
> $$
>
> This is a discrete Fourier sine expansion; its limit for a continuous string is the Fourier sine series ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-7|Theorem §B4.1.7]]).
>
> *Source: Series I §9.4 (discrete orthogonality) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §4.4 · PHY 287, week 13 N-very-large slides*

^thm-b3-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]] with $\mathsf M = m\mathbb 1$ and the mode shapes of [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]], for which $\mu_n = m\sum_j\sin^2(jn\pi/(N + 1)) = m(N + 1)/2$. The factor $m$ cancels between numerator and denominator of the projection.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.1.1: A Chain of 49 Oscillators
> In a fixed-end chain of $N = 49$ coupled oscillators the 10th normal mode has angular frequency $20\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Find the frequency of the 15th mode, and, if mass 5 has amplitude $0.1\ \mathrm m$ in the 15th mode, the amplitude of mass 6 in that mode.
>
> By [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]] with $N + 1 = 50$, $\omega_n = 2\omega_0\sin(n\pi/100)$, so $\omega_0$ cancels in the ratio:
>
> $$
> \omega_{15} = \omega_{10}\,\frac{\sin 27^\circ}{\sin 18^\circ} = (20\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})\,\frac{0.4540}{0.3090} = 29.4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} \qquad (\omega_0 = 32.4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}).
> $$
>
> The string's harmonic series would give $\tfrac{15}{10}\cdot 20 = 30\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$; the 2% shortfall is the chain's dispersion, since $n/(N + 1) = 0.3$ is not small.
>
> The shape of mode 15 is $v_j = \sin(15j\pi/50) = \sin(0.3\pi j)$: $v_5 = \sin(1.5\pi) = -1$ and $v_6 = \sin(1.8\pi) = -0.588$. Mass 5 sits at an extremum of the sampled wave ($|v_5| = 1$), so the mode's overall amplitude is $0.1\ \mathrm m$, and mass 6 moves in phase with it with amplitude $0.588 \times 0.1\ \mathrm m = 0.059\ \mathrm m$.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Midterm 1 practice*

^ex-b3-1-1

> [!example] Example §B3.1.2: An LC Ladder
> PHY 300's electrical analogue of the chain is a ladder: inductors $L$ in series along a line, joining nodes $p = 1, \dots, N$, and a capacitor $C$ from each node to ground. Let $V_p$ be the node voltages and $I_p$ the current in the inductor from node $p$ to node $p + 1$. The loop rule across that inductor ([[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-2|EM Theorem §A6.3.2]]) and the junction rule at node $p$ ([[§A6.3 Kirchhoff's Rules#^thm-a6-3-1|EM Theorem §A6.3.1]]) give
>
> $$
> L\,\frac{dI_p}{dt} = V_p - V_{p+1}, \qquad C\,\frac{dV_p}{dt} = I_{p-1} - I_p \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot V_p = \frac{1}{LC}\bigl(V_{p-1} - 2V_p + V_{p+1}\bigr),
> $$
>
> the chain equation with $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, the frequency of a single LC circuit ([[§A10.5 LC Oscillations#^thm-a10-5-4|EM Theorem §A10.5.4]]). Shorting the line at both ends, $V_0 = V_{N+1} = 0$, plays the part of the walls.
>
> With $L = 100\ \mu\mathrm H$ and $C = 400\ \mathrm{pF}$: $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{4.0 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm{s^2}} = 5.0 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $f_0 = \omega_0/2\pi = 796\ \mathrm{kHz}$. For $N = 12$ sections (the number in the worksheet's sketch), $f_n = 2f_0\sin(n\pi/26)$:
>
> $$
> f_1 = 192\ \mathrm{kHz}, \qquad f_2 = 381\ \mathrm{kHz}, \qquad f_3 = 564\ \mathrm{kHz}, \qquad \dots, \qquad f_{11} = 1545\ \mathrm{kHz}, \quad f_{12} = 1580\ \mathrm{kHz},
> $$
>
> nearly equally spaced at the bottom and crowding below $2f_0 = 1592\ \mathrm{kHz}$ at the top. In the highest mode neighbouring nodes are nearly in antiphase. Where to measure matters: in mode 2 the voltage amplitude along the line is $\propto\sin(2p\pi/13)$, which is $0.99$ of the maximum at $p = 3$ but only $0.24$ at the middle nodes $p = 6, 7$, close to the mode's node at the centre.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 4a*

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The counting argument uses [[§14 Invariant Subspaces#^ladr-5-12|LADR Thm. 5.12]]; orthogonality of the modes is the spectral theorem for the symmetric matrix $\mathsf A_N$ ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]), here verified by a geometric sum.
> - $\mathsf T_N$ is the second-difference matrix: [[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation]] shows it becomes $-a^2\,\partial^2/\partial x^2$, and the chain becomes the string.
> - Dispersion, phase and group velocity for the chain's travelling waves: [[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity]]. Quantised, the chain's modes are the phonons of a crystal (each a quantum oscillator, [[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|QM Theorem §A4.5.3]]).
> - The chain's dispersion, with its ceiling $2\omega_0$, in the heat capacity of real solids, where Debye's cutoff mimics the ceiling: [[§B11.2★ Phonons and the Debye Model#^rem-b11-2-3|TH Remark: Real solids]].
> - Quantum side: the same fixed phase per lattice cell (Theorem §B3.1.3) for an electron in a periodic potential is Bloch's theorem — [[§B7.3★ Solids, Free Electrons and Band Structure#^thm-b7-3-4|QM Theorem §B7.3.4]].
> - Quantum Mechanics level C: Bloch's theorem as a consequence of the lattice translation symmetry, with the phase per cell of Theorem §B3.1.3 as the eigenvalue of the translation operator — [[§C8.2★ Parity and Lattice Translations#^thm-c8-2-8|QM Theorem §C8.2.8]].
