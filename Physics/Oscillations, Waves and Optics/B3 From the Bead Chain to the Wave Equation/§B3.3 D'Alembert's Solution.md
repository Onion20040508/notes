Level A showed that any shape moving rigidly at speed $c$ solves the wave equation ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-5|Theorem §A2.2.5]]) and announced the converse ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^rem-a2-2-2|Remark: Why this equation matters]]). This section proves it: on an unbounded string every solution is a right-moving shape plus a left-moving shape. From the initial displacement and velocity the two shapes are found explicitly, which shows that the motion is unique, that disturbances travel at exactly $c$, and why a pulse let go from rest splits in two. The standing waves of [[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation|§B3.2]] and the travelling waves of this section turn out to be two bases for the same solutions, and odd reflection turns the formula for the infinite line into one for a string with fixed ends.

## Every solution travels

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.1: D'Alembert's General Solution
> Let $y(x, t)$ be twice continuously differentiable for all real $x$ and $t$ and satisfy the wave equation $\partial_t^2y = c^2\,\partial_x^2y$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-4|Def. §A2.2.4]]). Then there are twice differentiable functions $f$ and $g$ of one variable such that
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = f(x - ct) + g(x + ct) ,
> $$
>
> a shape $f$ moving rigidly toward $+x$ and a shape $g$ moving toward $-x$, both at speed $c$. Conversely, every such sum is a solution ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-5|Theorem §A2.2.5]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-6|Theorem §A2.2.6]]). The pair $(f, g)$ is unique up to $(f + C, g - C)$ for a constant $C$.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.5 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §6 · PHY 287, week 13 wave-equation slides; Problem Set 10*

^thm-b3-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The wave operator factors, $\partial_t^2 - c^2\partial_x^2 = (\partial_t - c\,\partial_x)(\partial_t + c\,\partial_x)$, and each factor kills one kind of travelling wave: $(\partial_t + c\,\partial_x)f(x - ct) = -cf' + cf' = 0$. This suggests the **characteristic variables**
>
> $$
> \xi = x - ct, \qquad \eta = x + ct \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad x = \frac{\xi + \eta}{2}, \quad t = \frac{\eta - \xi}{2c} .
> $$
>
> Let $Y(\xi, \eta) = y(x, t)$. By the chain rule ([[§12 Composition of Functions and the Chain Rule#^thm-12-2|452 Thm. §12.2]]), $\partial_\eta = \tfrac12\partial_x + \tfrac1{2c}\partial_t$ and $\partial_\xi = \tfrac12\partial_x - \tfrac1{2c}\partial_t$. Since $y$ is $C^2$, the mixed partials of $y$ agree ([[§6 Equality of Mixed Partials#^thm-6-1|452 Thm. §6.1]]) and
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2Y}{\partial\xi\,\partial\eta} = \frac14\,\partial_x^2y - \frac1{4c^2}\,\partial_t^2y = -\frac{1}{4c^2}\bigl(\partial_t^2y - c^2\partial_x^2y\bigr) = 0 .
> $$
>
> So $\partial Y/\partial\eta$ has zero derivative in $\xi$ and, for each fixed $\eta$, is constant in $\xi$ ([[§29 The Mean Value Theorem#^cor-29-4|451 Cor. §29.4]]): $\partial Y/\partial\eta = h(\eta)$, with $h$ continuous. Let $G$ be an antiderivative of $h$ ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-4|451 Thm. §34.4]]). Then $Y - G(\eta)$ has zero derivative in $\eta$, so it is a function $F(\xi)$ alone, and $y = F(x - ct) + G(x + ct)$. Both are twice differentiable: $F(\xi) = Y(\xi, 0) - G(0)$ and $G(\eta) = Y(0, \eta) - F(0)$ are restrictions of the $C^2$ function $Y$. If also $y = \tilde F(\xi) + \tilde G(\eta)$, then $F - \tilde F = \tilde G - G$ is a function of $\xi$ alone and of $\eta$ alone, hence a constant.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.2: D'Alembert's Formula
> On the infinite line, the wave equation with initial displacement $y(x, 0) = y_0(x)$ (twice continuously differentiable) and initial velocity $\partial_ty(x, 0) = v_0(x)$ (continuously differentiable) has exactly one $C^2$ solution,
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = \frac12\bigl[y_0(x - ct) + y_0(x + ct)\bigr] + \frac1{2c}\int_{x - ct}^{x + ct}v_0(s)\,ds .
> $$
>
> 1. **Release from rest** ($v_0 = 0$): the initial shape splits into two copies of half its height, moving apart at speed $c$.
> 2. **Finite speed:** $y(x, t)$ depends only on the data in the interval $[x - ct, x + ct]$. A disturbance confined to a region reaches a point at distance $d$ after exactly the time $d/c$, never earlier.
> 3. **No change of shape:** each travelling part keeps its form; the string, unlike the bead chain, is nondispersive ([[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]).
>
> *Source: derived here from Series I §9.5, which gives the general solution but not the initial-value form*

^thm-b3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]] any solution is $f(x - ct) + g(x + ct)$. At $t = 0$:
>
> $$
> f(x) + g(x) = y_0(x), \qquad -cf'(x) + cg'(x) = v_0(x) .
> $$
>
> Integrating the second from $0$ to $x$ ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]) gives $g(x) - f(x) = \frac1c\int_0^xv_0(s)\,ds + K$ with a constant $K$. Solving the two linear equations,
>
> $$
> f(x) = \frac12y_0(x) - \frac1{2c}\int_0^xv_0 - \frac K2, \qquad g(x) = \frac12y_0(x) + \frac1{2c}\int_0^xv_0 + \frac K2 .
> $$
>
> Then $f(x - ct) + g(x + ct)$ is the stated formula: $K$ cancels and $\int_0^{x+ct} - \int_0^{x-ct} = \int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}$. Since every solution has this form and the formula is fixed by the data, the solution is unique. Conversely, with $y_0 \in C^2$ and $v_0 \in C^1$, $f$ and $g$ are $C^2$ ([[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-4|451 Thm. §34.4]]), so the formula is a solution with the right initial values. Properties (1)–(3) can be read off the formula.

^der-b3-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution#^thm-b3-3-1|Theorem §B3.3.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-4|451 Thm. §34.4]]

> [!caution] Caution: The two waves are not the initial shape
> - Plucking a long string does not send the plucked shape off in one direction: it sends half of it each way. Even a release from rest needs both $f$ and $g$, each equal to $\tfrac12y_0$ up to a constant.
> - A shape travels unchanged only on a nondispersive medium. On the bead chain a pulse made of short wavelengths spreads, because its components travel at different speeds ([[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation#^rem-b3-2-2|Remark: The correction term is the chain's dispersion]]; [[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity]]); $f(x - ct) + g(x + ct)$ is the long-wavelength description.

## Two bases, and fixed ends

> [!remark] Remark: Standing waves and travelling waves
> Each normal mode of the string is a pair of opposite travelling waves:
>
> $$
> \sin(kx)\cos(\omega t) = \tfrac12\bigl[\sin k(x - ct) + \sin k(x + ct)\bigr], \qquad \omega = ck,
> $$
>
> the identity behind [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-1|Theorem §A2.6.1]] read backwards; for mode $n$ of a string of length $L$ the speed is $c = \omega_nL/n\pi$. Conversely a travelling pulse is a superposition of standing waves (Fourier analysis; on a string with fixed ends, [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]]). So the modes and the travelling waves are two bases for one space of solutions, as in [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange|§B2.2]] the blocks and the modes were two bases for the coupled pair. Which one to use depends on the problem: the mode basis fits fixed ends and a single frequency, the travelling basis fits pulses and the open line. Whether a standing or a travelling wave is produced depends on the initial conditions.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.5 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §6 · PHY 287, week 13 wave-equation slides · PHY 300, Worksheet 6*

^rem-b3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.3.3: Fixed Ends by Odd Extension
> 1. **One fixed end.** On the half-line $x \ge 0$ with $y(0, t) = 0$, and data $y_0$, $v_0$ compatible with the fixed end ($y_0$, $y_0''$ and $v_0$ vanish at $x = 0$), the solution is d'Alembert's formula applied to the odd extensions $y_0(-x) = -y_0(x)$, $v_0(-x) = -v_0(x)$. A pulse that reaches the end returns inverted, with the same shape and size.
> 2. **Two fixed ends.** For a string fixed at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ (data compatible at both ends), the solution is d'Alembert's formula with the extensions that are odd about $x = 0$ and $2L$-periodic. The motion is periodic in time with period $2L/c$, the period of the fundamental ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]]).
>
> Part 1 proves the reflection rule of [[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-3|Theorem §A2.5.3]] for a fixed end, together with uniqueness.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.5 (the method of reflections is mentioned there and not developed); derived here*

^thm-b3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Odd data give odd solutions.* Let $Y_0$, $V_0$ be odd and $u$ be given by d'Alembert's formula. Then $u(-x, t) = \tfrac12[Y_0(-x - ct) + Y_0(-x + ct)] + \tfrac1{2c}\int_{-x-ct}^{-x+ct}V_0(s)\,ds$. The first bracket is $-\bigl[Y_0(x + ct) + Y_0(x - ct)\bigr]$; substituting $s = -s'$, the integral becomes $\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}V_0(-s')\,ds' = -\int_{x-ct}^{x+ct}V_0$. So $u(-x, t) = -u(x, t)$, and in particular $u(0, t) = 0$ for all $t$.
>
> *Part 1.* The compatibility conditions make the odd extensions $C^2$ and $C^1$, so $u$ is a $C^2$ solution on the whole line ([[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]]) that vanishes at $x = 0$; its restriction to $x \ge 0$ solves the problem. Conversely, any solution on the half-line with $y(0, t) = 0$, continued oddly, is a solution on the whole line with the odd data, so it equals $u$: the solution is unique. For the reflection, write $u = F(x - ct) + G(x + ct)$. A pulse $G(x + ct)$ approaching the end from $x > 0$ comes with its image $F(\xi) = -G(-\xi)$, an inverted mirror copy approaching from $x < 0$. After the pulse has passed $x = 0$, only the image is left on $x > 0$: the reflected pulse $-G(-(x - ct))$, upside down and moving away.
>
> *Part 2.* An extension odd about $0$ and $2L$-periodic is also odd about $L$: $Y_0(L + x) = Y_0(x - L) = -Y_0(L - x)$. The argument above, centred at $L$, gives $u(L, t) = 0$. Shifting $t$ by $2L/c$ shifts the arguments $x \mp ct$ by $\mp 2L$, which leaves the $y_0$ terms unchanged, and adds to the integral $\int_{x - ct - 2L}^{x - ct}V_0 + \int_{x + ct}^{x + ct + 2L}V_0$, two integrals of the odd, $2L$-periodic $V_0$ over a full period, each $\int_{-L}^{L}V_0 = 0$. So $u(x, t + 2L/c) = u(x, t)$. This is $2\pi/\omega_1$ with $\omega_1 = \pi c/L$.

> [!remark] Remark: The chain and the string, side by side
> The whole of this chapter in one table:
>
> | | chain of $N$ masses | continuous string |
> |---|---|---|
> | configuration | vector $(y_1, \dots, y_N)$ | field $y(x, t)$ |
> | dynamics | $\ddot{\vec y} = -\mathsf A_N\vec y$ | $\partial_t^2y = c^2\partial_x^2y$ |
> | coupling | tridiagonal matrix $\mathsf A_N$ | operator $-c^2\partial_x^2$ |
> | second difference | $y_{j-1} - 2y_j + y_{j+1}$ | $a^2\,\partial_x^2y$ |
> | fixed ends | phantom sites $y_0 = y_{N+1} = 0$ | $y(0, t) = y(L, t) = 0$ |
> | mode label | $ka = n\pi/(N + 1)$, $n = 1, \dots, N$ | $k = n\pi/L$, $n = 1, 2, \dots$ |
> | mode shape | $\sin(jn\pi/(N + 1))$ | $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ |
> | inner product | $\sum_j u_jw_j$ | $\int_0^L u(x)w(x)\,dx$ |
> | initial data | expansion in eigenvectors | Fourier sine series |
> | dispersion | $\omega = 2\omega_0\sin(ka/2)$, ceiling $2\omega_0$ | $\omega = ck$, no ceiling |
> | travelling waves | spread (dispersive) | keep their shape (d'Alembert) |
>
> One ansatz, exponential in time and then in space, and one change of basis carried the subject from two masses on springs to the wave equation.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.6 (last row added)*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B3.3.1: A Bump Let Go from Rest
> A long string is held in the shape $y_0(x) = he^{-x^2/w^2}$ and released from rest. By [[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]] with $v_0 = 0$,
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = \frac h2\,e^{-(x - ct)^2/w^2} + \frac h2\,e^{-(x + ct)^2/w^2} .
> $$
>
> At first the two halves overlap and the bump just sinks and broadens; once $ct$ exceeds $w/\sqrt2 \approx 0.7\,w$, when the inner inflection point of each half has moved past the centre, a dip appears there, and for $ct \gg w$ two separate bumps of height $h/2$ run apart at speed $c$, leaving the string between them straight and at rest. The energy divides equally between them. A point at distance $d$ from the centre stays at rest until $t \approx (d - 2w)/c$: before that, the data that can influence it, those in $[d - ct, d + ct]$, are negligibly small.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.5 (travelling waves); worked here from Theorem §B3.3.2*

> [!example] Example §B3.3.2: A Struck String
> A long string at rest and straight is struck so that the piece $|x| < b$ starts with velocity $V$ and the rest at rest: $y_0 = 0$, $v_0 = V$ on $|x| < b$, $0$ outside. (This $v_0$ is discontinuous, so the result is a solution in a generalised sense; it is the limit of smooth data.) By [[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution#^thm-b3-3-2|Theorem §B3.3.2]] only the integral term survives:
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = \frac{V}{2c}\,\Bigl|\,[x - ct,\ x + ct] \cap [-b, b]\,\Bigr| ,
> $$
>
> $V/2c$ times the length of the overlap of the two intervals. For $ct < b$ the profile is a trapezoid rising in the struck region; for $ct > b$ it is a plateau of height $Vb/c$ over $|x| < ct - b$, with ramps of width $2b$ at its edges moving outward at speed $c$. Unlike a pluck, a strike does not leave the string where it was: behind the two outgoing fronts the string stays displaced by $Vb/c$, ever more of it. (The total transverse momentum, $2\mu bV$, is conserved, and a displaced but straight string carries none; on a string with fixed ends the reflected, inverted fronts undo the displacement, and the motion repeats after $2L/c$ by [[§B3.3 D'Alembert's Solution#^thm-b3-3-3|Theorem §B3.3.3]].)
>
> *Source: worked here from Theorem §B3.3.2*
