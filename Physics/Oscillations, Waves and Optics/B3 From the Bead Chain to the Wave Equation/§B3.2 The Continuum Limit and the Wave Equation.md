At level A the wave equation ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-4|Def. §A2.2.4]]) was derived for a string by applying Newton's second law to a short element of an already continuous string ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]), and the route through beads was only sketched ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^rem-a2-3-1|Remark: From beads to a string]]). This section takes that route properly. Refine the chain of [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain|§B3.1]] while keeping the physical string fixed: the second difference becomes a second derivative, the $N$ ordinary differential equations become one partial differential equation, and the chain's modes and dispersion relation become those of the string. Keeping the first correction term shows what the continuum description leaves out and when it may be trusted.

## Taking the limit

> [!definition] Definition §B3.2.1: Continuum Limit; Displacement Field
> The **continuum limit** of the beaded string ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^mod-b3-1-1|Model §B3.1.1]]) lets
>
> $$
> a \to 0, \qquad N \to \infty, \qquad\text{with}\quad L = (N + 1)\,a, \quad \mu = \frac ma, \quad T \quad\text{held fixed},
> $$
>
> so each bead's mass $m = \mu a$ tends to zero while the length, the mass per length ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^def-a2-3-1|Def. §A2.3.1]]) and the tension stay those of the physical string. (For the longitudinal chain, hold $L$, $\mu$ and $ka$ fixed, with $k$ the spring constant; Schwartz calls $ka$ the elastic modulus.) The displacements are taken to be the values at the sites of a smooth function, the **displacement field** $y(x, t)$:
>
> $$
> y_j(t) = y(x_j, t), \qquad x_j = ja .
> $$
>
> *Source: Series I §9.2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §5 · PHY 287, week 13 N-very-large slides*

^def-b3-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.1: The Wave Equation as the Limit of the Chain
> If the displacement field is smooth (six times continuously differentiable in $x$), the chain equation of [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-2|Theorem §B3.1.2]] reads
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2} = c^2\,\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2} + \frac{c^2a^2}{12}\,\frac{\partial^4y}{\partial x^4} + O(a^4), \qquad c^2 = \omega_0^2a^2 = \frac T\mu \ \ \text{(beaded string)}, \quad c^2 = \frac{ka}{\mu}\ \ \text{(spring chain)} .
> $$
>
> In the continuum limit the correction vanishes, and the field obeys the **wave equation** with fixed ends:
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2} = c^2\,\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}, \qquad y(0, t) = y(L, t) = 0 .
> $$
>
> For the string, $c = \sqrt{T/\mu}$ is the wave speed $v = \sqrt{F_T/\mu}$ of [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]] (level A writes $v$ and $F_T$ for $c$ and $T$).
>
> *Source: Series I §9.2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §5 · PHY 287, week 13 N-very-large slides*

^thm-b3-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Expand the neighbours of site $j$ by Taylor's theorem in the spacing ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]), all derivatives at $(x_j, t)$:
>
> $$
> y_{j\pm1} = y(x_j \pm a, t) = y \pm a\,\partial_xy + \frac{a^2}{2}\,\partial_x^2y \pm \frac{a^3}{6}\,\partial_x^3y + \frac{a^4}{24}\,\partial_x^4y \pm \frac{a^5}{120}\,\partial_x^5y + O(a^6) .
> $$
>
> In the sum the odd powers cancel in pairs, so the **second difference** is
>
> $$
> y_{j+1} - 2y_j + y_{j-1} = a^2\,\partial_x^2y + \frac{a^4}{12}\,\partial_x^4y + O(a^6) .
> $$
>
> The combination that has run through the chain since its tridiagonal matrix was a discrete second derivative all along. Insert it into $\ddot y_j = \omega_0^2(y_{j-1} - 2y_j + y_{j+1})$ and use $\omega_0^2a^2 = c^2$: for the beaded string $\omega_0^2a^2 = Ta^2/(ma) = T/(m/a) = T/\mu$, and for the spring chain $\omega_0^2a^2 = ka^2/m = ka/\mu$. With $c$ fixed and $a \to 0$, the terms of order $a^2$ and beyond vanish. The phantom sites $y_0 = 0$ at $x_0 = 0$ and $y_{N+1} = 0$ at $x_{N+1} = L$ become the boundary conditions.

> [!remark] Remark: One equation, two derivations
> The layer stays the same, a theorem at both levels; what changes is the model it rests on. At level A the wave equation for a string is a theorem about a continuous medium, [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^mod-a2-3-1|Model §A2.3.1]], obtained from Newton's second law for an element: the net force is $F_T$ times a *difference of slopes* at the two ends, which becomes $F_T\,\partial^2y/\partial x^2\,\Delta x$ ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^der-a2-3-2|derivation of Theorem §A2.3.2]]; Schwartz derives it the same way in Lecture 6, §2). Here the string is not assumed: it is the limit of a system of point masses whose equations follow from Newton's laws (with the same small-slope approximation), and the same difference of slopes appears as the second difference. The two routes agree, and the second one adds two things: the ideal string is seen as an idealisation of a grainy medium, and the size of the error is known.
>
> The wave equation is the same whether the chain is transverse or longitudinal; only the expression for $c$ in terms of the microscopic constants changes. For a solid rod the longitudinal version gives the speed of sound in terms of Young's modulus; strings, rods and fluids are treated together in [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]].
>
> *Source: Series I §9.1–9.2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §5 and Lecture 6, §1–2*

^rem-b3-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: The limit needs smooth displacements
> The step $y_{j\pm1} = y(x_j \pm a, t)$ with a smooth $y$ is a postulate about the motion, not a consequence of the chain's equations. It is good when the displacement changes little from one bead to the next, that is, for wavelengths much longer than $a$. The chain's high modes, in which neighbouring beads move nearly oppositely ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^rem-b3-1-2|Remark: Reading the modes]]), have no smooth interpolating field, and the wave equation says nothing correct about them. The continuum description keeps only the long-wavelength part of the chain's dynamics.

^cau-b3-2-1

## What happens to the modes

> [!theorem] Theorem §B3.2.2: Limit of the Chain's Normal Modes
> Fix the mode number $n$ and take the continuum limit. Then, with $k_n = n\pi/L$:
> 1. the mode shapes become the standing waves of the string: $\sin\bigl(jn\pi/(N + 1)\bigr) = \sin(k_nx_j) \to \sin(n\pi x/L)$;
> 2. the frequencies become the harmonic series:
>
> $$
> \omega_n = 2\omega_0\sin\frac{k_na}{2} = ck_n\Bigl[1 - \frac{(k_na)^2}{24} + O\bigl((k_na)^4\bigr)\Bigr] \ \longrightarrow\ ck_n = \frac{n\pi c}{L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots;
> $$
>
> 3. the dispersion relation becomes linear, $\omega = ck$: the string is nondispersive ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^def-b3-1-1|Def. §B3.1.1]]);
> 4. the number of modes and the ceiling $2\omega_0 = 2c/a$ go to infinity.
>
> In the limit these are the normal modes of [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]], $f_n = \omega_n/2\pi = nc/2L$. For finite $N$ the fractional error of the $n$th frequency is about $(k_na)^2/24 = \bigl(n\pi/(N + 1)\bigr)^2/24$.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §4.3 and §6 · PHY 287, week 13 N-very-large slides*

^thm-b3-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Start from [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]]. Since $(N + 1)a = L$, $jn\pi/(N + 1) = (n\pi/L)(ja) = k_nx_j$, and $k_n$ does not depend on $a$: the phase step per site $k_na \to 0$, but the phase per unit length stays finite. The sites fill $[0, L]$, so the sampled sine becomes the function $\sin(k_nx)$. For the frequency, $\omega_0 = c/a$ and $\sin u = u - u^3/6 + O(u^5)$ with $u = k_na/2$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]):
>
> $$
> 2\omega_0\sin\frac{k_na}{2} = \frac{2c}{a}\Bigl[\frac{k_na}{2} - \frac{(k_na)^3}{48} + \dots\Bigr] = ck_n\Bigl[1 - \frac{(k_na)^2}{24} + \dots\Bigr] .
> $$
>
> Every fixed $n$ eventually satisfies $n \le N$, and $2\omega_0 = 2c/a$ grows without bound. With $c = \sqrt{T/\mu} = v$, $\omega_n/2\pi = n\,v/2L$.

*The chain's normal frequencies divided by those of the continuous string with the same length, tension and mass per length, $\omega_n/(n\pi c/L) = \sin u/u$ with $u = n\pi/2(N + 1)$, for $N = 3$, $10$ and $30$ (Theorem §B3.2.2). For a fixed mode the ratio tends to 1 as $N$ grows; for any $N$ the top modes fall short by up to $1 - 2/\pi = 36\%$.*

> [!remark] Remark: The correction term is the chain's dispersion
> The two corrections agree. Inserting $e^{i(\omega t - kx)}$ into the corrected equation of [[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation#^thm-b3-2-1|Theorem §B3.2.1]] gives $\omega^2 = c^2k^2 - c^2a^2k^4/12$, which is the expansion of the exact $\omega^2 = 4\omega_0^2\sin^2(ka/2) = 2\omega_0^2(1 - \cos ka)$ to order $k^4$. So the fourth-derivative term is the first sign of graininess, and it makes the medium dispersive: short waves are slower, and a pulse made of them spreads ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity]]). On the dispersion curve of [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-3|Theorem §B3.1.3]] the continuum keeps only the tangent at the origin. A real string reveals its atomic structure exactly where the ideal model has none, at wavelengths approaching the atomic spacing.
>
> *Source: Series I §9.3 (the tangent line and the escaping ceiling); the correction term is computed here*

^rem-b3-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Sums become integrals
> The chain's general motion is a finite sum over modes with coefficients given by projections ([[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-5|Theorem §B3.1.5]]). In the limit it becomes $y(x, t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sin(n\pi x/L)\bigl[A_n\cos\omega_nt + B_n\sin\omega_nt\bigr]$, and the discrete orthogonality, multiplied by the spacing $a$,
>
> $$
> \sum_{j=1}^{N}a\,\sin(k_nx_j)\sin(k_mx_j) = \frac{(N + 1)a}{2}\,\delta_{nm} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \int_0^L\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{m\pi x}{L}\,dx = \frac L2\,\delta_{nm} ,
> $$
>
> becomes the orthogonality of the string's modes, the key to the Fourier sine series. That every reasonable initial shape can be expanded this way (completeness, which linear algebra gave for free when $N$ was finite) is Fourier's theorem ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-6|Theorem §B4.1.6]]); the string's initial-value problem is solved by modes in [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]].
>
> *Source: Series I §9.4 · PHY 287, week 13 wave-equation slides*

## Example

> [!example] Example §B3.2.1: A String versus Three Beads
> **(a)** A string of length $L$ and mass $M$ under tension $T$ is fixed at both ends. With $c = \sqrt{T/\mu} = \sqrt{TL/M}$, its lowest three angular frequencies are $\omega_n = n\pi c/L$ ([[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation#^thm-b3-2-2|Theorem §B3.2.2]]):
>
> $$
> \omega_{1,2,3} = (3.14,\ 6.28,\ 9.42)\,\sqrt{T/ML} .
> $$
>
> **(b)** Replace it by three beads of mass $M/3$ at $L/4$, $L/2$, $3L/4$ on a massless string under the same tension. Then $N = 3$, $a = L/4$, $\omega_0^2 = T/(ma) = 12\,T/ML$, and by [[§B3.1 The N-Mass Chain#^thm-b3-1-4|Theorem §B3.1.4]] $\omega_n = 2\omega_0\sin(n\pi/8)$:
>
> $$
> \omega_{1,2,3} = (2.65,\ 4.90,\ 6.40)\,\sqrt{T/ML}, \qquad\text{that is } 0.844,\ 0.780,\ 0.679 \text{ times the string's.}
> $$
>
> **(c) Where the difference comes from.** Two effects multiply. Graininess gives the factor $\sin u/u$ of Theorem §B3.2.2 with $u = n\pi/8$: $0.975$, $0.900$, $0.784$. And the beads do not have the string's mass per length: the whole mass $M$ sits on three beads spaced $L/4$, so $m/a = 4M/3L$, and $c$ is smaller by $\sqrt{3/4} = 0.866$. Indeed $0.975 \times 0.866 = 0.844$, and so on. The bookkeeping matters more than the graininess for the fundamental. Splitting $M$ into $N$ equal beads, the fundamental comes within 1% of the string's only for $N \ge 51$; giving each bead the mass $\mu a$ of one segment instead (total $NM/(N + 1)$, the convention of [[§B3.2 The Continuum Limit and the Wave Equation#^def-b3-2-1|Def. §B3.2.1]]) removes the second factor, and $N = 6$ beads already suffice: $(14/\pi)\sin(\pi/14) = 0.992$.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 10 (parts a, b); part (c) added here*
