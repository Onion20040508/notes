*Sources: Fowles, Introduction to Modern Optics, §6.4 · Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lectures 16–17.*

Why is the sky blue, the setting sun red, and a cloud white? The electrons that light drives in a medium radiate. Forward, in step with the incident wave, their radiation builds the index of refraction ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-7|Theorem §B13.1.7]]); sideways, from randomly placed molecules, it is scattered light. Level A noted that accelerating charges radiate ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^rem-a12-2-2|EM §A12.2, Remark: Antennas]]) and that skylight is polarized ([[§A4.5 Polarization#^rem-a4-5-6|§A4.5, Remark: Polarization by scattering]]). This section quantifies both: Larmor's formula for the radiated power, the cross-section of a bound electron with its three regimes, Rayleigh's $\lambda^{-4}$ law, the angular pattern and polarization of scattered light, and a link between scattering and the index of a gas that lets one compute how far sunlight gets through the air.

**Setup.** Classical radiation by the electrons that light drives: slowly moving charges ($v \ll c$) observed far away compared with the wavelength; scatterers small compared with the wavelength, each described by a polarizability ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^def-b13-3-1|Def. §B13.3.1]]), for a bound electron that of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^mod-b13-1-2|Model §B13.1.2]]. Fields are $\operatorname{Re}[\hat E e^{-i\omega t}]$ and intensities are averages over a period ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-2|EM Theorem §A12.3.2]]); scattered light is counted once (single scattering). Particles large compared with the wavelength are outside these results ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^rem-b13-3-3|Remark: Large particles: clouds and milk]]).

## Radiation from an accelerated charge

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.3.1: Radiation Field and Larmor's Formula
> A point charge $q$ moving slowly ($v \ll c$) with acceleration $\vec a$ produces, at a distance $R$ much larger than the wavelength of its radiation and in a direction at angle $\Theta$ to $\vec a$, a transverse **radiation field**
>
> $$
> E_{\text{rad}} = \frac{q\,a\sin\Theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2R}, \qquad B_{\text{rad}} = \frac{E_{\text{rad}}}{c},
> $$
>
> evaluated with the acceleration at the earlier time $t - R/c$, and directed along the component of $-\vec a$ perpendicular to the line of sight (for $q > 0$). It falls off as $1/R$, not $1/R^2$, is strongest perpendicular to $\vec a$ and zero along it. The total radiated power is **Larmor's formula**
>
> $$
> P = \frac{q^2a^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3} .
> $$
>
> For an oscillating dipole $p(t) = p_0\cos\omega t$ (replace $qa$ by $\ddot p$), the time-averaged power is $\bar P = \omega^4p_0^2/(12\pi\varepsilon_0c^3)$.
>
> The home of this theorem is Electromagnetism level B ([[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-2|EM Theorem §B11.4.2]], with the radiation field as the acceleration field of [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^def-b11-2-2|EM Def. §B11.2.2]]); it is stated here for the optics that needs it, with the field taken from Purcell's field-line argument (derivation below).
>
> *Model:* a point charge; *applies:* $v \ll c$, far zone ($R$ much larger than the wavelength); the general theorem: [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-2|EM Theorem §B11.4.2]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §1, eqs. (1)–(7) (his summary of Purcell and Morin, Electricity and Magnetism, Appendix H)*

^thm-b13-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The field is taken from Purcell's argument, which Schwartz summarizes: a charge that changes its velocity during a short time leaves a kink in its field lines, confined to a shell of thickness $c\times$(duration) expanding at $c$; inside and outside the shell Gauss's law fixes the radial field, and the geometry of the kink gives the transverse component $E_\Theta/E_r = aR\sin\Theta/c^2$ with $E_r = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2$. (The full derivation from Maxwell's equations: the fields of a point charge in arbitrary motion, [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^thm-b11-2-3|EM Theorem §B11.2.3]], and Larmor's formula from them, [[§B11.4★ Radiation from a Point Charge#^thm-b11-4-2|EM Theorem §B11.4.2]]; the kink picture is [[§B11.2★ The Fields of a Moving Point Charge#^rem-b11-2-4|EM Remark: The acceleration field is the kink in the field lines]].) At large $R$ the field is locally a plane wave, carrying the energy flux $S = \varepsilon_0cE_{\text{rad}}^2$ ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-2|EM Theorem §A12.3.2]]). Through a sphere of radius $R$,
>
> $$
> P = \oint \varepsilon_0cE_{\text{rad}}^2\,R^2\,d\Omega = \varepsilon_0c\Bigl(\frac{qa}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2}\Bigr)^2\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\sin^2\Theta\,\sin\Theta\,d\Theta\,d\varphi = \frac{q^2a^2}{16\pi^2\varepsilon_0c^3}\cdot\frac{8\pi}{3} = \frac{q^2a^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3},
> $$
>
> independent of $R$, as energy conservation requires. For the dipole, $\ddot p = -\omega^2p_0\cos\omega t$, and $\langle\cos^2\omega t\rangle = \tfrac12$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-6|Theorem §B1.1.6]]).

*(Not from the sources; animation added, vault only.)* The oscillating dipole $p(t) = p_0\cos\omega t$ of the last line of Theorem §B13.3.1, reused from Electromagnetism §B11.3★, where its electric field lines are computed exactly over one period in a plane through the dipole axis (vertical). This is the field of each electron driven by the incident light in Rayleigh scattering (Theorems §B13.3.2–§B13.3.4): the $\sin^2\Theta$ pattern of a driven dipole is why the scattered light is polarized at $90^\circ$ and why none is scattered along the direction of oscillation.

> [!remark] Remark: Why power cords do not broadcast
> An alternating current in a wire is a crowd of accelerating electrons, and radiates. A power cord carries equal and opposite currents in two wires a few millimetres apart; at $60\ \mathrm{Hz}$ the wavelength is about $5000\ \mathrm{km}$, so the two radiation fields cancel almost perfectly everywhere. At $1\ \mathrm{GHz}$ ($\lambda = 30\ \mathrm{cm}$) the separation is no longer negligible, the cancellation fails, and the radiated power, which for a given current grows rapidly with frequency, becomes large. A coaxial cable sends the return current through a cylinder around the inner conductor; by symmetry the outside fields cancel at any frequency, which is why high-frequency signals travel in coax. (Schwartz attributes the growth to the $\omega^4$ of [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|Theorem §B13.3.1]]; that power law holds at fixed charge displacement. At fixed current amplitude the displacement falls as $1/\omega$, and a short single wire radiates as $\omega^2$; the line, with its cancelling pair, grows faster still.)
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §3*

## Scattering by a bound electron

> [!definition] Definition §B13.3.1: Polarizability; Scattering Cross-Section
> A small scatterer (molecule, atom) in a field $\operatorname{Re}[\hat Ee^{-i\omega t}]$ acquires a dipole moment $\hat p = \alpha_{\text{pol}}(\omega)\hat E$; $\alpha_{\text{pol}}$ is its **polarizability**. For one Lorentz electron (Theorem §B13.1.3, with $\hat p = -e\hat r$),
>
> $$
> \alpha_{\text{pol}} = \frac{e^2/m}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega} .
> $$
>
> If the scatterer, in a plane wave of intensity $I$, re-radiates the time-averaged power $\bar P_s$, its **scattering cross-section** is $\sigma_s = \bar P_s/I$: the area of incident beam whose power it removes and sends elsewhere. (The symbol $\alpha_{\text{pol}}$ is not the attenuation constant $\alpha$ of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-3|Def. §B13.1.3]].) It is the frequency-dependent form of the polarizability of [[§B4.2 The Electric Dipole#^def-b4-2-1|EM Def. §B4.2.1]], in the same SI convention: $\alpha_{\text{pol}}$ has the unit $\mathrm{C\,m^2/V}$. Electromagnetism level C writes $\vec p = \alpha\varepsilon_0\vec E$, so its $\alpha = \alpha_{\text{pol}}/\varepsilon_0$ is a volume ([[§C5.4 The Microscopic Dielectric Constant#^def-c5-4-1|EM Def. §C5.4.1]]); the static polarizability of Quantum Mechanics is Gaussian, $\alpha_{\text{pol}}/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ in SI terms ([[§C9.2 Atoms in External Fields, Fine and Hyperfine Structure#^def-c9-2-1|QM Def. §C9.2.1]]).
>
> *Model:* [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^mod-b13-1-2|Model §B13.1.2]] for the Lorentz polarizability; *applies:* scatterers small compared with the wavelength, linear response.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.4, eqs. (6.15)–(6.20) (dipole of a bound electron) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §2 (power scattered per molecule); the cross-section is notation introduced here*

^def-b13-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.3.2: Light Scattered by a Small Scatterer
> A scatterer much smaller than the wavelength, with polarizability $\alpha_{\text{pol}}$, scatters
>
> $$
> \sigma_s = \frac{\omega^4|\alpha_{\text{pol}}|^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0^2c^4} .
> $$
>
> For a Lorentz electron,
>
> $$
> \sigma_s(\omega) = \sigma_{\mathrm T}\,\frac{\omega^4}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2}, \qquad \sigma_{\mathrm T} = \frac{8\pi}{3}r_e^2 = 6.65 \times 10^{-29}\ \mathrm{m^2}, \qquad r_e = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0mc^2} = 2.82 \times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm m ,
> $$
>
> with three regimes:
> 1. $\omega \ll \omega_0$: $\sigma_s \approx \sigma_{\mathrm T}(\omega/\omega_0)^4$, growing as the fourth power of the frequency (Rayleigh scattering, Theorem §B13.3.3);
> 2. $\omega \approx \omega_0$: a resonance peak of height $\sigma_{\mathrm T}(\omega_0/\gamma)^2$ and width about $\gamma$;
> 3. $\omega \gg \omega_0$: $\sigma_s \to \sigma_{\mathrm T}$, independent of frequency, the cross-section of a free electron (Thomson scattering).
>
> *Model:* a small scatterer of [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^def-b13-3-1|Def. §B13.3.1]], for the Lorentz electron [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^mod-b13-1-2|Model §B13.1.2]]; *applies:* as in the opening; size much less than the wavelength, single scattering, far zone.
>
> *Source: derived here from Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §1–2, and the oscillator of Fowles §6.4*

^thm-b13-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The dipole $\operatorname{Re}[\alpha_{\text{pol}}\hat Ee^{-i\omega t}]$ oscillates with amplitude $p_0 = |\alpha_{\text{pol}}|E_0$ and, by [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|Theorem §B13.3.1]], radiates $\bar P_s = \omega^4|\alpha_{\text{pol}}|^2E_0^2/(12\pi\varepsilon_0c^3)$. The incident intensity is $I = \tfrac12c\varepsilon_0E_0^2$ ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]]); the ratio is $\sigma_s$. The scatterer must be small so that all its charges feel the same phase of the wave. For the Lorentz electron $|\alpha_{\text{pol}}|^2 = (e^2/m)^2/[(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2]$, and
>
> $$
> \frac{e^4}{6\pi\varepsilon_0^2m^2c^4} = \frac{8\pi}{3}\Bigl(\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0mc^2}\Bigr)^2 .
> $$
>
> (1) For $\omega \ll \omega_0$ the denominator tends to $\omega_0^4$. (2) At $\omega = \omega_0$ the ratio is $\omega_0^4/\gamma^2\omega_0^2$; near it the shape is the resonance of [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-2|Theorem §B1.4.2]], since $|\alpha_{\text{pol}}|^2 \propto A^2$. (3) For $\omega \gg \omega_0, \gamma$ the denominator tends to $\omega^4$.

^der-b13-3-2

*Uses:* [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|Theorem §B13.3.1]], [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^def-b13-3-1|Def. §B13.3.1]], [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-3|Theorem §B13.1.3]], [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-2|Theorem §B1.4.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.3.3: Rayleigh's Law
> Light scattered by particles much smaller than the wavelength, whose resonances lie well above the light's frequency, has a power proportional to the fourth power of the frequency:
>
> $$
> \bar P_s \propto \omega^4 \propto \frac{1}{\lambda^4}
> $$
>
> for the same incident intensity. Across the visible, violet light at $400\ \mathrm{nm}$ is scattered $(700/400)^4 = 9.4$ times more strongly than red light at $700\ \mathrm{nm}$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §2, eqs. (8)–(11)*

^thm-b13-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Below its resonances a scatterer responds quasi-statically: its displacement, hence its dipole moment, follows the field with a frequency-independent amplitude, $\alpha_{\text{pol}} \approx \alpha_{\text{pol}}(0)$ (part 1 of [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-2|Theorem §B13.3.2]] for one electron; for several resonances each term of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-8|Theorem §B13.1.8]] behaves so). Its acceleration amplitude is then $\omega^2$ times a constant, and Larmor's power ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|Theorem §B13.3.1]]), quadratic in the acceleration, goes as $\omega^4$. With $\omega = 2\pi c/\lambda$, $\omega^4 \propto \lambda^{-4}$; and $1.75^4 = 9.38$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.3.4: Angular Pattern and Polarization of Scattered Light
> Unpolarized light travelling along $z$ is scattered by a small isotropic scatterer. At scattering angle $\psi$ (between the incident and the scattered direction), the scattered intensity at a given distance is proportional to
>
> $$
> \frac{1 + \cos^2\psi}{2} ,
> $$
>
> equal forward and backward and half as large at $90^\circ$. The scattered light is partly linearly polarized, with its field preferentially perpendicular to the **scattering plane** (the plane containing both directions), with degree of polarization
>
> $$
> \Pi = \frac{I_\perp - I_\parallel}{I_\perp + I_\parallel} = \frac{\sin^2\psi}{1 + \cos^2\psi} ,
> $$
>
> zero forward and backward and complete at $\psi = 90^\circ$. This is [[§A4.5 Polarization#^rem-a4-5-6|§A4.5, Remark: Polarization by scattering]] made quantitative. For the measured polarization of skylight see [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^law-b13-3-5|Law §B13.3.5]].
>
> *Source: derived here from Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §1 (the $\sin\Theta$ pattern, eq. (3))*

^thm-b13-3-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Unpolarized light is an equal, incoherent mixture of light polarized along $x$ and along $y$ ([[§A4.5 Polarization|§A4.5]]), so the intensities scattered from the two parts add. Let the scattered direction be $\hat n = (\sin\psi, 0, \cos\psi)$, in the $xz$ plane. The $y$-polarized part induces a dipole along $y$, at $\Theta = 90^\circ$ from $\hat n$: intensity $\propto \sin^2\Theta = 1$ ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|Theorem §B13.3.1]]), field along $y$, perpendicular to the scattering plane. The $x$-polarized part induces a dipole along $x$, with $\cos\Theta = \hat x\cdot\hat n = \sin\psi$: intensity $\propto 1 - \sin^2\psi = \cos^2\psi$, field in the scattering plane. The total is $\propto 1 + \cos^2\psi$; normalize by the two equal halves. Then $I_\perp \propto 1$, $I_\parallel \propto \cos^2\psi$, which gives $\Pi$.

^der-b13-3-4

*Uses:* [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|Theorem §B13.3.1]], [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-2|Theorem §B13.3.2]], [[§A4.5 Polarization#^rem-a4-5-6|§A4.5, Remark: Polarization by scattering]]

> [!law] Law §B13.3.5: Polarization of Skylight
> Light from the clear blue sky is partially linearly polarized, as a piece of Polaroid shows at once. The polarization is greatest in the direction $90^\circ$ from the Sun, where measurements give a degree of polarization ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^def-b8-1-3|Def. §B8.1.3]]) that can exceed $50\%$.
>
> *Valid when:* clear daytime sky, viewed about $90^\circ$ from the Sun, where the polarization is greatest. It stays below the complete polarization that [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-4|Theorem §B13.3.4]] gives at $90^\circ$ for single scattering by small isotropic scatterers; the course sources do not discuss the difference.
> *Source: Fowles §2.3 (Scattering and Polarization)*

## Scattering by a gas, and the colour of the sky

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.3.6: Scattering Cross-Section from the Index of a Gas
> A dilute gas of $N$ identical molecules per unit volume, at a frequency far below their resonances, has
>
> $$
> n - 1 = \frac{N\alpha_{\text{pol}}}{2\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \sigma_s = \frac{32\pi^3}{3}\,\frac{(n - 1)^2}{N^2\lambda^4} ,
> $$
>
> with $\lambda$ the vacuum wavelength: the index fixes the scattering. If the molecules are placed at random, independently, the scattered powers of different molecules add, and a beam crossing the gas loses intensity as
>
> $$
> I(z) = I_0\,e^{-N\sigma_sz} ,
> $$
>
> an attenuation length $1/N\sigma_s$ that grows as $\lambda^4$.
>
> *Source: derived here from Fowles §6.4 and Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §2 and §4*

^thm-b13-3-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The polarization is $\vec P = N\vec p = N\alpha_{\text{pol}}\vec E$, so $\chi_e = N\alpha_{\text{pol}}/\varepsilon_0$ ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-1|Def. §B13.1.1]]); for a dilute gas $n \approx 1 + \chi_e/2$ (part 1 of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-6|Theorem §B13.1.6]]), and far from resonance $\alpha_{\text{pol}}$ and $n$ are real. Substitute $\alpha_{\text{pol}} = 2\varepsilon_0(n - 1)/N$ and $\omega/c = 2\pi/\lambda$ into [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-2|Theorem §B13.3.2]]:
>
> $$
> \sigma_s = \Bigl(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Bigr)^4\frac{4\varepsilon_0^2(n - 1)^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0^2N^2} = \frac{32\pi^3}{3}\,\frac{(n - 1)^2}{N^2\lambda^4} .
> $$
>
> A slab of thickness $dz$ and unit area holds $N\,dz$ molecules; with random positions the phases of their sideways waves are random, so their powers add (cross terms average out), and the slab removes $N\sigma_sI\,dz$ from the beam: $dI/dz = -N\sigma_sI$, whose solution with $I(0) = I_0$ is the exponential ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]). Forward, the waves from all molecules are in phase whatever their positions; that coherent part is the index, not a loss ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-7|Theorem §B13.1.7]]).

*Uses:* [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-1|Def. §B13.1.1]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-6|Theorem §B13.1.6]], [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-2|Theorem §B13.3.2]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-7|Theorem §B13.1.7]]

> [!example] Example §B13.3.1: Blue Sky, Red Sunset
> Air at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ and $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ has $n - 1 = 2.93 \times 10^{-4}$ ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^law-a4-1-2|Law §A4.1.2]]) and, by the ideal-gas law ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|TH Law §A2.1.1]]), $N = p/k_BT = 101\,325/(1.381 \times 10^{-23} \times 273.15)\ \mathrm{m^{-3}} = 2.69 \times 10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$. By [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-6|Theorem §B13.3.6]], per molecule,
>
> $$
> \sigma_s(450\ \mathrm{nm}) = \frac{32\pi^3(2.93 \times 10^{-4})^2}{3\,(2.69 \times 10^{25})^2(450 \times 10^{-9})^4}\ \mathrm{m^2} = 9.6 \times 10^{-31}\ \mathrm{m^2}, \qquad \sigma_s(650\ \mathrm{nm}) = 2.2 \times 10^{-31}\ \mathrm{m^2},
> $$
>
> and the attenuation lengths $1/N\sigma_s$ are $39\ \mathrm{km}$ for blue and $169\ \mathrm{km}$ for red light.
>
> How much air does sunlight cross? Hydrostatic equilibrium ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^thm-a9-1-2|CM Theorem §A9.1.2]]) makes the pressure at the ground the weight of the air column above unit area, so the column is equivalent to a layer of sea-level air ($\rho = 1.29\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$) of thickness $H = p/\rho g = 101\,325/(1.29 \times 9.80)\ \mathrm m = 8.0\ \mathrm{km}$. With the Sun overhead the transmitted fractions are $e^{-8.0/39} = 0.81$ for blue and $e^{-8.0/169} = 0.95$ for red: a fifth of the blue is scattered out of the beam and fills the sky, against a twentieth of the red. With the Sun on the horizon the path through such a layer on an Earth of radius $R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6\ \mathrm m$ is about $\sqrt{2R_EH} = 320\ \mathrm{km}$, forty times longer: the transmissions become $e^{-320/39} = 3 \times 10^{-4}$ and $e^{-320/169} = 0.15$. The setting Sun has lost nearly all its blue and looks red.
>
> The estimate ignores dust and droplets (which scatter more evenly across the spectrum and make real sunsets variable), the slight variation of $n - 1$ with wavelength, and the curvature of the light path near the horizon; it shows the size of the effect.
>

^ex-b13-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Why the sky is blue and not violet
> The scattered spectrum, $\lambda^{-4}$ times the solar spectrum, still increases toward the violet (figure above), so the physics of scattering alone would predict a violet sky. Its colour is decided by how the eye weighs the spectrum: integrating the scattered spectrum against the colour-matching functions of human vision gives a pale blue ([[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^ex-b13-4-2|Example §B13.4.2]]). Schwartz's Lecture 17 states that the $\lambda^{-4}$ spectrum "pushes the peak intensity away from violet"; it does the opposite, and the explanation lies in perception.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §4*

^rem-b13-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Large particles: clouds and milk
> For scatterers much larger than the wavelength (water droplets in clouds, fat globules in milk, about $10\ \mu\mathrm m$ across, compared with molecules of a nanometre or less), light is reflected and refracted at the particles' surfaces as by small mirrors and lenses, nearly independently of wavelength, and the scattered light is white: clouds and milk are white, and full-fat milk is whiter than skim milk. Schwartz calls this large-particle limit Mie scattering; Mie's theory proper is the exact solution for a sphere of any size, which contains both the Rayleigh and the geometric limits. The intermediate range, particles comparable to the wavelength, needs that full theory and is not treated here.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §2*
