*Sources: Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lecture 17.*

Physics describes a light by its spectrum, the power at each wavelength; we see a colour. How are the two related? Level A identified colours with bands of wavelength ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^def-a4-3-2|Def. §A4.3.2]]), recalled Newton's demonstration that white light is a mixture ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^rem-a4-3-3|Remark: Where dispersion comes from]]), and treated the eye as a lens that forms an image on the retina ([[§A5.4 The Eye and the Camera#^mod-a5-4-1|Model §A5.4.1]]). This section asks what the retina and brain do with that image. The answer is linear algebra: colour perception maps the infinite-dimensional space of spectra onto three numbers. That fact, an empirical law of vision (Grassmann's), explains colour matching, the CIE chromaticity diagram, the gamut of a screen, and additive and subtractive mixing; three types of cone cells in the retina explain the number three. This is the only section of the chapter whose only typed source is Schwartz's lecture; Fowles does not treat colour.

## Colour is not wavelength

> [!remark] Remark: Three experiments
> - Newton (1704) showed with prisms that white light is a mixture of the spectral colours and that the prism only sorts them. He also arranged the colours on a **circle**, joining red to violet through purples, although the spectrum is a line from about $400$ to $750\ \mathrm{nm}$. Purple and magenta are colours that no single wavelength produces; so are brown and the pale, whitish tints.
> - Maxwell photographed a coloured object three times in black and white, through red, green and blue filters, and projected the three pictures through the same filters onto one screen. All the colours reappeared, including oranges and yellows that none of the three filters passes. Every colour photograph, film or digital, works this way.
> - A mixture of red ($650\ \mathrm{nm}$) and blue ($480\ \mathrm{nm}$) light can look exactly like violet light of $420\ \mathrm{nm}$.
>
> So a colour is not a wavelength. It is what the visual system makes of a spectrum, and many spectra make the same colour.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §1–2*

^rem-b13-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §B13.4.1: Spectrum; Metamers
> A light is described physically by its **spectral power distribution** $I(\lambda) \ge 0$, the power per unit wavelength interval. Two lights with different spectra that an observer cannot distinguish when seen side by side are **metamers** (written $I_1 \cong I_2$).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §2*

^def-b13-4-1

## Colour matching

> [!law] Law §B13.4.1: Grassmann's Laws of Colour Matching
> 1. **Linearity.** If $I_1 \cong I_2$, then $I_1 + I_3 \cong I_2 + I_3$ for every light $I_3$, and $cI_1 \cong cI_2$ for every $c > 0$.
> 2. **Trichromacy.** For suitable fixed **primaries** $P_1$, $P_2$, $P_3$ (for instance monochromatic red, green and blue), every light $I$ can be matched by a mixture of the three, $I \cong a_1P_1 + a_2P_2 + a_3P_3$, with real coefficients that are unique, provided negative coefficients are allowed. A negative $a_k$ means that $|a_k|P_k$ is added to $I$ instead of to the mixture. Two primaries never suffice for a normal observer; three always do.
>
> Colour perception is therefore a linear system with three dimensions.
>
> *Valid when:* a normal (trichromatic) observer; daylight (cone) vision; the two lights seen side by side, of the same size and in the same surroundings. It fails for colour-blind observers and most animals (two numbers suffice), in dim light where rods take over, and when context changes how a colour looks (Remark: Context).
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §1–2*

^law-b13-4-1

> [!definition] Definition §B13.4.2: Colour-Matching Functions; Tristimulus Values
> For fixed primaries, the **colour-matching functions** $\bar r(\lambda)$, $\bar g(\lambda)$, $\bar b(\lambda)$ are the coefficients $(a_1, a_2, a_3)$ of [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^law-b13-4-1|Law §B13.4.1]] that match monochromatic light of wavelength $\lambda$ and unit power. They were measured by Wright and Guild (1931) by asking observers to match each spectral colour with a mixture of three monochromatic primaries; $\bar r$ comes out negative over part of the spectrum. The **tristimulus values** of a light $I(\lambda)$ are
>
> $$
> R = \int I(\lambda)\,\bar r(\lambda)\,d\lambda, \qquad G = \int I(\lambda)\,\bar g(\lambda)\,d\lambda, \qquad B = \int I(\lambda)\,\bar b(\lambda)\,d\lambda .
> $$
>
> In Schwartz's illustration, with primaries at $650$, $530$ and $480\ \mathrm{nm}$, violet light of $420\ \mathrm{nm}$ is matched by $0.20$ parts red, $0.02$ green and $0.97$ blue.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §2 and §4, eq. (12)*

^def-b13-4-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.4.2: Matching an Arbitrary Spectrum
> Under [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^law-b13-4-1|Law §B13.4.1]], every light $I$ matches the mixture of its tristimulus values, $I \cong R\,P_1 + G\,P_2 + B\,P_3$, and two lights are metamers exactly when their tristimulus values agree. The map $I \mapsto (R, G, B)$ is linear: perception projects the infinite-dimensional space of spectra onto $\mathbb R^3$. Its null space is enormous, and any spectrum with zero tristimulus values can be added to a light (keeping it nonnegative) without visible change; that is the origin of metamerism.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §2 and §4, eq. (12)*

^thm-b13-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Divide the spectrum into narrow bands of width $\Delta\lambda$. Band $i$ is close to monochromatic light at $\lambda_i$ with power $I(\lambda_i)\Delta\lambda$, which by [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^def-b13-4-2|Def. §B13.4.2]] and the scaling part of [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^law-b13-4-1|Law §B13.4.1]] matches $I(\lambda_i)\Delta\lambda\,[\bar r(\lambda_i)P_1 + \bar g(\lambda_i)P_2 + \bar b(\lambda_i)P_3]$ (negative parts moved to the other side). Adding the bands one at a time, the additivity part of the law gives $I \cong \sum_iI(\lambda_i)\Delta\lambda\,[\cdots]$, and as $\Delta\lambda \to 0$ the sums become the integrals of $R$, $G$, $B$ (assuming that matches are stable under small changes of the spectrum). If two lights have equal tristimulus values, both match the same mixture, so they match each other. If $I_1 \cong I_2$ with different tristimulus values, the two mixtures would match each other, contradicting the uniqueness in part 2 of the law. Linearity of $I \mapsto (R, G, B)$ is linearity of the integral ([[§7 Vector Space of Linear Maps#^ladr-3-1|LADR Def. 3.1]]); a spectrum with zero image lies in the null space ([[§8 Null Spaces and Ranges#^ladr-3-11|LADR Def. 3.11]]).

> [!remark] Remark: Bookkeeping with negative amounts
> Schwartz's illustration of the algebra: suppose yellow plus violet matches $1.3$ blue $+ 0.1$ red $+ 0.3$ green, while violet alone matches $0.97$ blue $+ 0.20$ red $+ 0.02$ green. Subtracting (linearity), yellow matches $0.33$ blue $- 0.10$ red $+ 0.28$ green: to match the yellow, $0.10$ of red must be added to the yellow side. The negative value is bookkeeping for a match made with a primary on the test side, not a perceived negative intensity. (The numbers are illustrative.) Why some negative values are unavoidable with any real primaries: Caution: The second lobe and the negative lobes, below.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §2, eqs. (1)–(4)*

## The CIE system

> [!definition] Definition §B13.4.3: CIE Tristimulus Values; Chromaticity Coordinates
> The International Commission on Illumination (CIE, 1931) replaced the Wright–Guild functions by the linear combinations
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix}\bar X(\lambda)\\ \bar Y(\lambda)\\ \bar Z(\lambda)\end{pmatrix} = M\begin{pmatrix}\bar r(\lambda)\\ \bar g(\lambda)\\ \bar b(\lambda)\end{pmatrix}, \qquad M = \frac{1}{0.17}\begin{pmatrix}0.49 & 0.31 & 0.20\\ 0.17 & 0.81 & 0.01\\ 0.00 & 0.01 & 0.99\end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> chosen so that $\bar X$, $\bar Y$, $\bar Z$ are nonnegative at every wavelength. The **CIE tristimulus values** of a light are $X = \int I\bar X\,d\lambda$, and similarly $Y$, $Z$. With $S = X + Y + Z$, the **chromaticity coordinates**
>
> $$
> x = \frac XS, \qquad y = \frac YS, \qquad z = \frac ZS = 1 - x - y
> $$
>
> discard the amount of light and keep the two numbers that describe its colour; a colour is a point $(x, y)$ of the **chromaticity diagram**.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §3, eqs. (5)–(9)*

^def-b13-4-3

> [!remark] Remark: X, Y and Z are not lights
> The "primaries" $X$, $Y$, $Z$ sit at the corners $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, $(0, 0)$ of the diagram, outside the region of real colours: no light can be made of them, and $M$ is just a convenient invertible change of basis ([[§10 Invertibility and Isomorphisms#^ladr-3-80|LADR Def. 3.80]]) that makes all coefficients positive. Monochromatic lights trace a curved boundary, the **spectral locus**; the straight segment joining its red and violet ends, the **line of purples**, holds the mixtures of red and violet, which no single wavelength matches. That is why Newton's colours close into a circle. The real colours fill the region bounded by these two curves. (The colour-matching data needed to draw the locus are not tabulated in the sources, so it is not drawn here.) The sum $S$ is a normalization: equal $S$ does not mean equal brightness, since the eye is far more sensitive to yellow-green than to blue or red.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §3 and §5*

^rem-b13-4-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.4.3: Mixtures Lie on Straight Lines
> If two lights have chromaticities $\mathbf c_1 = (x_1, y_1)$ and $\mathbf c_2$ and sums $S_1$, $S_2$, their additive mixture has chromaticity
>
> $$
> \mathbf c = \alpha\,\mathbf c_1 + (1 - \alpha)\,\mathbf c_2, \qquad \alpha = \frac{S_1}{S_1 + S_2} \in [0, 1],
> $$
>
> on the segment joining them, nearer the component with the larger $S$. Consequently, the colours that three primaries can make with nonnegative amounts fill exactly the triangle with vertices at their chromaticities, the **gamut** of the primaries; and the set of all real colours is the smallest convex set containing the spectral locus.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §3, eq. (11), and Figures 6–8*

^thm-b13-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Tristimulus values are linear in the spectrum ([[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^thm-b13-4-2|Theorem §B13.4.2]]; $M$ is a fixed matrix), so the mixture has $X = X_1 + X_2$, $Y = Y_1 + Y_2$, $S = S_1 + S_2$, and
>
> $$
> x = \frac{X_1 + X_2}{S_1 + S_2} = \frac{S_1x_1 + S_2x_2}{S_1 + S_2} = \alpha x_1 + (1 - \alpha)x_2 ,
> $$
>
> likewise for $y$. For three primaries in amounts $a_k \ge 0$ the same computation gives $\mathbf c = \sum_kw_k\mathbf c_k$ with weights $w_k = a_kS_k/\sum_ja_jS_j \ge 0$ summing to $1$, a point of the triangle; conversely every such weighting is reached by choosing $a_k = w_k/S_k$. A real light is a superposition, with nonnegative weights, of monochromatic lights, so its chromaticity is a weighted average of points of the spectral locus.

> [!example] Example §B13.4.1: Primaries, White and Gamut from the CIE Matrix
> The primaries for which $M$ is written have unit tristimulus vectors $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 1, 0)$, $(0, 0, 1)$, so their $(X, Y, Z)$ are the columns of $M$:
>
> | primary | $(X, Y, Z) \times 0.17$ | $S \times 0.17$ | $(x, y)$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | R | $(0.49,\ 0.17,\ 0.00)$ | $0.66$ | $(0.742,\ 0.258)$ |
> | G | $(0.31,\ 0.81,\ 0.01)$ | $1.13$ | $(0.274,\ 0.717)$ |
> | B | $(0.20,\ 0.01,\ 0.99)$ | $1.20$ | $(0.167,\ 0.008)$ |
>
> Equal amounts of the three, $R = G = B = 1$, give the row sums: $(X, Y, Z) = (1.00,\ 0.99,\ 1.00)/0.17$, so $(x, y) = (0.334,\ 0.331)$, practically the centre $(\tfrac13, \tfrac13)$, the white point E of a flat spectrum. The matrix is normalized so that equal amounts of the primaries make white. All three primaries lie close to the edge $x + y = 1$ or to the bottom of the diagram, far from white: they are nearly spectral colours, and their triangle is the gamut of Theorem §B13.4.3. A screen whose primaries lie farther inside (cheaper light sources) has a smaller triangle, and the colours outside it, including the most saturated spectral ones, cannot be displayed. That is why no photograph of a rainbow looks like a rainbow.
>
> *Source: matrix from Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §3, eq. (7); computed here*

> [!example] Example §B13.4.2: The Colour of the Sky
> Skylight has the spectrum $I(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{-4}$ times that of sunlight ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-3|Theorem §B13.3.3]]). Schwartz integrates $\lambda^{-4}$ against the colour-matching functions ([[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^thm-b13-4-2|Theorem §B13.4.2]]) and normalizes to $r + g + b = 1$:
>
> $$
> (r, g, b) = (0.117,\ 0.295,\ 0.587) .
> $$
>
> With $M$, $(X, Y, Z) = (1.566,\ 1.557,\ 3.436)$, $S = 6.559$, so
>
> $$
> (x, y) = (0.239,\ 0.237) ,
> $$
>
> inside the gamut and displaced from white $(0.334, 0.331)$ toward the blue corner: a blue mixed with a good deal of white, the pale blue of the sky, and not the deep violet of the spectrum's short-wavelength end, although $\lambda^{-4}$ is largest there ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^rem-b13-3-2|§B13.3, Remark: Why the sky is blue and not violet]]). The colour-matching functions are small at the violet end, so the violet excess counts for little.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §4, eq. (13); chromaticity computed here*

^ex-b13-4-2

> [!definition] Definition §B13.4.4: Hue, Saturation and Value
> A colour has three coordinates of a different kind:
> - **hue**, the spectral colour (or purple) toward which it lies as seen from the white point, a direction in the chromaticity diagram;
> - **saturation**, how far it lies from white toward the boundary: zero for white or grey, maximal for spectral colours;
> - **value**, the amount of light, which the chromaticity coordinates discard.
>
> Colours that do not occur in the rainbow are those of lower saturation (spectral colours mixed with white) and the purples.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §5*

^def-b13-4-4

> [!remark] Remark: The colour of thermal light
> A blackbody at temperature $T$ has a definite spectrum ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-4|QM Theorem §A1.1.4]]), hence a definite chromaticity. As $T$ rises the point moves along a curve, the **Planckian locus**, from red through orange and yellow to white and then bluish white: red giants such as Betelgeuse are cooler than the yellowish Sun, and hot stars such as Sirius look bluish white. An incandescent bulb at about $3000\ \mathrm K$ peaks, by Wien's law ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^thm-a1-1-5|QM Theorem §A1.1.5]]), at $2.898\ \mathrm{mm\,K}/3000\ \mathrm K = 966\ \mathrm{nm}$, in the infrared: most of its power is invisible heat, and its visible light is the short-wavelength tail, which is why it tints a room yellow. The Sun, near $5700\ \mathrm K$ on Schwartz's Planckian locus, is close to white; it looks yellowish from the ground because the sky has scattered some of its blue ([[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^ex-b13-3-1|Example §B13.3.1]]). (Schwartz gives Wien's peak for $5000\ \mathrm K$ as $501\ \mathrm{nm}$; that is the value for $5780\ \mathrm K$, while $5000\ \mathrm K$ gives $580\ \mathrm{nm}$.)
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §4 and §9.2*

## Additive and subtractive mixing

> [!model] Model §B13.4.4: Three-Band Model of Colour Mixing
> Divide the visible spectrum into three bands, red, green and blue, each acting as one primary. A light is described by its power in each band, $(r, g, b)$; a filter, dye or ink by its transmittance (or reflectance) in each band, $(t_R, t_G, t_B)$ with $0 \le t \le 1$.
> - **Additive mixing**, lights superposed on a screen or in the eye: the triples **add**. Red $+$ green $+$ blue $=$ white; magenta $(1, 0, 1)$ $+$ cyan $(0, 1, 1)$ $= (1, 1, 2)$, a pale violet (lavender).
> - **Subtractive mixing**, white light passing through two filters in turn, or reflected by a mixture of pigments each of which absorbs: the transmittances **multiply** band by band. Magenta $\times$ cyan $= (1, 0, 1)\times(0, 1, 1) = (0, 0, 1)$, blue.
>
> Two colours are **complementary** if they add to white. The subtractive primaries cyan, magenta and yellow are white minus red, green and blue: cyan $= (1, 1, 1) - (1, 0, 0)$. Additive mixing makes colours whiter; subtractive mixing makes them darker, toward black.
> - *Assumes:* filters that pass or block whole bands. For real, smooth transmittances the product rule holds for spectra, $I_{\text{out}}(\lambda) = I(\lambda)\,T_1(\lambda)\,T_2(\lambda)$, not for three numbers per colour; real pigment mixtures also scatter.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §6, eqs. (16)–(19)*

^mod-b13-4-4

> [!remark] Remark: Printing
> Printers mix inks subtractively. Red, green and blue inks laid side by side would each reflect only a third of the light, a dark grey; cyan, magenta and yellow inks, the complements, are used instead, and each removes one band. A fourth ink, black (the K of CMYK, "key"), gives a deeper black than the three together and is cheaper. Lighter shades are made by halftoning: dots of ink of varying size, with white paper between them, which the eye averages additively at a distance.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §6.1*

## The eye

> [!remark] Remark: Rods and cones
> The image formed by the eye's optics ([[§A5.4 The Eye and the Camera#^mod-a5-4-1|Model §A5.4.1]]) falls on the retina, a mosaic of about $120$ million **rods** and $6$ million **cones**. The **fovea**, at the centre of the macula, contains only cones and gives the sharpest, coloured, central vision; the rods are spread over the rest of the retina, with none in the fovea and none where the optic nerve leaves (the blind spot). There are three kinds of cones, S (short), M (medium) and L (long), with sensitivity curves peaking in the blue, the green, and the yellow-green (the "red" cone peaks closer to yellow than to red); their curves overlap broadly. Rods are far more sensitive, dominate night (scotopic) vision and peripheral motion detection, and do not respond to red light. Overall the eye works over about $12$ orders of magnitude of intensity; adapting to the dark takes the rods $7$–$10$ minutes, adapting to bright light about $30\ \mathrm s$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §7*

^rem-b13-4-6

> [!model] Model §B13.4.5: Three-Receptor Model of Colour Matching
> Each cone type $j \in \{S, M, L\}$ responds to a light $I$ with
>
> $$
> c_j[I] = \int I(\lambda)\,s_j(\lambda)\,d\lambda ,
> $$
>
> where $s_j(\lambda) \ge 0$ is its sensitivity, and two lights look identical exactly when they produce the same three responses $\mathbf c = (c_S, c_M, c_L)$.
> - *Assumes:* daylight vision with the rods silent; responses linear in the light power at a fixed state of adaptation (the roughly logarithmic perception of brightness comes later in the visual pathway and is the same for two lights compared side by side); no effects of context.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §7.1 (trichromacy because there are three kinds of cones); formalized here*

^mod-b13-4-5

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.4.6: Grassmann's Laws from Three Receptors
> In [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^mod-b13-4-5|Model §B13.4.5]]:
> 1. both parts of [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^law-b13-4-1|Law §B13.4.1]] hold, for any three primaries whose response matrix $A_{jk} = c_j[P_k]$ is invertible;
> 2. the colour-matching functions are fixed linear combinations of the cone sensitivities,
>
> $$
> \begin{pmatrix}\bar r(\lambda)\\ \bar g(\lambda)\\ \bar b(\lambda)\end{pmatrix} = A^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}s_S(\lambda)\\ s_M(\lambda)\\ s_L(\lambda)\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix}\bar X\\ \bar Y\\ \bar Z\end{pmatrix} = M A^{-1}\,\mathbf s(\lambda) ;
> $$
>
> 3. an observer with only two kinds of cones (a dichromat) needs only two primaries, and lights that differ only in the missing response look the same to him.
>
> The layer changes: Grassmann's empirical law becomes a consequence of the number of receptor types.
>
> *Source: derived here from Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §2 and §7.1*

^thm-b13-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $\mathbf c[I]$ is linear in $I$, so equal responses stay equal when the same light is added to both or both are scaled by $c > 0$. A mixture $\sum_ka_kP_k$ has responses $A\mathbf a$; it matches $I$ exactly when $A\mathbf a = \mathbf c[I]$, which has the unique solution $\mathbf a = A^{-1}\mathbf c[I]$ ([[§10 Invertibility and Isomorphisms#^ladr-3-80|LADR Def. 3.80]]). Negative components are realized by moving those primaries to the side of $I$, by linearity. (2) For unit monochromatic light at $\lambda$, $\mathbf c = \mathbf s(\lambda)$, so $\mathbf a(\lambda) = A^{-1}\mathbf s(\lambda)$; the CIE functions are $M$ times these ([[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^def-b13-4-3|Def. §B13.4.3]]). (3) The same argument with a $2\times2$ matrix.

^der-b13-4-6

*Uses:* [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^mod-b13-4-5|Model §B13.4.5]], [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^law-b13-4-1|Law §B13.4.1]], [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^def-b13-4-2|Def. §B13.4.2]], [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^def-b13-4-3|Def. §B13.4.3]], [[§10 Invertibility and Isomorphisms#^ladr-3-80|LADR Def. 3.80]]

> [!caution] Caution: The second lobe and the negative lobes
> The CIE function $\bar X$ has a small second peak in the violet besides its main one in the red, and the Wright–Guild $\bar r$ is negative over part of the spectrum. Schwartz attributes the first to higher-level cognitive processing and suggests that we somehow perceive negative intensities. In the three-receptor model neither needs the brain: by [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^thm-b13-4-6|Theorem §B13.4.6]] every colour-matching function is a fixed combination of the three cone curves, and a combination that contains some of the S-cone curve has a lobe where that curve peaks. Negative coefficients are unavoidable because the cone curves overlap: light that excites the M cones also excites the L cones, so no real primary stimulates one cone type alone, and some spectral colours lie outside the triangle of any three real primaries. The brain does shape colour appearance (next remark), but not the matching functions.

^cau-b13-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Two cones, and no cones
> Most mammals, dogs among them, have two kinds of cones. They distinguish hues across the spectrum, but their colour space has one dimension fewer, so many colours that we tell apart look identical to them. In dim light our cones fall silent and the rods, which do not see red, take over: a red flower that glows by day looks nearly black at dusk while its leaves still show (the Purkinje effect). Darkrooms and the instrument lights of ships at night are red so that the rods, already adapted to the dark, are not disturbed.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §7.1*

^rem-b13-4-7

> [!remark] Remark: Context
> Colour appearance depends on the surroundings: the same ring looks different on different backgrounds, two squares of identical grey look different in a shaded checkerboard, and the brain fills the blind spot with whatever pattern surrounds it. Such effects are why [[§B13.4 Colour and Vision#^law-b13-4-1|Law §B13.4.1]] requires identical viewing conditions; colorimetry describes matches, not appearance.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §8*

^rem-b13-4-8

> [!remark] Remark: Cameras
> A digital camera ([[§A5.4 The Eye and the Camera#^rem-a5-4-4|§A5.4, Remark: A camera is a single lens]]) covers its pixels with red, green and blue filters in a Bayer array, with twice as many green pixels as red or blue because the eye is most sensitive to green; neighbouring pixels are combined into an RGB value. The recorded intensities are compressed as $I \to I^\gamma$ (gamma encoding) to fit the eye's huge range into $8$ bits per channel, and **white balance** rescales the channels so that a scene lit by a yellowish bulb looks as if lit by daylight, as the brain does unasked.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 17 §9*
