*Sources: Fowles, Introduction to Modern Optics, §6.1–6.4 · Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lecture 16.*

Why is light slower in glass than in vacuum, and why does the slowing depend on the colour? Level A took the index of refraction as a number per material ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]), recorded its rise toward the violet as an empirical law ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]]), and described in words how driven, re-radiating electrons slow the wave ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^rem-a4-1-2|Remark: Why light is slower in matter]], [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^rem-a4-3-3|Remark: Where dispersion comes from]]). This section turns that picture into a theory. Maxwell's equations with the polarization of the medium as a source give a wave equation in matter; modelling each bound electron as the driven damped oscillator of [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator|§B1.3]] gives a complex index $\tilde n(\omega) = n + i\kappa$ whose real part is the index and whose imaginary part is absorption. The layer changes: the shape of $n(\lambda)$, and Cauchy's formula, become consequences of the model, and $n \ge 1$ turns out to be a property of the visible range, not of matter.

## Fields and waves in matter

> [!definition] Definition §B13.1.1: Polarization; Complex Susceptibility
> The **polarization** $\vec P$ of a medium is its electric dipole moment per unit volume, averaged over a region large compared with the atoms and small compared with the wavelength. A medium is **linear and isotropic** if the polarization is proportional and parallel to the field,
>
> $$
> \vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E ,
> $$
>
> with the **electric susceptibility** $\chi_e$ (the $\chi$ of [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|Model §B8.1.1]], which takes it real); then the **electric displacement** is $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P = \varepsilon\vec E$ with **permittivity** $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(1 + \chi_e)$. For a field oscillating at angular frequency $\omega$ the relation holds between complex amplitudes, $\hat P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e(\omega)\hat E$, and the **complex susceptibility** $\chi_e(\omega)$ may have an imaginary part: the polarization need not be in phase with the field, and then the medium absorbs (Theorem §B13.1.4). In a crystal $\chi_e$ is a tensor ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity|§B8.3]]). At $\omega = 0$, $1 + \chi_e$ is the dielectric constant of [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^def-a4-4-1|EM Def. §A4.4.1]] and $\varepsilon$ the permittivity of [[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^rem-a4-4-2|EM §A4.4, Remark: Permittivity]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.2, eqs. (6.5)–(6.9)*

^def-b13-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Notation in this chapter
> - $\kappa$ is, from here on, the **extinction index** (Def. §B13.1.3), as in Fowles. Electromagnetism level A uses $\kappa$ for the static dielectric constant, which is $1 + \chi_e(0)$ here.
> - $\chi_e$ is the susceptibility, written $\chi$ in Chapter B8. The $\chi(\omega)$ of [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^def-b1-3-1|Def. §B1.3.1]] is the response function of one oscillator; Theorem §B13.1.3 relates the two.
> - $e > 0$ is the elementary charge, so an electron carries $-e$; $N$ is a number of electrons per unit volume, not a number of moles.

^cau-b13-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.1.1: The General Wave Equation in a Medium
> In a nonmagnetic, electrically neutral medium with polarization $\vec P$ and conduction current density $\vec J$, the electric field obeys
>
> $$
> \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec E) + \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2} = -\mu_0\frac{\partial^2\vec P}{\partial t^2} - \mu_0\frac{\partial\vec J}{\partial t} .
> $$
>
> The right side holds the **source terms**: the oscillating bound charges of the polarization, and the conduction electrons. If the medium is homogeneous and $\vec P$, $\vec J$ are linear isotropic responses to $\vec E$, then for a wave of frequency $\omega$ with $\varepsilon(\omega) \neq 0$, $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ and $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec E) = -\nabla^2\vec E$. In vacuum both source terms vanish and the equation becomes the wave equation of [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|EM Theorem §A12.2.4]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.3, eqs. (6.10)–(6.14), and §6.4, eq. (6.24)*

^thm-b13-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The macroscopic Maxwell equations of a medium with $\vec M = \vec 0$ and no free charge are
>
> $$
> \nabla\times\vec E = -\mu_0\frac{\partial\vec H}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla\times\vec H = \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial\vec P}{\partial t} + \vec J, \qquad \nabla\cdot(\varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P) = 0, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec H = 0 .
> $$
>
> They are the differential form of [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|EM Principle §A12.1.3]] with the charges of the medium entered as sources (for a lossless dielectric they reduce to the equations of [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|Model §B8.1.1]]): the bound charges as the polarization current $\partial\vec P/\partial t$ (and density $-\nabla\cdot\vec P$), the free electrons as $\vec J$. (How the averaging is done: in statics, the bound charges of [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^def-b5-1-2|EM Def. §B5.1.2]] and the macroscopic field of [[§B5.1 Polarization and Bound Charge#^thm-b5-1-4|EM Theorem §B5.1.4]]; the polarization current and the macroscopic equations are [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-4|EM Theorem §B8.4.4]] and [[§B8.4 Maxwell's Equations#^thm-b8-4-5|EM Theorem §B8.4.5]]; time-dependent averaging itself is Electromagnetism level C, planned.) Take the curl of the first equation and insert the second:
>
> $$
> \nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec E) = -\mu_0\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\vec H) = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2} - \mu_0\frac{\partial^2\vec P}{\partial t^2} - \mu_0\frac{\partial\vec J}{\partial t},
> $$
>
> and $\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2$. For a linear isotropic response at frequency $\omega$, $\varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P = \varepsilon(\omega)\vec E$, so the third equation gives $\varepsilon(\omega)\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$, hence $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ when $\varepsilon \neq 0$; then the identity $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec E) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec E) - \nabla^2\vec E$ leaves $-\nabla^2\vec E$.

*Uses:* [[§A12.1 Maxwell's Equations and EM Waves#^pr-a12-1-3|EM Principle §A12.1.3]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-1|Def. §B13.1.1]], [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^mod-b8-1-1|Model §B8.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Which source term matters where
> In a dielectric such as glass the electrons stay bound, $\vec J = \vec 0$, and the polarization term produces dispersion, absorption, double refraction ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity|§B8.3]]) and optical activity. In a metal the conduction term dominates and explains why metals are opaque and shiny ([[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media|§B13.2]]). Semiconductors need both terms; a full account of them needs quantum theory.

## The Lorentz model

> [!model] Model §B13.1.2: Lorentz (Bound-Electron) Model of a Dielectric
> A nonconducting isotropic medium contains $N$ electrons per unit volume, of charge $-e$ and mass $m$. Each is bound to its equilibrium position by a linear restoring force $-K\vec r = -m\omega_0^2\vec r$, damped by a force $-m\gamma\,\dot{\vec r}$, and driven by the electric field of the light:
>
> $$
> m\ddot{\vec r} + m\gamma\dot{\vec r} + m\omega_0^2\vec r = -e\vec E, \qquad \vec P = -Ne\vec r .
> $$
>
> Each electron is the driven damped oscillator of [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], with natural frequency $\omega_0$, damping $\gamma = b/m$ as in Chapter B1, and drive $-e\vec E/m$ per unit mass.
> - *Assumes:* the nuclei stay put (far heavier than electrons); the magnetic force $-e\vec v\times\vec B$ is neglected (it is of order $v/c$ of the electric force); the field acting on each electron is the macroscopic $\vec E$ at its position (no correction for the field of neighbouring dipoles, so best for dilute media); the displacement is tiny compared with the wavelength; the restoring force is linear (fields far below atomic fields).
> - *One resonance:* all electrons are bound alike; several kinds of electrons: Theorem §B13.1.7.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.4, eqs. (6.15)–(6.18) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §4 (undamped)*

^mod-b13-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: Importing the oscillator into the optics convention
> Chapters B1–B7 write $x = \operatorname{Re}[\hat x\,e^{+i\omega t}]$; optics, from Chapter B8 on, writes $e^{i(kz - \omega t)}$ ([[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^cau-b8-1-1|Caution: The optics sign convention]]). Since $\operatorname{Re}z = \operatorname{Re}z^{\ast}$, a drive $\operatorname{Re}[\hat f e^{-i\omega t}] = \operatorname{Re}[\hat f^{\ast} e^{+i\omega t}]$ has, by [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], the steady state $\operatorname{Re}[\hat f^{\ast}\chi(\omega)e^{+i\omega t}] = \operatorname{Re}[\hat f\chi(\omega)^{\ast}e^{-i\omega t}]$. So the response function to use here is the **complex conjugate**
>
> $$
> \chi(\omega)^* = \frac{1}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega} = \chi'(\omega) + i\chi''(\omega),
> $$
>
> with the elastic and absorptive parts $\chi'$, $\chi'' \ge 0$ of [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^def-b1-4-2|Def. §B1.4.2]] unchanged: absorption now shows up as a **positive** imaginary part. Amplitudes, the phase lag $\delta \in [0, \pi)$ and powers are the same in both conventions. Schwartz's Lecture 16 still uses $e^{+i\omega t}$; his formulas are translated below.

^cau-b13-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.1.3: Polarization of a Lorentz Medium
> For a field $\operatorname{Re}[\hat E e^{-i\omega t}]$ the steady-state polarization is $\hat P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e(\omega)\hat E$ with
>
> $$
> \chi_e(\omega) = \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega} = \omega_p^2\bigl[\chi'(\omega) + i\chi''(\omega)\bigr], \qquad \omega_p^2 = \frac{Ne^2}{m\varepsilon_0} .
> $$
>
> At $\omega = 0$ it reduces to the static polarization $\vec P = (Ne^2/K)\vec E$, where the spring force balances the field. Both the size and the phase of $\vec P$ relative to $\vec E$ depend on frequency, and near $\omega_0$ the response is resonant.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.4, eqs. (6.19)–(6.22)*

^thm-b13-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The vector equation of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^mod-b13-1-2|Model §B13.1.2]] is three uncoupled scalar driven oscillators, one per Cartesian component, with drive $-(e/m)E_j$. By [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^cau-b13-1-2|Caution: Importing the oscillator]] (for $\gamma > 0$ every $\omega$ is allowed), $\hat r = -(e/m)\,\chi(\omega)^*\hat E$, so
>
> $$
> \hat P = -Ne\hat r = \frac{Ne^2}{m}\,\frac{\hat E}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega} = \varepsilon_0\,\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega}\,\hat E .
> $$
>
> At $\omega = 0$, $\varepsilon_0\omega_p^2/\omega_0^2 = Ne^2/(m\omega_0^2) = Ne^2/K$.

*Uses:* [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^mod-b13-1-2|Model §B13.1.2]], [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]], [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^def-b1-4-2|Def. §B1.4.2]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-1|Def. §B13.1.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §B13.1.2: Plasma Frequency
> For $N$ electrons per unit volume the **plasma frequency** is
>
> $$
> \omega_p = \sqrt{\frac{Ne^2}{m\varepsilon_0}} = 56.4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\times\sqrt{N\ [\mathrm{m^{-3}}]} .
> $$
>
> It measures how strongly the electrons as a whole respond to a field. For a free-electron gas it is the frequency below which light is reflected ([[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-4|Theorem §B13.2.4]]); the dispersion relation $\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2$ of light in a plasma ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]]) contains the same $\omega_p$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.5, eq. (6.54)*

## The complex index of refraction

> [!definition] Definition §B13.1.3: Complex Wave Number; Complex Index; Extinction Index; Absorption Coefficient
> A homogeneous plane wave $\vec E = \vec E_0\,e^{i(\tilde kz - \omega t)}$ in a medium may have a complex **wave number** $\tilde k = k + i\alpha$; one writes
>
> $$
> \tilde k = \frac{\omega}{c}\,\tilde n, \qquad \tilde n = n + i\kappa, \qquad \vec E = \vec E_0\,e^{-\alpha z}\,e^{i(kz - \omega t)} .
> $$
>
> $\tilde n$ is the **complex index of refraction**, its real part $n$ the **index of refraction** and its imaginary part $\kappa$ the **extinction index**. The crests move at the phase velocity $\omega/k = c/n$; the amplitude decays as $e^{-\alpha z}$ with $\alpha = \kappa\omega/c$, and the intensity as $e^{-2\alpha z}$, so
>
> $$
> 2\alpha = \frac{2\kappa\omega}{c} = \frac{4\pi\kappa}{\lambda_0}
> $$
>
> is the **absorption coefficient** ($\lambda_0$ the vacuum wavelength). For a transparent medium, $\kappa = 0$ and $n = c/v$ is the index of [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]], now defined through the phase velocity.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.4, eqs. (6.25)–(6.32)*

^def-b13-1-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.1.4: Dispersion Relation of the Lorentz Medium
> In the Lorentz medium of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^mod-b13-1-2|Model §B13.1.2]], homogeneous plane waves $\vec E_0\,e^{i(\tilde kz - \omega t)}$ exist at every real $\omega$; they are transverse ($\vec E_0 \perp \hat z$), and
>
> $$
> \tilde n^2 = \Bigl(\frac{c\tilde k}{\omega}\Bigr)^2 = 1 + \chi_e(\omega) = 1 + \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega} .
> $$
>
> In real and imaginary parts,
>
> $$
> n^2 - \kappa^2 = 1 + \frac{\omega_p^2(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2} = 1 + \omega_p^2\chi', \qquad 2n\kappa = \frac{\omega_p^2\,\gamma\omega}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2} = \omega_p^2\chi'' ,
> $$
>
> and with $A = n^2 - \kappa^2$, $B = 2n\kappa \ge 0$, the root with $n, \kappa \ge 0$ (a wave travelling and decaying toward $+z$) is
>
> $$
> n = \sqrt{\tfrac12\bigl(\sqrt{A^2 + B^2} + A\bigr)}, \qquad \kappa = \sqrt{\tfrac12\bigl(\sqrt{A^2 + B^2} - A\bigr)} .
> $$
>
> The absorption $\kappa$ peaks near $\omega_0$. Below the resonance $n > 1$ and rises with $\omega$, from $n(0) = \sqrt{1 + \omega_p^2/\omega_0^2}$; across the absorption line it falls; above it $n < 1$ and climbs back toward $1$ from below.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.4, eqs. (6.23)–(6.35) and Figure 6.1*

^thm-b13-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\vec J = \vec 0$ and $\vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E$ ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-3|Theorem §B13.1.3]]), $\partial_t^2\vec P = -\omega^2\varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E$, and [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-1|Theorem §B13.1.1]] becomes $\nabla^2\vec E + (\omega^2/c^2)(1 + \chi_e)\vec E = \vec 0$ for a wave with $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$. For $\vec E_0\,e^{i(\tilde kz - \omega t)}$, $\nabla\cdot\vec E = i\tilde k\,E_{0z}\,e^{i(\tilde kz - \omega t)}$ vanishes exactly when $E_{0z} = 0$, and $\nabla^2 \to -\tilde k^2$, so
>
> $$
> -\tilde k^2 + \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\bigl(1 + \chi_e\bigr) = 0, \qquad \tilde n^2 = 1 + \chi_e .
> $$
>
> Split $\chi_e = \omega_p^2(\chi' + i\chi'')$ and $\tilde n^2 = n^2 - \kappa^2 + 2in\kappa$ into real and imaginary parts. To solve, note $n^2 + \kappa^2 = |\tilde n^2| = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}$; adding and subtracting $n^2 - \kappa^2 = A$ gives $n^2$ and $\kappa^2$. Since $B = \omega_p^2\chi'' \ge 0$, $n$ and $\kappa$ have the same sign; the other root, $-\tilde n$, is the same kind of wave travelling toward $-z$. At $\omega = 0$, $\tilde n^2 = 1 + \omega_p^2/\omega_0^2$.

> [!definition] Definition §B13.1.4: Normal and Anomalous Dispersion
> Dispersion is **normal** at frequencies where $n$ increases with frequency, $dn/d\omega > 0$ (equivalently $dn/d\lambda_0 < 0$), and **anomalous** where $n$ decreases, $dn/d\omega < 0$. The rise of $n$ toward the violet in [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]] is normal dispersion: transparent materials have their main resonances in the ultraviolet, above the visible. Anomalous dispersion occurs inside absorption bands. Some dyes absorb within the visible, and a prism made of such a dye refracts the longer wavelengths more near its band, a reversed spectrum.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.4*

^def-b13-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.1.5: Weak Response and the Shape of an Absorption Line
> 1. If $|\chi_e| \ll 1$ at all frequencies of interest (a dilute medium: $\omega_p^2 \ll \gamma\omega_0$ suffices), then to first order in $\chi_e$
>
> $$
> n - 1 \approx \frac{\omega_p^2}{2}\,\frac{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2}, \qquad \kappa \approx \frac{\omega_p^2}{2}\,\frac{\gamma\omega}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2} .
> $$
>
> 2. If moreover the line is narrow, $\gamma \ll \omega_0$, then near it ($|\omega - \omega_0| \ll \omega_0$)
>
> $$
> \kappa \approx \kappa_{\max}\,\frac{(\gamma/2)^2}{(\omega - \omega_0)^2 + (\gamma/2)^2}, \qquad n - 1 \approx \kappa_{\max}\,\frac{(\gamma/2)(\omega_0 - \omega)}{(\omega - \omega_0)^2 + (\gamma/2)^2}, \qquad \kappa_{\max} = \frac{\omega_p^2}{2\gamma\omega_0} .
> $$
>
>    The absorption line is a Lorentzian of full width $\gamma$ ([[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^def-b1-4-1|Def. §B1.4.1]]). The index has its maximum $1 + \kappa_{\max}/2$ at $\omega_0 - \gamma/2$ and its minimum $1 - \kappa_{\max}/2$ at $\omega_0 + \gamma/2$, exactly where $\kappa$ is half its peak value: dispersion is anomalous across the full width at half maximum of the line and normal outside it.
> 3. Far from the line, $\gamma\omega \ll |\omega_0^2 - \omega^2|$: $\kappa \approx 0$ and
>
> $$
> n \approx 1 + \frac{\omega_p^2}{2(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)} .
> $$
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.4 and Problems 6.1–6.2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §5, eq. (25)*

^thm-b13-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1) $|\chi_e|$ is largest near $\omega_0$, where it is about $\omega_p^2/(\gamma\omega_0)$. For $|\chi_e| \ll 1$, $\tilde n = \sqrt{1 + \chi_e} = 1 + \tfrac12\chi_e + O(|\chi_e|^2)$ (Taylor, [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]); take real and imaginary parts of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-3|Theorem §B13.1.3]]. (2) As in the derivation of [[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power#^thm-b1-4-2|Theorem §B1.4.2]], near $\omega_0$ replace $\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 = (\omega_0 - \omega)(\omega_0 + \omega)$ by $2\omega_0(\omega_0 - \omega)$ and $\gamma\omega$ by $\gamma\omega_0$; the common denominator becomes $4\omega_0^2[(\omega - \omega_0)^2 + (\gamma/2)^2]$, which gives both formulas. With $u = \omega_0 - \omega$ and $a = \gamma/2$, the function $u/(u^2 + a^2)$ has derivative $(a^2 - u^2)/(u^2 + a^2)^2$, so its extremes are at $u = \pm a$, with values $\pm 1/(2a)$; multiplying by $\kappa_{\max}a$ gives $\pm\kappa_{\max}/2$. At $u = \pm a$ the Lorentzian equals $\tfrac12$. (3) Drop $\gamma^2\omega^2$ against $(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2$ in (1).

> [!caution] Caution: The approximations need the right conditions
> Fowles's Problem 6.1 states the formulas of part 1 under the condition $\kappa \ll n$ and with $\gamma^2\omega^2$ dropped from the denominators. What is needed is $|\tilde n^2 - 1| \ll 1$: a dense transparent solid has $\kappa \ll n$ but $n - 1 = 0.5$, and there the full Theorem §B13.1.4 must be used. And near the line the damping term must be kept: without it $n$ diverges at $\omega_0$ and has no maximum and minimum, so Problem 6.2 (the extremes of $n$ at the half-maximum points of $\kappa$) needs part 2, not the formulas as printed.

^cau-b13-1-3

> [!remark] Remark: An index below one
> [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]] states $n \ge 1$. That holds for transparent materials in the visible, which lies below their resonances, but it is not a law of nature. Above a resonance $n < 1$ (Theorem §B13.1.4), and at very high frequencies, above all resonances, $\tilde n^2 \to 1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2$ (Theorem §B13.1.7): every material has an index slightly below $1$ for X-rays, as measured for quartz (Fowles, Figure 6.3). The phase velocity $c/n$ then exceeds $c$, but no signal travels at it ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^rem-b6-2-2|§B6.2, Remark: Faster than light?]]). With $n \approx 1 - \omega_p^2/2\omega^2$ the group index is $n + \omega\,dn/d\omega \approx 1 + \omega_p^2/2\omega^2 > 1$, so pulses travel slower than $c$. Inside an absorption band the picture of a packet moving at $v_g$ ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^mod-b6-2-2|Model §B6.2.2]], which excludes absorption) does not apply.

^rem-b13-1-2

## The microscopic picture

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.1.6: The Index as Interference with Forward-Scattered Light
> A plane wave $E_0\,e^{i(kz - \omega t)}$, $k = \omega/c$, crosses a thin sheet, of thickness $dz$, of a dilute medium containing $N$ undamped oscillating charges $q$ (mass $m$, natural frequency $\omega_0$) per unit volume. Downstream, the field radiated by the driven charges adds to the incident wave
>
> $$
> E_{\text{sheet}} = i\,\frac{\omega}{c}\,\delta\,dz\;E_0\,e^{i(kz - \omega t)}, \qquad \delta = \frac{Nq^2}{2\varepsilon_0m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)} ,
> $$
>
> a quarter period behind it ($\delta$ is Schwartz's symbol here, not the phase lag of Chapter B1). Sheet after sheet, the small lagging additions build up the wave $E_0\,e^{i(n\omega z/c - \omega t)}$ with
>
> $$
> n = 1 + \delta ,
> $$
>
> part 3 of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-5|Theorem §B13.1.5]] for $q = -e$. Between the molecules every piece of field moves at $c$; the slower phase velocity is the cumulative interference of the incident wave with the light scattered forward by the medium.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §4, eqs. (13)–(24)*

^thm-b13-1-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take as given the field of a uniform plane sheet of charges, surface density $\sigma$, all moving together along the sheet with velocity $v(t)$: at distance $z$ downstream it is
>
> $$
> E_{\text{sheet}}(z, t) = -\frac{\sigma q}{2\varepsilon_0c}\,v\Bigl(t - \frac zc\Bigr),
> $$
>
> along the motion (Schwartz's eq. (17), which he quotes from the Feynman Lectures; it follows from the radiation field of [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|Theorem §B13.3.1]] summed over the sheet; the field of a current sheet with arbitrary time dependence, from the retarded potentials, is [[§B11.1★ Potentials, Gauges and Retarded Potentials#^ex-b11-1-3|EM Example §B11.1.3]]). The charges in the sheet at $z'$ obey [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-3|Theorem §B13.1.3]] with $\gamma = 0$ and charge $q$: $\hat x = (q/m)\hat E/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)$, so $\hat v = -i\omega\hat x$ (optics convention). With $\sigma = N\,dz$ and $\hat E = E_0e^{ikz'}$ at the sheet,
>
> $$
> E_{\text{sheet}}(z, t) = -\frac{N\,dz\,q}{2\varepsilon_0c}\,(-i\omega)\,\frac{qE_0}{m(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)}\,e^{i(kz' - \omega(t - (z - z')/c))} = i\,\frac{\omega}{c}\,\delta\,dz\;E_0\,e^{i(kz - \omega t)} .
> $$
>
> The total downstream field is $E_0\,e^{i(kz - \omega t)}(1 + i\tfrac{\omega}{c}\delta\,dz) = E_0\,e^{i(kz - \omega t) + i\frac{\omega}{c}\delta\,dz}$ to first order in $dz$: each sheet adds the phase $(\omega/c)\delta\,dz$. The factor $i = e^{i\pi/2}$ in $\operatorname{Re}[\,\cdot\,e^{-i\omega t}]$ delays the peaks by a quarter period, so the added phase is a delay. A thickness $z$ adds $(\omega/c)\delta z$, so the wave is $E_0\,e^{i((1 + \delta)\omega z/c - \omega t)}$, and by [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-3|Def. §B13.1.3]] $n = 1 + \delta$. With $q = -e$, $Nq^2/(m\varepsilon_0) = \omega_p^2$ and $\delta = \omega_p^2/2(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)$. In Schwartz's $e^{+i\omega t}$ convention the same step reads $E_0\,e^{i\omega(t - z/c)}(1 - i\tfrac{\omega}{c}\delta\,dz)$, his eq. (20).

^der-b13-1-6

*Uses:* [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-3|Theorem §B13.1.3]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-3|Def. §B13.1.3]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^cau-b13-1-2|Caution: Importing the oscillator]], [[§B13.3 Scattering of Light#^thm-b13-3-1|Theorem §B13.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Two routes to the same index
> [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^rem-a4-1-2|Remark: Why light is slower in matter]] described this mechanism in words; Theorem §B13.1.6 is its quantitative form. Fowles's route (Theorem §B13.1.4) averages the charges into a polarization and solves Maxwell's equations; Schwartz's route follows the wave sheet by sheet. Where both apply, a dilute medium with negligible damping, they agree. The macroscopic route also covers dense media and absorption. Below resonance the electrons move in phase with the field, their forward-radiated field lags by a quarter period, and the wave is delayed: $n > 1$. Above resonance the electrons move in antiphase, the forward field leads, and $n < 1$.

## Many resonances: Sellmeier and Cauchy

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.1.7: Several Resonances
> If a fraction $f_j$ of the $N$ electrons per unit volume has natural frequency $\omega_j$ and damping $\gamma_j$ ($\sum_j f_j = 1$), then
>
> $$
> \tilde n^2 = 1 + \omega_p^2\sum_j\frac{f_j}{\omega_j^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma_j\omega} .
> $$
>
> The $f_j$ are the **oscillator strengths**. Each band adds an absorption peak and, below it, a step to $n^2$, so $n$ falls in steps from ultraviolet bands through the visible to infrared bands (Fowles, Figure 6.2). At $\omega = 0$, $\tilde n^2 = 1 + \omega_p^2\sum_jf_j/\omega_j^2$, the contribution of these oscillators to the static dielectric constant. Above all resonances ($\omega \gg \omega_j, \gamma_j$), $\tilde n^2 \approx 1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2$: all electrons respond as if free, and $n$ approaches $1$ from below.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.4, eq. (6.36) and Figures 6.2–6.3*

^thm-b13-1-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The equation of motion is linear, so the polarizations of the groups add ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]). Group $j$ has $Nf_j$ electrons per unit volume and by [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-3|Theorem §B13.1.3]] contributes $\varepsilon_0 f_j\omega_p^2/(\omega_j^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma_j\omega)$ to $\hat P/\hat E$. Then $\tilde n^2 = 1 + \chi_e$ as in the derivation of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-4|Theorem §B13.1.4]]. For $\omega \gg \omega_j, \gamma_j$ each term tends to $-f_j\omega_p^2/\omega^2$, and the $f_j$ add up to $1$.

^der-b13-1-7

*Uses:* [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-3|Theorem §B13.1.3]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-4|Theorem §B13.1.4]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-1|Theorem §B1.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Water, with dielectric constant 80 and index 1.33
> The static dielectric constant of water is $80$ ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^rem-a4-4-1|EM §A4.4, Remark: Representative values]]), yet $n^2 = 1.333^2 = 1.78$ in the visible ([[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]]). Most of the static polarization of water comes from the alignment of its permanent molecular dipoles ([[§A4.4 Dielectrics#^mod-a4-4-3|EM Model §A4.4.3]]), which is not an oscillator of the Lorentz type and cannot follow a field that reverses $10^{15}$ times a second; at optical frequencies only the electrons respond. The zero-frequency limit of Theorem §B13.1.7 gives the static dielectric constant only when all the polarization comes from bound oscillating charges.

^rem-b13-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.1.8: Sellmeier's Formula
> Away from all absorption bands ($\gamma_j\omega \ll |\omega_j^2 - \omega^2|$ for every $j$), $\tilde n = n$ is real, and in terms of the vacuum wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi c/\omega$
>
> $$
> n^2 = 1 + \sum_j\frac{A_j\lambda^2}{\lambda^2 - \lambda_j^2}, \qquad \lambda_j = \frac{2\pi c}{\omega_j}, \qquad A_j = \frac{f_j\omega_p^2}{\omega_j^2} .
> $$
>
> $A_j$ is the contribution of band $j$ to the static $n^2$. If all bands lie at wavelengths far below $\lambda$ (in the ultraviolet), then
>
> $$
> n = A + \frac{B}{\lambda^2} + O(\lambda^{-4}), \qquad A^2 = 1 + \sum_jA_j, \qquad B = \frac{1}{2A}\sum_jA_j\lambda_j^2 > 0 .
> $$
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.4, eq. (6.37), and Problem 6.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §5, eq. (26)*

^thm-b13-1-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Drop $i\gamma_j\omega$ in [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-7|Theorem §B13.1.7]]. With $\omega = 2\pi c/\lambda$ and $\omega_j = 2\pi c/\lambda_j$,
>
> $$
> \omega_j^2 - \omega^2 = (2\pi c)^2\,\frac{\lambda^2 - \lambda_j^2}{\lambda^2\lambda_j^2}, \qquad \frac{f_j\omega_p^2}{\omega_j^2 - \omega^2} = \frac{f_j\omega_p^2\lambda_j^2}{(2\pi c)^2}\,\frac{\lambda^2}{\lambda^2 - \lambda_j^2} = A_j\,\frac{\lambda^2}{\lambda^2 - \lambda_j^2},
> $$
>
> since $\lambda_j^2/(2\pi c)^2 = 1/\omega_j^2$. For $\lambda_j \ll \lambda$, the geometric series gives $\lambda^2/(\lambda^2 - \lambda_j^2) = 1 + \lambda_j^2/\lambda^2 + O(\lambda^{-4})$, so $n^2 = A^2 + 2AB/\lambda^2 + O(\lambda^{-4})$ with $A$, $B$ as stated, and $\sqrt{A^2 + u} = A + u/2A + O(u^2)$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) with $u = 2AB/\lambda^2$.

^der-b13-1-8

*Uses:* [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-7|Theorem §B13.1.7]], [[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]

> [!law] Law §B13.1.9: Cauchy's Dispersion Formula
> In its range of transparency the index of a glass, of water and of many other transparent materials is fitted well by
>
> $$
> n(\lambda) = A + \frac{B}{\lambda^2} \qquad \Bigl(\text{or } A + \frac{B}{\lambda^2} + \frac{C}{\lambda^4}\Bigr),
> $$
>
> with $\lambda$ the vacuum wavelength and constants $A$, $B > 0$ fitted for each material. Level A quoted this form ([[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^rem-a4-3-3|Remark: Where dispersion comes from]]); [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-8|Theorem §B13.1.8]] explains why it works and what $A$ and $B$ measure. Sellmeier's formula, with one or more fitted terms, matches data over a wider range.
>
> *Valid when:* $\lambda$ is well above all ultraviolet absorption wavelengths and well below the infrared bands, which pull $n$ down at long wavelengths; it fails near any absorption band, where dispersion becomes anomalous.
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §5, eq. (26) and Figure 3 · Fowles §6.4*

^law-b13-1-9

> [!caution] Caution: Schwartz's Cauchy coefficient
> For one undamped resonance in the dilute approximation, $n = 1 + \omega_p^2/2(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)$, the expansion gives $B = 2\pi^2c^2\omega_p^2/\omega_0^4 = 2\pi^2c^2q^2\rho/(\varepsilon_0m\omega_0^4)$, with $\rho$ the number density. Schwartz's eq. (26) prints $2\pi^2q^2\rho/(m\omega_0^4)$, without the factor $c^2/\varepsilon_0$ (which also drops out of the wavelength form of his eq. (25)); the units show the slip: $B$ must be an area.

> [!example] Example §B13.1.1: Where Does Crown Glass Absorb?
> [[§A4.3 Total Internal Reflection and Dispersion#^law-a4-3-2|Law §A4.3.2]] gives for crown glass $n = 1.512$, $1.518$, $1.524$, $1.530$ at $660$, $580$, $470$, $410\ \mathrm{nm}$.
>
> *(a) Cauchy fit* through the two end points:
>
> $$
> B = \frac{1.530 - 1.512}{410^{-2} - 660^{-2}}\ \mathrm{nm^2} = \frac{0.018}{3.653 \times 10^{-6}}\ \mathrm{nm^2} = 4.93 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{nm^2}, \qquad A = 1.512 - \frac{4.93 \times 10^3}{660^2} = 1.501 .
> $$
>
> It predicts $1.515$ at $580\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $1.523$ at $470\ \mathrm{nm}$, within $0.003$ and $0.001$ of the table.
>
> *(b) One-term Sellmeier fit*, $n^2 = 1 + A_1\lambda^2/(\lambda^2 - \lambda_1^2)$, through the same points. With $n^2 - 1 = 1.28614$ at $660\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $1.34090$ at $410\ \mathrm{nm}$, the ratio $r = 1.04258$ must equal $(1 - \lambda_1^2/660^2)/(1 - \lambda_1^2/410^2)$, so
>
> $$
> \lambda_1^2 = \frac{r - 1}{r/410^2 - 1/660^2}\ \mathrm{nm^2} = 1.090 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{nm^2}, \qquad \lambda_1 = 104\ \mathrm{nm}, \qquad A_1 = 1.28614\Bigl(1 - \frac{\lambda_1^2}{660^2}\Bigr) = 1.254 .
> $$
>
> The visible dispersion of crown glass is that of a single effective resonance in the far ultraviolet, at $104\ \mathrm{nm}$ (photon energy $hc/\lambda_1 = 12\ \mathrm{eV}$), and the electrons' static contribution is $n^2 = 1 + A_1 = 2.25$, whose square root $1.50$ is Cauchy's $A$ (Theorem §B13.1.8). Its predictions, $1.5152$ at $580\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $1.5228$ at $470\ \mathrm{nm}$, agree with (a).
>
> *(c) Group index.* From the Sellmeier form, $dn/d\lambda = -A_1\lambda\lambda_1^2/[n(\lambda^2 - \lambda_1^2)^2] = -4.94 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$ at $580\ \mathrm{nm}$, so the group index ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^ex-b6-2-3|Example §B6.2.3]]) is
>
> $$
> n_g = n - \lambda\frac{dn}{d\lambda} = 1.5152 + 580 \times 4.94 \times 10^{-5} = 1.544 ,
> $$
>
> a little below the $1.555$ that Example §B6.2.3 obtained from a coarse difference quotient of the rounded table. The fit also gives the curvature $d^2n/d\lambda^2 = 2.7 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{nm^{-2}}$ at $580\ \mathrm{nm}$, which the table was too coarse to show: the group velocity itself depends on wavelength, so pulses in glass spread.
>
> *Source: data from UP3 Table 1.2 (via Law §A4.3.2); fits after Fowles §6.4 and Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §5*

> [!remark] Remark: The quantum picture of absorption lines
> In quantum mechanics a resonance frequency of the model is a transition frequency $\omega_j = (E_j - E_0)/\hbar$ between atomic or molecular energy levels, where a photon of that energy is absorbed ([[§A5.4 Atomic Spectra, X-Rays and Lasers#^thm-a5-4-1|QM Theorem §A5.4.1]]): the absorption bands of Theorem §B13.1.7 are the absorption lines of the spectrum. Quantum theory gives a dispersion formula of the same form, and in addition predicts the oscillator strengths $f_j$ and the widths $\gamma_j$, which the classical model can only fit. The width of a line and the coherence time of the light it emits are related in [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^thm-b10-2-5|Theorem §B10.2.5]]; where the widths come from: [[§B10.2 Temporal Coherence and Line Width#^rem-b10-2-2|§B10.2, Remark: Where line widths come from]].
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.1 and §6.5*

^rem-b13-1-5

> [!remark] Remark: Beyond linear response
> The restoring force of the model is linear because ordinary light fields are tiny compared with the fields that bind electrons in atoms. Laser fields can approach them; then $\vec P$ acquires terms in $E^2$, $E^3$, …, and a wave at $\omega$ drives polarization at $2\omega$, $3\omega$: optical harmonics. The $E^2$ term needs a crystal without inversion symmetry, and efficient frequency doubling needs the fundamental and the harmonic to travel in step, arranged with the two indices of a birefringent crystal ([[§B8.3 Birefringence, Wave Plates and Optical Activity|§B8.3]]). Nonlinear optics: Chapter C5 (planned).
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.12*
