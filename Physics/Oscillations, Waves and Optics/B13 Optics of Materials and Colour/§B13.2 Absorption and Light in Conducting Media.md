*Sources: Fowles, Introduction to Modern Optics, §6.5–6.6 · Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lecture 16.*

Why are metals opaque and shiny, and why do they become transparent in the ultraviolet? Electromagnetism level A explained steady conduction with the Drude model of free electrons colliding with the lattice ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^mod-a5-2-3|EM Model §A5.2.3]], [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-4|EM Theorem §A5.2.4]]). This section drives the same electrons with the field of a light wave. They are the oscillators of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model|§B13.1]] without a spring, so the complex index of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-3|Def. §B13.1.3]] applies with $\omega_0 = 0$. The result has three regimes, separated by the collision rate $1/\tau$ and the plasma frequency $\omega_p$: an absorbing good conductor with a thin skin depth, a nearly perfect reflector, and a transparent medium with $n < 1$. The second half treats reflection from an absorbing medium, at normal and oblique incidence, where the Fresnel coefficients of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]] become complex.

## Free electrons at optical frequencies

> [!model] Model §B13.2.1: Drude Model at Optical Frequencies
> A metal contains $N$ conduction electrons per unit volume, of charge $-e$ and mass $m$, free of any restoring force. Collisions with the lattice act on each as an average drag $-(m/\tau)\vec v$, with a **relaxation time** $\tau$ independent of the field, so that
>
> $$
> m\frac{d\vec v}{dt} + \frac{m}{\tau}\vec v = -e\vec E, \qquad \vec J = -Ne\vec v .
> $$
>
> This is [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^mod-a5-2-3|EM Model §A5.2.3]] in the drag form of [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^rem-a5-2-2|EM §A5.2, Remark: Collisions act like linear drag]], now with a field that changes in times comparable to $\tau$. It is the Lorentz model of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^mod-b13-1-2|Model §B13.1.2]] with $\omega_0 = 0$ and $\gamma = 1/\tau$, written for the current instead of the polarization. Bound electrons, magnetic forces and quantum effects are ignored.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.5, eqs. (6.38)–(6.39)*

^mod-b13-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.2: Conductivity at Frequency ω
> In the Drude model a transient current decays as $e^{-t/\tau}$; a static field gives Ohm's law $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ ([[§A5.3 Resistivity, Resistance and Ohm's Law#^law-a5-3-1|EM Law §A5.3.1]]) with
>
> $$
> \sigma = \frac{Ne^2\tau}{m} ,
> $$
>
> the result of [[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^thm-a5-2-4|EM Theorem §A5.2.4]]. A field $\operatorname{Re}[\hat Ee^{-i\omega t}]$ drives the current
>
> $$
> \hat J = \frac{\sigma}{1 - i\omega\tau}\,\hat E ,
> $$
>
> in phase with the field for $\omega\tau \ll 1$, lagging it by nearly a quarter period for $\omega\tau \gg 1$, when the electrons' inertia, not the collisions, limits the current. In terms of the plasma frequency ([[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-2|Def. §B13.1.2]]), $\sigma/\varepsilon_0 = \omega_p^2\tau$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.5, eqs. (6.40)–(6.45), (6.54)*

^thm-b13-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Multiply the equation of motion by $-Ne/m$: $d\vec J/dt + \vec J/\tau = (Ne^2/m)\vec E$. With $\vec E = \vec 0$ the solution is $\vec J_0e^{-t/\tau}$ ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]]). A static field gives the steady state $\vec J = (Ne^2\tau/m)\vec E$. For $e^{-i\omega t}$, $d/dt \to -i\omega$: $(-i\omega + 1/\tau)\hat J = (Ne^2/m)\hat E$, i.e. $\hat J = \sigma\hat E/(1 - i\omega\tau)$. The phase of $1/(1 - i\omega\tau)$ is $\arctan(\omega\tau)$, between $0$ and $\pi/2$; in the optics convention a positive phase of the response is a lag. Finally $\sigma/\varepsilon_0 = Ne^2\tau/(m\varepsilon_0) = \omega_p^2\tau$.

^der-b13-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^mod-b13-2-1|Model §B13.2.1]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-3|Theorem §B1.1.3]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-2|Def. §B13.1.2]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.3: Complex Index of a Free-Electron Metal
> In the Drude model, homogeneous plane waves $e^{i(\tilde kz - \omega t)}$ have $\tilde k = \tilde n\omega/c$ with
>
> $$
> \tilde n^2 = 1 + \frac{i\sigma}{\varepsilon_0\omega(1 - i\omega\tau)} = 1 - \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2 + i\omega/\tau},
> $$
>
> that is,
>
> $$
> n^2 - \kappa^2 = 1 - \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2 + \tau^{-2}}, \qquad 2n\kappa = \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2 + \tau^{-2}}\,\frac{1}{\omega\tau} ,
> $$
>
> and $n$, $\kappa$ follow as in [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-4|Theorem §B13.1.4]]. They depend only on $\omega_p$, $\tau$ and $\omega$. The first form is the Lorentz result $1 + \omega_p^2/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega)$ with $\omega_0 = 0$, $\gamma = 1/\tau$. If bound electrons also contribute (poor conductors, semiconductors), their Lorentz terms of [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-7|Theorem §B13.1.7]] are added to $\tilde n^2$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.5, eqs. (6.46)–(6.48), (6.53)–(6.57)*

^thm-b13-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $\vec P = \vec 0$ and $\hat J$ from [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-2|Theorem §B13.2.2]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-1|Theorem §B13.1.1]] for a transverse plane wave reads $-\tilde k^2 + \omega^2/c^2 = \mu_0(-i\omega)\sigma/(1 - i\omega\tau)$. Divide by $\omega^2/c^2$ and use $\mu_0c^2 = 1/\varepsilon_0$: $\tilde n^2 = 1 + i\sigma/[\varepsilon_0\omega(1 - i\omega\tau)]$. Insert $\sigma/\varepsilon_0 = \omega_p^2\tau$, divide numerator and denominator by $\tau$, and then multiply both by $i$:
>
> $$
> \frac{i\omega_p^2\tau}{\omega(1 - i\omega\tau)} = \frac{i\omega_p^2}{\omega/\tau - i\omega^2} = -\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2 + i\omega/\tau} .
> $$
>
> Multiplying by the conjugate $\omega^2 - i\omega/\tau$ separates real and imaginary parts: $-\omega_p^2(\omega^2 - i\omega/\tau)/(\omega^4 + \omega^2/\tau^2)$, which gives the two equations.

^der-b13-2-3

*Uses:* [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-2|Theorem §B13.2.2]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-1|Theorem §B13.1.1]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-3|Def. §B13.1.3]]

## Three regimes

> [!definition] Definition §B13.2.1: Skin Depth
> The **skin depth** $\delta$ of a medium is the distance over which the amplitude of a wave entering it falls to $1/e$ of its value at the surface:
>
> $$
> \delta = \frac{1}{\alpha} = \frac{c}{\kappa\omega} = \frac{\lambda_0}{2\pi\kappa},
> $$
>
> with $\alpha$, $\kappa$ as in [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-3|Def. §B13.1.3]]. The intensity falls to $1/e$ in $\delta/2$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.5, eq. (6.52)*

^def-b13-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.4: Good Conductor, Reflector, Transparent Medium
> For a metal with $\omega_p\tau \gg 1$ (typically $\tau \sim 10^{-14}$–$10^{-13}\ \mathrm s$, while $\omega_p \sim 10^{16}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$):
> 1. **Good conductor**, $\omega\tau \ll 1$ and $\omega \ll \sigma/\varepsilon_0$: $\tilde n^2 \approx i\sigma/\varepsilon_0\omega$, so
>
> $$
> n \approx \kappa \approx \sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0\omega}} \gg 1, \qquad \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\mu_0\sigma\omega}} = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda_0}{\pi c\mu_0\sigma}} .
> $$
>
>    The wave is damped within a fraction $1/2\pi n$ of a vacuum wavelength: good conductors are opaque.
> 2. **Reflecting region**, $1/\tau \ll \omega < \omega_p$: $\tilde n^2 \approx 1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2 < 0$, so
>
> $$
> n \approx 0, \qquad \kappa \approx \sqrt{\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2} - 1} .
> $$
>
>    The field decays over $c/\sqrt{\omega_p^2 - \omega^2}$ without travelling and with almost no absorption; nearly all the light is reflected ([[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-5|Theorem §B13.2.5]]).
> 3. **Transparent region**, $\omega > \omega_p$: $\tilde n^2 \approx 1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2 \in (0, 1)$, so $\kappa \approx 0$ and $n = \sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2} < 1$. With $\tilde k = n\omega/c$ this is the dispersion relation $\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2$ of light in a plasma ([[§B6.2 Dispersion, Phase Velocity and Group Velocity#^thm-b6-2-1|Theorem §B6.2.1]]), here derived.
>
> Qualitatively this is observed for the alkali metals and for good conductors such as silver, gold and copper: opaque and highly reflecting up to the visible or near ultraviolet, transparent beyond.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.5, eqs. (6.49)–(6.56) and Figure 6.4*

^thm-b13-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Use the first form of [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-3|Theorem §B13.2.3]]. (1) For $\omega\tau \ll 1$, $1 - i\omega\tau \approx 1$ and $\tilde n^2 \approx 1 + i\sigma/\varepsilon_0\omega$; for $\sigma/\varepsilon_0\omega \gg 1$ the $1$ is negligible. Since $\sqrt i = (1 + i)/\sqrt2$, $\tilde n \approx (1 + i)\sqrt{\sigma/2\varepsilon_0\omega}$. Then $\delta = c/\kappa\omega = \sqrt{2\varepsilon_0c^2/\sigma\omega} = \sqrt{2/\mu_0\sigma\omega}$, and with $\omega = 2\pi c/\lambda_0$, $\delta^2 = \lambda_0/(\pi c\mu_0\sigma)$. (2), (3) For $\omega\tau \gg 1$ use the second form: $\omega^2 + i\omega/\tau \approx \omega^2$, so $\tilde n^2 \approx 1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2$, real up to a small positive imaginary part $\omega_p^2/(\omega^3\tau)$. If it is negative, $\tilde n$ is nearly $i\sqrt{\omega_p^2/\omega^2 - 1}$; if positive, nearly real. In (3), $c^2\tilde k^2 = \omega^2\tilde n^2 = \omega^2 - \omega_p^2$.

^der-b13-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-3|Theorem §B13.2.3]], [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^def-b13-2-1|Def. §B13.2.1]], [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-3|Def. §B13.1.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Orders of magnitude of τ and ω_p
> Fowles gives "typical" values $\tau \sim 10^{-13}\ \mathrm s$ and $\omega_p \sim 10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the latter "corresponding to the visible and near ultraviolet". As an angular frequency, $10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ is a vacuum wavelength $2\pi c/\omega_p = 1.9\ \mu\mathrm m$, in the infrared; a plasma wavelength in the near ultraviolet needs $\omega_p \sim 10^{16}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, as his own data for silver give ([[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^ex-b13-2-2|Example §B13.2.2]]: $6.9 \times 10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$). For copper, Electromagnetism level A finds $\tau = 2.5 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm s$ ([[§A5.2 Conduction in Metals#^ex-a5-2-2|EM Example §A5.2.2]]).

^cau-b13-2-1

> [!example] Example §B13.2.1: The Skin Depth of Copper
> For copper, $\sigma = 5.8 \times 10^7\ \mathrm{S/m}$. In the good-conductor regime, $\delta = \sqrt{2/\mu_0\sigma\omega}$:
>
> | frequency | $\omega$ ($\mathrm{s^{-1}}$) | $\delta$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | $60\ \mathrm{Hz}$ (mains) | $377$ | $8.5\ \mathrm{mm}$ |
> | $1\ \mathrm{MHz}$ (AM radio) | $6.3 \times 10^6$ | $66\ \mu\mathrm m$ |
> | $1\ \mathrm{GHz}$ | $6.3 \times 10^9$ | $2.1\ \mu\mathrm m$ |
> | $300\ \mathrm{GHz}$ ($\lambda_0 = 1\ \mathrm{mm}$) | $1.9 \times 10^{12}$ | $0.12\ \mu\mathrm m$ |
>
> The last line is Fowles's: about $10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mm}$ for $1$-mm microwaves. The regime's conditions hold throughout: even at $1\ \mathrm{mm}$, $\sigma/\varepsilon_0\omega = 3.5 \times 10^6 \gg 1$ and, with $\tau = 2.5 \times 10^{-14}\ \mathrm s$, $\omega\tau = 0.05$. Mains current fills a household wire; a gigahertz signal flows in a surface layer a few micrometres thick, which is why high-frequency conductors are often plated with a thin layer of a better conductor. At $1\ \mathrm{mm}$ copper has $n = \kappa = \sqrt{\sigma/2\varepsilon_0\omega} = 1.3 \times 10^3$, and the Hagen–Rubens formula ([[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-6|Theorem §B13.2.6]]) gives a reflectance $1 - 2/n = 0.9985$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.5 (copper at 1 mm); τ from EM Example §A5.2.2*

^ex-b13-2-1

> [!example] Example §B13.2.2: Silver in the Near Infrared
> Fowles's Problem 6.6: silver has $\sigma = 6.8 \times 10^7\ \mathrm{S/m}$; take the carriers to be free electrons of density $N = 1.5 \times 10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Then
>
> $$
> \omega_p = 56.4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\sqrt{1.5 \times 10^{28}} = 6.9 \times 10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}, \qquad \tau = \frac{m\sigma}{Ne^2} = \frac{(9.11 \times 10^{-31})(6.8 \times 10^7)}{(1.5 \times 10^{28})(1.60 \times 10^{-19})^2}\ \mathrm s = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}\ \mathrm s .
> $$
>
> The plasma wavelength is $2\pi c/\omega_p = 273\ \mathrm{nm}$, in the near ultraviolet. At $\lambda_0 = 1\ \mu\mathrm m$, $\omega = 1.88 \times 10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $\omega\tau = 303$ and $\omega/\omega_p = 0.273$: the reflecting regime. [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-3|Theorem §B13.2.3]] gives, solved exactly,
>
> $$
> \tilde n = 0.0063 + 3.53\,i, \qquad R = \Bigl|\frac{1 - \tilde n}{1 + \tilde n}\Bigr|^2 = 0.998, \qquad \delta = \frac{c}{\kappa\omega} = 45\ \mathrm{nm} ,
> $$
>
> close to the approximation $\kappa \approx \sqrt{\omega_p^2/\omega^2 - 1} = 3.53$. The light penetrates about $45\ \mathrm{nm}$ and is almost entirely reflected. The Hagen–Rubens formula would give $1 - \sqrt{8\varepsilon_0\omega/\sigma} = 0.956$, wrong here because $\omega\tau \gg 1$.
>
> *Source: Fowles Problem 6.6*

^ex-b13-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: Faraday cages
> A grid of conducting wires reflects radio waves and microwaves almost completely if its spacing $d$ is smaller than the wavelength, although most of its area is holes. The wave drives the nearly free electrons in the wires, and their radiation, summed over the grid, is a plane wave that cancels the incident wave behind the grid; that requires $d \lesssim \lambda$, so that the waves from the separate wires merge into a plane front. A microwave-oven door ($2.5\ \mathrm{GHz}$, $\lambda = 12\ \mathrm{cm}$) has holes about $0.5\ \mathrm{cm}$ across, and the Arecibo radio dish was a metal mesh with a spacing of about $10\ \mathrm{cm}$ for wavelengths near $1\ \mathrm m$. For $d \gg \lambda$ the grid would let most of the light through, as the particle picture suggests. Schwartz stresses that the bound-electron model with springs does not describe conductors; in the language of this section the electrons have $\omega_0 = 0$, and a solid metal surface is the limit of a fine grid.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 16 §6*

## Reflection at normal incidence

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.5: Normal-Incidence Reflection from an Absorbing Medium
> Light in vacuum (or air) falls normally on a medium of complex index $\tilde n = n + i\kappa$. The amplitude reflection coefficient, its modulus squared and its phase are
>
> $$
> r = \frac{1 - \tilde n}{1 + \tilde n}, \qquad R = |r|^2 = \frac{(1 - n)^2 + \kappa^2}{(1 + n)^2 + \kappa^2}, \qquad \tan\varphi = \frac{2\kappa}{n^2 + \kappa^2 - 1},
> $$
>
> with $r = |r|e^{i\varphi}$ and $\varphi$ in the quadrant of $(\operatorname{Re}r, \operatorname{Im}r) = (1 - n^2 - \kappa^2,\ -2\kappa)/|1 + \tilde n|^2$; as $\kappa \to 0$, $\varphi \to \pm\pi$ for $n > 1$ and $\varphi \to 0$ for $n < 1$. For $\kappa = 0$ this is [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-2|Theorem §B9.2.2]] with $n_1 = 1$; a large $\kappa$ makes $R$ approach $1$; for $n = 0$ exactly, $|r| = 1$ for any $\kappa$.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.6, eqs. (6.82)–(6.83), and Problem 6.7*

^thm-b13-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For a wave $\vec E_0e^{i(\tilde kz - \omega t)}$, Faraday's law $\nabla\times\vec E = -\mu_0\partial_t\vec H$ gives $i\tilde k\,\hat z\times\vec E = i\omega\mu_0\vec H$, so $\vec H = (\tilde n/\mu_0c)\,\hat z\times\vec E$, the relation of [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]] with $n$ made complex; a wave travelling toward $-z$ has the opposite sign. Let $\vec E$ be along $x$ with amplitudes $E_i$, $E_r$ (vacuum, $z < 0$) and $E_t$ (medium, $z > 0$). The tangential components of $\vec E$ and $\vec H$ are continuous at $z = 0$ ([[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]]):
>
> $$
> E_i + E_r = E_t, \qquad E_i - E_r = \tilde nE_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{E_r}{E_i} = \frac{1 - \tilde n}{1 + \tilde n} .
> $$
>
> Then $R = |1 - \tilde n|^2/|1 + \tilde n|^2$. Multiplying numerator and denominator of $r$ by $(1 + \tilde n)^* = 1 + n - i\kappa$, the numerator becomes $(1 - n - i\kappa)(1 + n - i\kappa) = 1 - n^2 - \kappa^2 - 2i\kappa$, which gives $\tan\varphi$ and the quadrant. For $n = 0$, $r = (1 - i\kappa)/(1 + i\kappa)$ is a ratio of complex conjugates, of modulus $1$.

^der-b13-2-5

*Uses:* [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^def-b13-1-3|Def. §B13.1.3]], [[§B8.1 Polarization States of Plane Waves#^thm-b8-1-2|Theorem §B8.1.2]], [[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-1|Theorem §B9.1.1]], [[§13 Polynomials#^ladr-4-2|LADR Def. 4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: The impedance picture
> The ratio $E/H$ of a wave in a medium is $Z_0/\tilde n$, so the medium acts like a load of impedance proportional to $1/\tilde n$ ([[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^rem-b9-2-1|§B9.2, Remark: Light at normal incidence is a wave on a string]]), and $r = (1 - \tilde n)/(1 + \tilde n)$ is the reflection coefficient of a terminating load, [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^thm-b5-2-5|Theorem §B5.2.5]] (up to the sign convention for which field is quoted, and with the optics complex convention). A metal in the reflecting regime, $\tilde n \approx i\kappa$, is a purely reactive load: it stores and returns the energy each cycle and absorbs none, so $|r| = 1$, as for the string ending on a ring ([[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^ex-b5-2-2|Example §B5.2.2]]). The small real part $n$ that collisions add is the resistive part, and it alone lets energy into the metal.

^rem-b13-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.6: Hagen–Rubens Formula
> In the good-conductor regime of [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-4|Theorem §B13.2.4]] ($\omega\tau \ll 1$, $\sigma \gg \varepsilon_0\omega$), the normal reflectance is
>
> $$
> R \approx 1 - \frac{2}{n} \approx 1 - \sqrt{\frac{8\varepsilon_0\omega}{\sigma}} .
> $$
>
> So $(1 - R)^2$ is proportional to the frequency: metals reflect better the longer the wavelength. Good conductors (copper, silver, gold) reflect very well in the near infrared, $\lambda \approx 1$–$2\ \mu\mathrm m$, and almost perfectly in the far infrared, $\lambda > 20\ \mu\mathrm m$; the formula has been confirmed for several metals in the far infrared.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.6, eq. (6.84)*

^thm-b13-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $n = \kappa$ in [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-5|Theorem §B13.2.5]],
>
> $$
> R = \frac{(n - 1)^2 + n^2}{(n + 1)^2 + n^2} = \frac{2n^2 - 2n + 1}{2n^2 + 2n + 1} = 1 - \frac{4n}{2n^2 + 2n + 1} = 1 - \frac2n + O(n^{-2}),
> $$
>
> and $2/n = 2\sqrt{2\varepsilon_0\omega/\sigma} = \sqrt{8\varepsilon_0\omega/\sigma}$ by [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-4|Theorem §B13.2.4]] (1).

*Uses:* [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-5|Theorem §B13.2.5]], [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-4|Theorem §B13.2.4]]

> [!example] Example §B13.2.3: What a Reflectance Does Not Tell
> (a) Fowles's Problem 6.5: a metal reflects $80\%$ at normal incidence and has $\kappa = 4$. Then
>
> $$
> \frac{(n - 1)^2 + 16}{(n + 1)^2 + 16} = 0.80 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0.2n^2 - 3.6n + 3.4 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n^2 - 18n + 17 = 0,
> $$
>
> so $n = 1$ or $n = 17$. Both give $R = 0.80$; the reflectance alone does not fix $\tilde n$.
>
> (b) Problem 6.8: aluminium at $500\ \mathrm{nm}$, with Fowles's values $n = 1.5$, $\kappa = 3.2$. Then $R = (0.25 + 10.24)/(6.25 + 10.24) = 0.636$, and $\tan\varphi = 6.4/11.49 = 0.557$ with $\operatorname{Re}r < 0$, $\operatorname{Im}r < 0$, so $\varphi = -180^\circ + 29.1^\circ = -150.9^\circ$. The reflected field at the surface is $|r|E_0\cos(\omega t + 150.9^\circ)$ for an incident $E_0\cos\omega t$; a dielectric would give $180^\circ$. (In the $e^{+i\omega t}$ convention the same field has $\varphi = +150.9^\circ$.) Measuring a phase, or the reflectance at two angles of incidence, adds the information that (a) lacked: this is the idea of ellipsometry (Theorem §B13.2.8).
>
> *Source: Fowles Problems 6.5, 6.7, 6.8*

## Oblique incidence

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.7: Refraction into an Absorbing Medium
> Light from vacuum falls at angle $\theta$ on a plane surface $z = 0$ of a medium with $\tilde n = n + i\kappa$, $\kappa > 0$. The refracted wave $\vec E_0e^{i(\tilde{\vec k}\cdot\vec r - \omega t)}$ has a complex wave vector $\tilde{\vec k} = \vec k + i\vec\alpha$ with:
> 1. $\vec\alpha$ normal to the surface: the planes of constant amplitude are parallel to it, whatever the angle of incidence;
> 2. $\vec k$ in the plane of incidence at an angle $\phi$ to the normal with $k\sin\phi = k_0\sin\theta$, $k_0 = \omega/c$;
> 3. $k\cos\phi + i\alpha = k_0\sqrt{\tilde n^2 - \sin^2\theta}$, the root with positive real and imaginary parts.
>
> For $\theta \neq 0$ the planes of constant phase (normal to $\vec k$) are tilted against those of constant amplitude: the refracted wave is **inhomogeneous**. Its wave number $k$, and so the angle of refraction, depend on $\theta$; Snell's law holds with $k/k_0$ in place of a fixed index. Defining a complex angle $\tilde\phi$ by $\sin\tilde\phi = \sin\theta/\tilde n$, item 3 reads $k\cos\phi + i\alpha = k_0\tilde n\cos\tilde\phi$: formally, Snell's law with a complex index and a complex angle.
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.6, eqs. (6.58)–(6.73) and Figure 6.5*

^thm-b13-2-7

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At $z = 0$ the incident, reflected and refracted phases must agree for all $x$, $y$ and $t$, as in [[§B9.1 Boundary Conditions and the Laws of Reflection and Refraction#^thm-b9-1-2|Theorem §B9.1.2]]: $\vec k_i\cdot\vec r = (\vec k + i\vec\alpha)\cdot\vec r$ for every $\vec r$ in the surface. The real part gives (2); the imaginary part, $\vec\alpha\cdot\vec r = 0$ for all such $\vec r$, gives (1). The wave equation $\nabla^2\vec E + \tilde n^2k_0^2\vec E = \vec 0$ (as in [[§B13.1 Dispersion in Dielectrics and the Lorentz Model#^thm-b13-1-4|Theorem §B13.1.4]]) requires $\tilde{\vec k}\cdot\tilde{\vec k} = \tilde n^2k_0^2$, a dot product without complex conjugation. With $\vec k = (k\sin\phi, 0, k\cos\phi)$ and $\vec\alpha = (0, 0, \alpha)$,
>
> $$
> \tilde{\vec k}\cdot\tilde{\vec k} = k^2\sin^2\phi + (k\cos\phi + i\alpha)^2 = k_0^2\sin^2\theta + (k\cos\phi + i\alpha)^2 = \tilde n^2k_0^2 ,
> $$
>
> which is (3). The number $\tilde n^2 - \sin^2\theta$ has imaginary part $2n\kappa > 0$, so its square roots lie in the first and third quadrants; a wave that enters the medium and decays into it needs $k\cos\phi > 0$ and $\alpha > 0$, the first-quadrant root. Finally $\tilde n\cos\tilde\phi = \tilde n\sqrt{1 - \sin^2\theta/\tilde n^2} = \sqrt{\tilde n^2 - \sin^2\theta}$.

> [!theorem] Theorem §B13.2.8: Fresnel Coefficients of an Absorbing Medium
> With $\tilde q = \sqrt{\tilde n^2 - \sin^2\theta} = \tilde n\cos\tilde\phi$ from [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-7|Theorem §B13.2.7]], the amplitude reflection coefficients for light from vacuum are
>
> $$
> r_s = \frac{\cos\theta - \tilde n\cos\tilde\phi}{\cos\theta + \tilde n\cos\tilde\phi}, \qquad r_p = \frac{-\tilde n\cos\theta + \cos\tilde\phi}{\tilde n\cos\theta + \cos\tilde\phi} ,
> $$
>
> the dielectric Fresnel coefficients of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^thm-b9-2-1|Theorem §B9.2.1]] (in their form with the relative index) with $n$ and the angle of refraction made complex ($r_p$ in the sign convention of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^cau-b9-2-1|Caution: Sign conventions for r_p]], Fowles's; the reflectances do not depend on it). At normal incidence both reduce to [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-5|Theorem §B13.2.5]]. For a typical metal $R_s = |r_s|^2$ rises steadily from its normal value to $1$ at grazing incidence, while $R_p = |r_p|^2$ passes through a shallow, nonzero minimum at the **principal angle of incidence**, the counterpart of Brewster's angle, before rising to $1$. Linearly polarized light that is neither pure s nor pure p is reflected elliptically polarized, because $r_s$ and $r_p$ differ in phase; measuring the ellipse determines $n$ and $\kappa$ (**ellipsometry**).
>
> *Source: Fowles §6.6, eqs. (6.74)–(6.81), Figure 6.6, and Problem 6.9*

^thm-b13-2-8

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *s polarization* ($\vec E$ along $y$, normal to the plane of incidence). By Faraday's law, as in the derivation of [[§B13.2 Absorption and Light in Conducting Media#^thm-b13-2-5|Theorem §B13.2.5]], $\vec H = \tilde{\vec k}\times\vec E/\mu_0\omega$, whose tangential ($x$) component is $-\tilde k_zE_y/\mu_0\omega$. The $z$-components of the wave vectors are $k_0\cos\theta$ (incident), $-k_0\cos\theta$ (reflected) and $k_0\tilde q$ (refracted, Theorem §B13.2.7 (3)). Continuity of $E_y$ and of $H_x$ at $z = 0$:
>
> $$
> E_i + E_r = E_t, \qquad \cos\theta\,(E_i - E_r) = \tilde q\,E_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r_s = \frac{\cos\theta - \tilde q}{\cos\theta + \tilde q} .
> $$
>
> *p polarization* ($\vec H$ along $y$). In a medium of permittivity $\varepsilon_0\tilde n^2$, Ampère–Maxwell gives $\vec E = -\tilde{\vec k}\times\vec H/(\varepsilon_0\tilde n^2\omega)$, with tangential component $E_x = \tilde k_zH_y/(\varepsilon_0\tilde n^2\omega)$. Continuity of $H_y$ and $E_x$:
>
> $$
> H_i + H_r = H_t, \qquad \cos\theta\,(H_i - H_r) = \frac{\tilde q}{\tilde n^2}\,H_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{H_r}{H_i} = \frac{\tilde n^2\cos\theta - \tilde q}{\tilde n^2\cos\theta + \tilde q} = \frac{\tilde n\cos\theta - \cos\tilde\phi}{\tilde n\cos\theta + \cos\tilde\phi} .
> $$
>
> Incident and reflected waves are in the same medium, so $E_r/E_i = \pm H_r/H_i$, the sign depending on the convention for the positive direction of $\vec E$ in the plane of incidence; the convention of [[§B9.2 Fresnel Coefficients and the Brewster Angle#^def-b9-2-1|Def. §B9.2.1]] gives the minus sign, as for dielectrics. At $\theta = 0$, $\tilde q = \tilde n$ and $\cos\tilde\phi = 1$, and both coefficients have modulus $|1 - \tilde n|/|1 + \tilde n|$. The behaviour of $R_s$, $R_p$ is read off the formulas (figure below).

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The complex square roots in Theorems §B13.2.4 and §B13.2.7 are chosen by the physical requirement that the wave decay into the medium, the same rule as for the evanescent wave of total internal reflection ([[§B9.3 Total Internal Reflection and the Evanescent Wave|§B9.3]]), where $\tilde n$ is real but $\tilde n^2 - \sin^2\theta < 0$.
