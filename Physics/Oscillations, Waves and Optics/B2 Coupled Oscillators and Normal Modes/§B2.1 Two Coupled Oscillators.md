*Sources: Series Part I (the user's notes), §6.1–6.3 and §6.6 · Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lectures 3–4 · PHY 287 (Kandula, Fall 2023) · PHY 300 (Mihaly, Fall 2024).*

One oscillator has one frequency ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]]). Join two oscillators by a spring and neither moves simply any more, because each pushes on the other. Yet there are special motions in which both oscillate at one frequency: the normal modes. Level A met normal modes only on a continuous string, as the standing waves that fit between its ends ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^def-a2-6-2|Def. §A2.6.2]], [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]]). This section finds them for a discrete system as the eigenvectors of a matrix, and shows why they always exist: the matrix is symmetric, by Newton's third law. The same method carries Chapters B2–B3 from two masses to the wave equation.

## The coupled pair

> [!model] Model §B2.1.1: Two Coupled Oscillators
> Two blocks of equal mass $m$ slide on a line between two walls. Each block is tied to its wall by a spring of constant $k$, and the blocks are tied to each other by a **coupling spring** of constant $k_c$. The coordinates $x_1, x_2$ are the displacements of the blocks from equilibrium, both positive to the right. Assumptions:
> - the springs are massless and obey Hooke's law ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^law-a3-6-3|CM Law §A3.6.3]]) over the displacements that occur;
> - no friction or other damping;
> - the blocks move along the line only.
>
> Two identical simple pendulums of length $\ell$ joined by a light horizontal spring obey the same equations for small swings, with the pendulum's $g/\ell$ ([[§A1.4 Pendulums#^mod-a1-4-1|Model §A1.4.1]]) in place of $k/m$ and $x_1, x_2$ the horizontal displacements of the bobs.
>
> *Source: Series I §6.1 · PHY 287, week 11 coupled-oscillator slides (pendulums)*

^mod-b2-1-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.2: Equations of Motion of the Coupled Pair
> The blocks of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^mod-b2-1-1|Model §B2.1.1]] obey
>
> $$
> m\ddot x_1 = -kx_1 + k_c(x_2 - x_1), \qquad m\ddot x_2 = -kx_2 - k_c(x_2 - x_1) .
> $$
>
> With $\omega_0^2 = k/m$ and the **coupling frequency** $\omega_c^2 = k_c/m$, and $\vec x = (x_1, x_2)^{\mathsf T}$, this is the single matrix equation
>
> $$
> \ddot{\vec x} = -\mathsf A\,\vec x, \qquad \mathsf A = \begin{pmatrix} \omega_0^2 + \omega_c^2 & -\omega_c^2 \\ -\omega_c^2 & \omega_0^2 + \omega_c^2 \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> $\mathsf A$ is real and symmetric. The equations are **coupled**: $\ddot x_1$ depends on $x_2$ and $\ddot x_2$ on $x_1$, so neither can be solved alone.
>
> *Source: Series I §6.1–6.2 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 3 · PHY 287, Problem Set 9*

^thm-b2-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The coupling spring is where sign errors happen. Its extension beyond its relaxed length is $x_2 - x_1$: moving block 2 to the right stretches it, moving block 1 to the right compresses it. A stretched spring pulls both of its ends toward its middle, so it pulls block 1 to the right with $+k_c(x_2 - x_1)$ and block 2 to the left with $-k_c(x_2 - x_1)$. If the spring is compressed, $x_2 - x_1 < 0$ and both forces reverse, pushing the blocks apart, as they should. The two forces are equal and opposite, as Newton's third law requires ([[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|CM Principle §A3.5.1]]). The wall springs add $-kx_1$ and $-kx_2$. Newton's second law ([[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]) for each block gives the two equations. Dividing by $m$ and collecting,
>
> $$
> \ddot x_1 = -(\omega_0^2 + \omega_c^2)\,x_1 + \omega_c^2\,x_2, \qquad \ddot x_2 = \omega_c^2\,x_1 - (\omega_0^2 + \omega_c^2)\,x_2 ,
> $$
>
> which is the matrix equation. For the pendulums, the tangential gravity force on a bob is $-mg\sin\theta \approx -(mg/\ell)\,x$ for small angles ([[§A1.4 Pendulums#^thm-a1-4-2|Theorem §A1.4.2]]), which plays the part of $-kx$.

^der-b2-1-2

> [!caution] Caution: The coupling term
> - Check the sign of each coupling term by moving one block by hand while holding the other: pull block 2 to the right and the coupling spring drags block 1 to the right, so the $x_2$ term in the equation for block 1 is positive.
> - The coupling spring appears twice in each equation: $-k_c x_1$ (block 1 displaced, block 2 held) sits on the diagonal of $\mathsf A$ next to $-kx_1$, and $+k_c x_2$ off the diagonal. Forgetting the diagonal part gives the wrong frequencies.

^cau-b2-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.1: Mass, Stiffness and Dynamical Matrices
> A system of $N$ point masses $m_i$ with linear restoring forces has equations of motion $m_i\ddot x_i = -\sum_j K_{ij}x_j$, or
>
> $$
> \mathsf M\,\ddot{\vec x} = -\mathsf K\,\vec x, \qquad \mathsf M = \operatorname{diag}(m_1, \dots, m_N) .
> $$
>
> $\mathsf M$ is the **mass matrix** and $\mathsf K$ the **stiffness matrix**: $-K_{ij}$ is the force on mass $i$ per unit displacement of mass $j$ with all other masses held at equilibrium. The **dynamical matrix** is $\mathsf A = \mathsf M^{-1}\mathsf K$, so that $\ddot{\vec x} = -\mathsf A\,\vec x$. For the coupled pair, $\mathsf M = m\,\mathbb 1$, $\mathsf K = \begin{pmatrix} k + k_c & -k_c \\ -k_c & k + k_c\end{pmatrix}$, and $\mathsf A$ is the matrix of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 3 · Series I §6.2, §6.6*

^def-b2-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: A number promoted to a matrix
> $\ddot{\vec x} = -\mathsf A\,\vec x$ is the oscillator equation $\ddot x = -\omega_0^2x$ with the number $\omega_0^2$ promoted to a matrix. That suggests the strategy of the whole chapter: look for the directions in which $\mathsf A$ acts like a number, its eigenvectors. Along each of them the system is a single oscillator.

^rem-b2-1-1

## Normal modes as an eigenvalue problem

> [!definition] Definition §B2.1.2: Normal Mode of a Linear System
> A **normal mode** of $\ddot{\vec x} = -\mathsf A\,\vec x$ is a solution of the form
>
> $$
> \vec x(t) = \vec v\,\cos(\omega t - \phi),
> $$
>
> with $\vec v$ a constant nonzero real vector, the **mode shape**, and $\omega \ge 0$ the **normal frequency**. Every coordinate oscillates at the one frequency $\omega$, with amplitudes in the fixed ratios of the components of $\vec v$; components of opposite sign move in antiphase. This makes [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^def-a2-6-2|Def. §A2.6.2]] precise for a system with finitely many coordinates.
>
> *Source: Series I §6.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 3 · PHY 287, week 11 coupled-oscillator slides*

^def-b2-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.3: Normal Modes Are Eigenvectors
> $\vec x(t) = \vec v\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ with $\vec v \ne \vec 0$ solves $\ddot{\vec x} = -\mathsf A\,\vec x$ if and only if
>
> $$
> \mathsf A\,\vec v = \omega^2\,\vec v .
> $$
>
> So the squared normal frequencies are the eigenvalues of the dynamical matrix, the roots of the **characteristic equation** $\det\bigl(\mathsf A - \omega^2\,\mathbb 1\bigr) = 0$, and the mode shapes are the corresponding eigenvectors. Equivalently $\mathsf K\vec v = \omega^2\mathsf M\vec v$ and $\det(\mathsf K - \omega^2\mathsf M) = 0$.
>
> *Source: Series I §6.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 3*

^thm-b2-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> For $\vec x = \vec v\cos(\omega t - \phi)$, $\ddot{\vec x} = -\omega^2\vec v\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ and $-\mathsf A\vec x = -(\mathsf A\vec v)\cos(\omega t - \phi)$. These agree for all $t$ exactly when $\mathsf A\vec v = \omega^2\vec v$ (take a $t$ with $\cos(\omega t - \phi) \ne 0$). The series and Schwartz reach the same equation with the complex stand-in $\tilde{\vec x} = \vec v\,e^{i\omega t}$, whose real part is the mode ([[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]): each time derivative brings down $i\omega$, so $-\omega^2\vec v\,e^{i\omega t} = -\mathsf A\vec v\,e^{i\omega t}$, and $e^{i\omega t} \ne 0$ cancels.
>
> A nonzero $\vec v$ with $(\mathsf A - \omega^2\mathbb 1)\vec v = \vec 0$ exists exactly when $\mathsf A - \omega^2\mathbb 1$ is not invertible, that is, when its determinant vanishes ([[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-50|LADR Thm. 9.50]], [[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-51|LADR Thm. 9.51]]). Multiplying $\mathsf A\vec v = \omega^2\vec v$ by $\mathsf M$ gives the form with $\mathsf K$ and $\mathsf M$; $\det(\mathsf K - \omega^2\mathsf M) = \det\mathsf M\,\det(\mathsf A - \omega^2\mathbb 1)$ with $\det\mathsf M = m_1\cdots m_N \neq 0$.

^der-b2-1-3

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^def-b2-1-2|Def. §B2.1.2]], [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]], [[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-50|LADR Thm. 9.50]], [[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-51|LADR Thm. 9.51]], [[§B1.1 Linearity and the Complex-Exponential Method#^thm-b1-1-4|Theorem §B1.1.4]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.4: Normal Modes of the Coupled Pair
> The coupled pair of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^mod-b2-1-1|Model §B2.1.1]] has two normal modes:
>
> $$
> \omega_1 = \omega_0, \quad \vec v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}; \qquad\qquad \omega_2 = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 + 2\omega_c^2}, \quad \vec v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> In mode 1 the blocks move together, in phase; in mode 2 they move in antiphase, and faster.
>
> *Source: Series I §6.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 3 · PHY 287, week 11 coupled-oscillator slides; Problem Set 9 · PHY 300, Worksheet 3*

^thm-b2-1-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Write $s = \omega_0^2 + \omega_c^2$. By [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]] the normal frequencies solve
>
> $$
> \det\begin{pmatrix} s - \omega^2 & -\omega_c^2 \\ -\omega_c^2 & s - \omega^2 \end{pmatrix} = (s - \omega^2)^2 - \omega_c^4 = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s - \omega^2 = \pm\,\omega_c^2 ,
> $$
>
> so $\omega_1^2 = s - \omega_c^2 = \omega_0^2$ and $\omega_2^2 = s + \omega_c^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\omega_c^2$. For $\omega_1^2$, $\mathsf A - \omega_1^2\mathbb 1$ has every entry $\pm\omega_c^2$ and its first row gives $\omega_c^2(v_1 - v_2) = 0$, so $v_1 = v_2$. For $\omega_2^2$ every entry is $-\omega_c^2$ and $v_1 + v_2 = 0$. Eigenvectors are fixed only up to a factor; $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ are the simplest choices.

^der-b2-1-4

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]

*Top: the coupled pair. Middle and bottom: the two normal modes, with arrows showing the displacements at one instant. In mode 1 the coupling spring is never stretched; in mode 2 its two ends move oppositely, so it adds a restoring force on each block.*

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the modes off the picture
> Both results can be seen before any algebra.
> - In mode 1 the blocks move together, the coupling spring keeps its length and exerts no force, and each block oscillates as if alone, at $\omega_0$, whatever $k_c$ is.
> - In mode 2 the far end of the coupling spring moves opposite to the near end, so the spring stretches by twice the block's displacement: each block feels $-(k + 2k_c)x$, and $\omega_2^2 = (k + 2k_c)/m$.
> - Mirror symmetry decides the shapes: exchanging the two blocks leaves the system unchanged, so a mode must be either unchanged (symmetric) or reversed (antisymmetric) by the exchange. This is why the sum and the difference of the two equations separate ([[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]); Schwartz finds the modes this way first.
>
> For coupled pendulums, mode 1 is the two pendulums swinging together at the single-pendulum frequency $\sqrt{g/\ell}$, mode 2 swinging toward and away from each other, faster.
>
> *Source: Series I §6.3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 3 · PHY 287, week 11 coupled-oscillator slides*

## Why normal modes always exist

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.1.5: Normal Modes of a Spring Network
> Let $\mathsf M\ddot{\vec x} = -\mathsf K\vec x$ with positive masses and with $\mathsf K$ real, symmetric and positive definite ($\vec u^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K\vec u > 0$ for every $\vec u \ne \vec 0$). Then:
> 1. the system has $N$ normal modes, with real mode shapes $\vec v_1, \dots, \vec v_N$ and positive normal frequencies $\omega_1, \dots, \omega_N$ (repeated frequencies counted with multiplicity);
> 2. mode shapes of different frequencies are orthogonal in the **mass-weighted inner product**,
>
> $$
> \langle \vec u, \vec w\rangle_{\mathsf M} = \vec u^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\,\vec w = \sum_i m_i\,u_iw_i , \qquad \langle\vec v_n, \vec v_{n'}\rangle_{\mathsf M} = 0 \quad (\omega_n \neq \omega_{n'}) ;
> $$
>
> 3. the mode shapes can be chosen to form an $\mathsf M$-orthogonal basis of $\mathbb R^N$.
>
> For masses joined to each other and to walls by ideal springs, $\mathsf K$ is symmetric, and it is positive definite as long as every mass is linked to a wall through some chain of springs. With equal masses $\mathsf A = \mathsf K/m$ is itself symmetric, and (2) is ordinary orthogonality, as for $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ above.
>
> *Source: Series I §6.6 (Remark: why this always works; stated there) · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4*

^thm-b2-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The stiffness matrix of a spring network.* A spring of constant $k_{ij}$ between masses $i$ and $j$ adds $k_{ij}$ to $K_{ii}$ and $K_{jj}$ and $-k_{ij}$ to $K_{ij}$ and $K_{ji}$, the same number in both off-diagonal places: this is Newton's third law, as in [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^der-b2-1-2|the derivation of Theorem §B2.1.2]]. A spring from mass $i$ to a wall adds to $K_{ii}$ only. So $\mathsf K$ is symmetric, and the stored energy ([[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-2|CM Theorem §A4.4.2]]) is
>
> $$
> U = \tfrac12\,\vec x^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K\,\vec x = \sum_{\text{wall springs}} \tfrac12 k_i x_i^2 + \sum_{\text{springs } i\text{–}j} \tfrac12 k_{ij}(x_i - x_j)^2 \ \ge 0 .
> $$
>
> $U = 0$ only if every spring is unstretched: $x_i = 0$ for each mass tied to a wall, and $x_i = x_j$ for every connected pair, hence $\vec x = \vec 0$ if every mass is linked to a wall. That is positive definiteness.
>
> *Reduction to a symmetric matrix.* Put $\vec y = \mathsf M^{1/2}\vec x$, with $\mathsf M^{1/2} = \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{m_i})$. Then $\ddot{\vec y} = -\mathsf S\vec y$ with $\mathsf S = \mathsf M^{-1/2}\mathsf K\mathsf M^{-1/2}$, which is symmetric. By the real spectral theorem ([[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]]) $\mathbb R^N$ has an orthonormal basis $\vec u_1, \dots, \vec u_N$ of eigenvectors of $\mathsf S$, with real eigenvalues $\lambda_n$ ([[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-12|LADR Thm. 7.12]]). Each is positive: with $\vec v_n = \mathsf M^{-1/2}\vec u_n$,
>
> $$
> \lambda_n = \vec u_n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf S\,\vec u_n = \vec v_n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K\,\vec v_n > 0 .
> $$
>
> Multiplying $\mathsf S\vec u_n = \lambda_n\vec u_n$ by $\mathsf M^{1/2}$ gives $\mathsf K\vec v_n = \lambda_n\mathsf M\vec v_n$, so by [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]] $\vec v_n$ is a mode shape with $\omega_n = \sqrt{\lambda_n}$. Finally $\vec v_n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\vec v_{n'} = \vec u_n^{\mathsf T}\vec u_{n'} = \delta_{nn'}$, and $\mathsf M^{-1/2}$ maps a basis to a basis: this proves (1) and (3).
>
> *Orthogonality directly.* If $\mathsf K\vec v = \omega^2\mathsf M\vec v$ and $\mathsf K\vec w = \omega'^2\mathsf M\vec w$, then, since $\mathsf K$ and $\mathsf M$ are symmetric, $\omega^2\,\vec w^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\vec v = \vec w^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K\vec v = \vec v^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K\vec w = \omega'^2\,\vec v^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\vec w$, so $(\omega^2 - \omega'^2)\langle\vec v, \vec w\rangle_{\mathsf M} = 0$. This is (2).

^der-b2-1-5

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^def-b2-1-1|Def. §B2.1.1]], [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-3|Theorem §B2.1.3]], [[§A3.5 Newton's Third Law#^pr-a3-5-1|CM Principle §A3.5.1]], [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-2|CM Theorem §A4.4.2]], [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]], [[§22 Self-Adjoint and Normal Operators#^ladr-7-12|LADR Thm. 7.12]]

> [!caution] Caution: Completeness needs the symmetry
> A basis of eigenvectors is not automatic: the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ has only one eigenvector direction. Schwartz states that the eigenvectors of "any matrix" are complete (Lecture 4, §3); for oscillators the statement is true because of the symmetry of $\mathsf K$, which is Newton's third law in matrix form. With unequal masses, $\mathsf A = \mathsf M^{-1}\mathsf K$ is not symmetric (Schwartz writes it out in Lecture 3, §3), and its eigenvectors are not orthogonal in the ordinary sense; they are orthogonal in $\langle\,\cdot\,,\cdot\,\rangle_{\mathsf M}$, and the basis exists because $\mathsf A$ is similar to the symmetric $\mathsf S$.

^cau-b2-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Zero modes
> If some masses are not linked to any wall, $\mathsf K$ is only positive semidefinite and a normal frequency can be zero. Two masses $m$ joined by a spring $k_c$ and nothing else have $\omega^2 = 0$ with $\vec v = (1, 1)$, which is not an oscillation but a uniform drift $\vec x = (a + bt)(1, 1)$, and $\omega^2 = 2k_c/m$ with $\vec v = (1, -1)$: the vibration of a free diatomic molecule, at $\sqrt{k_c/\mu}$ with the reduced mass $\mu = m/2$ ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^ex-a1-2-2|Example §A1.2.2]]).

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.1.1: Coupled Pendulums of Unequal Mass
> Two pendulums of the same length $L$ and masses $m_1$, $m_2$ are joined by a spring of constant $k$. Find the normal modes for small swings.
>
> With $x_1, x_2$ the horizontal bob displacements, gravity gives $-(m_ig/L)\,x_i$ and the spring couples as in [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]]:
>
> $$
> \mathsf M = \begin{pmatrix} m_1 & 0 \\ 0 & m_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathsf K = \begin{pmatrix} m_1g/L + k & -k \\ -k & m_2g/L + k\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> With $\omega_p^2 = g/L$ and $\lambda = \omega^2$, $\det(\mathsf K - \lambda\mathsf M) = \bigl[m_1(\omega_p^2 - \lambda) + k\bigr]\bigl[m_2(\omega_p^2 - \lambda) + k\bigr] - k^2 = m_1m_2(\omega_p^2 - \lambda)^2 + k(m_1 + m_2)(\omega_p^2 - \lambda) = 0$, so
>
> $$
> \omega_1^2 = \frac gL, \quad \vec v_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1\end{pmatrix}; \qquad \omega_2^2 = \frac gL + k\Bigl(\frac1{m_1} + \frac1{m_2}\Bigr) = \frac gL + \frac k\mu, \quad \vec v_2 = \begin{pmatrix} m_2 \\ -m_1\end{pmatrix},
> $$
>
> with $\mu = m_1m_2/(m_1 + m_2)$. (For $\vec v_2$: the first row of $(\mathsf K - \omega_2^2\mathsf M)\vec v = \vec 0$ reads $-k(m_1/m_2)\,v_1 = k\,v_2$.) In mode 1 the pendulums swing together, the spring idle, at the single-pendulum frequency. In mode 2 they swing oppositely with $m_1x_1 + m_2x_2 = 0$: the horizontal position of the centre of mass stays fixed, and the lighter bob swings wider.
>
> The two mode shapes are not orthogonal in the ordinary sense, $(1, 1)\cdot(m_2, -m_1) = m_2 - m_1$, but they are in the mass-weighted product: $\vec v_1^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\vec v_2 = m_1m_2 - m_2m_1 = 0$, as [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]] says. For $m_1 = m_2 = m$ both results reduce to [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]] with $\omega_c^2 = k/m$.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Midterm 1 practice*

^ex-b2-1-1

> [!example] Example §B2.1.2: Two Masses, One Wall
> Two equal masses $m$ hang in a line from one wall: wall, spring $k$, mass 1, spring $k$, mass 2, with mass 2 free on the right. Find the normal modes.
>
> Mass 1 feels both springs, mass 2 only the second: $m\ddot x_1 = -kx_1 + k(x_2 - x_1)$, $m\ddot x_2 = -k(x_2 - x_1)$. So $\mathsf A = \omega_0^2\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ with $\omega_0^2 = k/m$, and with $\lambda = \omega^2/\omega_0^2$,
>
> $$
> (2 - \lambda)(1 - \lambda) - 1 = \lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 1 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega^2 = \frac{3 \mp \sqrt5}{2}\,\frac km, \qquad \omega_{\text{slow}} = 0.618\,\omega_0, \quad \omega_{\text{fast}} = 1.618\,\omega_0 .
> $$
>
> The first row of $(\mathsf A - \omega^2\mathbb 1)\vec v = \vec 0$ gives the amplitude ratio $v_2/v_1 = 2 - \lambda$: $+1.618$ in the slow mode (in phase, the free outer mass swinging wider) and $-0.618$ in the fast mode (antiphase, the outer mass swinging less). Both numbers are the golden ratio $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt5)/2$ or $-1/\varphi$, and the shapes $(1, \varphi)$, $(1, -1/\varphi)$ are orthogonal, as the symmetric $\mathsf A$ requires.
>
> The system has no mirror symmetry, so the modes are not $(1, \pm1)$ and the sum-and-difference trick fails; the determinant is the systematic route Schwartz recommends for such cases.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 10*

^ex-b2-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The eigenvalue problem $\det(\mathsf A - \omega^2\mathbb 1) = 0$ uses [[§34 Determinants#^ladr-9-51|LADR Thm. 9.51]]; existence of a full set of modes is the real spectral theorem [[§23 Spectral Theorem#^ladr-7-29|LADR Thm. 7.29]], applied to $\mathsf M^{-1/2}\mathsf K\mathsf M^{-1/2}$. Equivalently, the kinetic energy $\tfrac12\dot{\vec x}^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\dot{\vec x}$ and the potential energy $\tfrac12\vec x^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K\vec x$ are two quadratic forms diagonalised by one basis ([[§32 Bilinear Forms and Quadratic Forms#^ladr-9-23|LADR Thm. 9.23]]).
> - For one coordinate, $\mathsf K$ is the curvature of the potential energy at a stable equilibrium ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-3|Theorem §A1.2.3]]); the matrix version, small oscillations about equilibrium of any mechanical system, belongs to Lagrangian mechanics at the Classical Mechanics upper level (planned).
> - Symmetric (Hermitian) eigenvalue problems are the language of quantum mechanics (Schwartz, Lecture 3); each normal mode, quantised, is a quantum oscillator ([[§A4.5 The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator#^thm-a4-5-3|QM Theorem §A4.5.3]]).
