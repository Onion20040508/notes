A single driven oscillator responds most strongly near its natural frequency ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]], [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-2|Theorem §A1.6.2]]). What happens when only one block of a coupled pair is pushed? In normal coordinates the problem falls apart into two driven oscillators, one per mode, so everything from [[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator|§B1.3]]–[[§B1.4 Resonance Width, Phase and Power|§B1.4]] applies mode by mode. The result has two resonances, at the normal frequencies, and between them something new: an antiresonance, at which the block being pushed does not move at all. This is the theory behind the tuned mass damper that level A described ([[§A1.4 Pendulums#^ex-a1-4-2|Example §A1.4.2]], [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^rem-a1-6-6|Remark: Resonance in the world]]).

## Driving one block

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.1: Response of a Driven Coupled Pair
> Drive block 1 of the coupled pair ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^mod-b2-1-1|Model §B2.1.1]]) with $F_0\cos\omega t$, and allow linear damping ([[§A1.5 Damped Oscillations#^mod-a1-5-1|Model §A1.5.1]]) of the same rate $\gamma = b/m$ on each block:
>
> $$
> \ddot{\vec x} + \gamma\dot{\vec x} + \mathsf A\vec x = f_0\cos\omega t\;\vec e_1, \qquad f_0 = \frac{F_0}{m}, \quad \vec e_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} .
> $$
>
> 1. The normal coordinates are two driven oscillators, each pushed with half the force:
>
> $$
> \ddot q_n + \gamma\dot q_n + \omega_n^2q_n = \tfrac12f_0\cos\omega t, \qquad n = 1, 2 .
> $$
>
> 2. In the steady state $x_j = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\hat X_j\,e^{i\omega t}\bigr]$ with
>
> $$
> \hat X_1 = \frac{f_0}{2}\bigl[\chi_1(\omega) + \chi_2(\omega)\bigr], \qquad \hat X_2 = \frac{f_0}{2}\bigl[\chi_1(\omega) - \chi_2(\omega)\bigr], \qquad \chi_n(\omega) = \frac{1}{\omega_n^2 - \omega^2 + i\gamma\omega} ,
> $$
>
> where $\chi_n$ is the complex response function of a single oscillator ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^def-b1-3-1|Def. §B1.3.1]]) with natural frequency $\omega_n$.
>
> 3. Without damping the amplitudes are real:
>
> $$
> X_1 = \frac{f_0}{2}\Bigl[\frac{1}{\omega_1^2 - \omega^2} + \frac{1}{\omega_2^2 - \omega^2}\Bigr], \qquad X_2 = \frac{f_0}{2}\Bigl[\frac{1}{\omega_1^2 - \omega^2} - \frac{1}{\omega_2^2 - \omega^2}\Bigr] = \frac{f_0\,\omega_c^2}{(\omega_1^2 - \omega^2)(\omega_2^2 - \omega^2)} .
> $$
>
> Both blocks respond resonantly at $\omega_1$ and at $\omega_2$: **the resonances of a coupled system sit at its normal frequencies**, not at the natural frequencies of its parts. With light damping each peak is a resonance of width $\gamma$ ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-3|Theorem §A1.6.3]]).
>
> *Source: Series I §7.1–7.3 · PHY 287, week 11 coupled-oscillator slides; Problem Set 9*

^thm-b2-3-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take half the sum and half the difference of the two component equations, as in [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]. Only the first equation carries the drive, so both combinations receive $\tfrac12f_0\cos\omega t$; in vector form, $\vec e_1 = \tfrac12\vec v_1 + \tfrac12\vec v_2$, a push on block 1 alone pushes on both modes. The damping term $\gamma\dot{\vec x}$ is $\gamma$ times the identity, which keeps its form in any basis, so the modes stay decoupled.
>
> Each normal coordinate is now a driven damped oscillator. Its steady state, by the complex method ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-1|Theorem §B1.3.1]]), is $q_n = \operatorname{Re}\bigl[\tfrac12f_0\chi_ne^{i\omega t}\bigr]$: substituting $\hat q_ne^{i\omega t}$ turns the equation into $(\omega_n^2 - \omega^2 + i\gamma\omega)\,\hat q_n = \tfrac12f_0$. Then $x_1 = q_1 + q_2$ and $x_2 = q_1 - q_2$. For $\gamma = 0$ the $\chi_n$ are real; combining the fractions in $X_2$, the numerator is $\omega_2^2 - \omega_1^2 = 2\omega_c^2$ ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-4|Theorem §B2.1.4]]).

> [!remark] Remark: What "steady state" means without damping
> With $\gamma = 0$ the free oscillations of the two modes never decay, so strictly there is no unique long-time state: the undamped formulas are the limit $\gamma \to 0^+$ of the damped steady state. With any damping, however small, the transients die out mode by mode, as for a single oscillator ([[§B1.3 Transients and Steady State of the Driven Oscillator#^thm-b1-3-2|Theorem §B1.3.2]]). Damping that differs from block to block does couple the modes; the steady state then follows from the $2 \times 2$ complex linear system $(\mathsf A - \omega^2\mathbb 1 + i\omega\Gamma)\hat{\vec X} = f_0\vec e_1$, with $\Gamma$ the diagonal matrix of damping rates. The sources treat only the equal-damping case.
>
> *Source: Series I §7.4 (Remark: on the undamped idealization)*

## Antiresonance

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.2: Antiresonance
> Without damping, the steady-state amplitude of the driven block,
>
> $$
> X_1(\omega) = \frac{f_0}{2}\,\frac{\omega_1^2 + \omega_2^2 - 2\omega^2}{(\omega_1^2 - \omega^2)(\omega_2^2 - \omega^2)},
> $$
>
> vanishes at the **antiresonance** frequency
>
> $$
> \omega_a = \sqrt{\frac{\omega_1^2 + \omega_2^2}{2}} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 + \omega_c^2}, \qquad \omega_1 < \omega_a < \omega_2 .
> $$
>
> There the pushed block stands still while the other block moves with $X_2(\omega_a) = -f_0/\omega_c^2 = -F_0/k_c$, so that the coupling spring pulls on block 1 with $-F_0\cos\omega t$, cancelling the drive at every instant. $\omega_a$ is the natural frequency of block 2 with block 1 clamped. With light damping ($\gamma\omega_a \ll \omega_c^2$) the zero becomes a sharp minimum of depth
>
> $$
> \bigl|\hat X_1(\omega_a)\bigr| \approx \frac{f_0\,\gamma\,\omega_a}{\omega_c^4} ,
> $$
>
> proportional to the damping.
>
> *Source: Series I §7.4*

^thm-b2-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put the two fractions of $X_1$ over a common denominator: the numerator is $(\omega_2^2 - \omega^2) + (\omega_1^2 - \omega^2)$. It vanishes at $\omega^2 = \tfrac12(\omega_1^2 + \omega_2^2) = \tfrac12\bigl(\omega_0^2 + \omega_0^2 + 2\omega_c^2\bigr) = \omega_0^2 + \omega_c^2$, which lies between $\omega_1^2 = \omega_0^2$ and $\omega_2^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\omega_c^2$, where the denominator is nonzero. At $\omega_a$, $\omega_1^2 - \omega_a^2 = -\omega_c^2$ and $\omega_2^2 - \omega_a^2 = +\omega_c^2$, so
>
> $$
> X_2(\omega_a) = \frac{f_0}{2}\Bigl[\frac{1}{-\omega_c^2} - \frac{1}{\omega_c^2}\Bigr] = -\frac{f_0}{\omega_c^2} .
> $$
>
> *Force balance.* With $x_1 \equiv 0$ and $x_2 = -(f_0/\omega_c^2)\cos\omega t$, the coupling spring exerts on block 1 the force $k_c(x_2 - x_1) = m\omega_c^2x_2 = -mf_0\cos\omega t = -F_0\cos\omega t$; the wall spring exerts nothing, since $x_1 = 0$. So block 1 feels no net force and stays at rest, consistently. Block 2, meanwhile, sits between two springs whose other ends are fixed (the wall, and the motionless block 1): it is a free oscillator of constant $k + k_c$ and frequency $\sqrt{(k + k_c)/m} = \omega_a$, which is why it can oscillate with no net drive of its own.
>
> *Light damping.* At $\omega_a$, $\chi_{1,2} = 1/(\mp\omega_c^2 + i\gamma\omega_a)$, so
>
> $$
> \hat X_1(\omega_a) = \frac{f_0}{2}\,\frac{2i\gamma\omega_a}{(i\gamma\omega_a)^2 - \omega_c^4} = -\,\frac{i f_0\gamma\omega_a}{\omega_c^4 + \gamma^2\omega_a^2} \approx -\,\frac{if_0\gamma\omega_a}{\omega_c^4} .
> $$

^der-b2-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: Phases across the spectrum
> Without damping the amplitudes are real, and their signs say whether a block moves with the force ($+$) or against it ($-$):
>
> | range of $\omega$ | below $\omega_1$ | $\omega_1$ to $\omega_a$ | $\omega_a$ to $\omega_2$ | above $\omega_2$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | driven block, $X_1$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ |
> | undriven block, $X_2$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ |
>
> The ratio $X_2/X_1 = \omega_c^2/(\omega_a^2 - \omega^2)$ shows that the blocks move together below $\omega_a$ and oppositely above it. Each resonance flips the phase of both blocks by $\pi$, as for one oscillator ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]]); the antiresonance flips only the driven block, whose amplitude passes through zero there. PHY 287's slides note the in-phase response below the first resonance and the out-of-phase response above it; the table completes the picture.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 11 coupled-oscillator slides*

^rem-b2-3-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.3.3: Dynamic Vibration Absorber
> A main mass $M$ on a spring $K$ is driven by $F_0\cos\omega t$. Attach to it an absorber, a mass $m_a$ on a spring $k_a$ connected to the main mass only. Without damping, the steady-state amplitudes are
>
> $$
> X_M = \frac{F_0\,(k_a - m_a\omega^2)}{\Delta(\omega)}, \qquad X_a = \frac{F_0\,k_a}{\Delta(\omega)}, \qquad \Delta(\omega) = (K + k_a - M\omega^2)(k_a - m_a\omega^2) - k_a^2 .
> $$
>
> 1. The main mass stands still when the drive is at the absorber's own frequency, $\omega = \sqrt{k_a/m_a}$, whatever $M$ and $K$ are. The absorber then moves with amplitude $X_a = -F_0/k_a$, and its spring cancels the drive.
> 2. The combined system has two resonances, one below and one above $\sqrt{k_a/m_a}$.
>
> This is the **tuned mass damper**: to protect a structure from vibration at a known frequency, attach a small oscillator tuned to that frequency.
>
> *Source: Series I §7.4 (the principle, for the symmetric pair); derived here for a general main system*

^thm-b2-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $x$ and $x_a$ the displacements of main mass and absorber, Newton's second law gives $M\ddot x = -Kx - k_a(x - x_a) + F_0\cos\omega t$ and $m_a\ddot x_a = -k_a(x_a - x)$. Insert $x = X_M\cos\omega t$, $x_a = X_a\cos\omega t$:
>
> $$
> (K + k_a - M\omega^2)\,X_M - k_a\,X_a = F_0, \qquad -k_a\,X_M + (k_a - m_a\omega^2)\,X_a = 0 ,
> $$
>
> a $2 \times 2$ linear system with determinant $\Delta(\omega)$, solved by Cramer's rule. At $\omega^2 = k_a/m_a$, $\Delta = -k_a^2 \neq 0$, so $X_M = 0$ and $X_a = -F_0/k_a$; the absorber spring then pulls on the main mass with $k_a(x_a - x) = -F_0\cos\omega t$.
>
> Resonances are the zeros of $\Delta$. As a function of $\omega^2$, $\Delta$ is continuous, equals $Kk_a > 0$ at $\omega = 0$, equals $-k_a^2 < 0$ at $\omega^2 = k_a/m_a$, and grows like $Mm_a\omega^4 > 0$ for large $\omega$. By the intermediate value theorem ([[§18 Properties of Continuous Functions#^thm-18-3|451 Thm. §18.3]]) it has a zero on each side of $k_a/m_a$, and being quadratic in $\omega^2$ it has no others.
>
> The symmetric pair is the case $M = m_a = m$, $k_a = k_c$, except that block 2 also has its own wall spring $k$; its "absorber frequency" is therefore $\sqrt{(k + k_c)/m} = \omega_a$, as in [[§B2.3 Driven Coupled Oscillators and Antiresonance#^thm-b2-3-2|Theorem §B2.3.2]].

> [!remark] Remark: Tuned mass dampers in practice
> The skyscraper pendulum of [[§A1.4 Pendulums#^ex-a1-4-2|Example §A1.4.2]] is this absorber at scale: a pendulum tuned to the building's sway frequency, whose swing pulls back on the building. The undamped absorber protects exactly one frequency, and it does so by moving the system's resonances to either side of it (Theorem §B2.3.3, part 2), so a drive that wanders off the tuned frequency can do worse than without the absorber. Real tuned dampers therefore include damping in the absorber, as [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^rem-a1-6-6|Remark: Resonance in the world]] describes ("takes up and dissipates the energy of the sway"), trading the perfect zero for a lower response over a band. How to choose that damping is not treated in the sources.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.3.1: What the Undriven Mass Does Between the Peaks
> In PHY 300's coupled-oscillator lab two masses hang in a line on three equal springs, one mass is driven, and the amplitude of the *other* (bottom) mass is measured from below $f_1$ to above $f_2$, including the midpoint $(f_1 + f_2)/2$. Gravity only shifts the equilibrium positions ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-4|Theorem §A1.1.4]]), so the model is the coupled pair with $k_c = k$: $\omega_2 = \sqrt3\,\omega_1$, $f_2 = 1.732\,f_1$. Assume the drive acts as a force on the top mass (a support moved with amplitude $d$ through the top spring gives $F_0 = kd$).
>
> By [[§B2.3 Driven Coupled Oscillators and Antiresonance#^thm-b2-3-1|Theorem §B2.3.1]] the bottom mass responds with $|X_2| = f_0\,\omega_c^2/\bigl|(\omega_1^2 - \omega^2)(\omega_2^2 - \omega^2)\bigr|$. This never vanishes: the undriven mass has no antiresonance. Between the peaks it is smallest where $(\omega^2 - \omega_1^2)(\omega_2^2 - \omega^2)$ is largest, at the midpoint of $\omega_1^2$ and $\omega_2^2$, that is, at $\omega_a$:
>
> $$
> f_{\min} = \sqrt{\frac{f_1^2 + f_2^2}{2}} = \sqrt2\,f_1 = 1.414\,f_1, \qquad |X_2|_{\min} = \frac{f_0\,\omega_c^2}{\omega_c^2\cdot\omega_c^2} = \frac{F_0}{k_c} = d .
> $$
>
> The minimum lies 3.5% above the midpoint $(f_1 + f_2)/2 = 1.366\,f_1$, close enough that the suggested midpoint measurement samples it; at the minimum the bottom mass moves just as far as the top support. Measuring the top mass instead would show the sharp antiresonance dip of the figure at the same frequency.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 3*

^ex-b2-3-1

> [!example] Example §B2.3.2: Sizing an Absorber
> A machine of mass $M = 200\ \mathrm{kg}$ on its mounts runs at $25\ \mathrm{Hz}$, which happens to be its own resonance frequency, and is shaken by an unbalanced force of amplitude $F_0 = 500\ \mathrm N$. Design an absorber of mass $m_a = 10\ \mathrm{kg}$.
>
> By [[§B2.3 Driven Coupled Oscillators and Antiresonance#^thm-b2-3-3|Theorem §B2.3.3]] it must be tuned to the running frequency, $\omega = 2\pi(25\ \mathrm{Hz}) = 157\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$:
>
> $$
> k_a = m_a\omega^2 = (10\ \mathrm{kg})(157\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})^2 = 2.47 \times 10^5\ \mathrm{N/m}, \qquad |X_a| = \frac{F_0}{k_a} = 2.0\ \mathrm{mm} .
> $$
>
> The machine then stands still; the absorber swings by $2\ \mathrm{mm}$, and its spring carries the full $500\ \mathrm N$.
>
> The price: with $K = M\omega_0^2$ and $k_a = m_a\omega_0^2$ ($\omega_0 = 157\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$) and mass ratio $\epsilon = m_a/M = 0.05$, the condition $\Delta(\omega) = 0$ becomes, with $r = \omega^2/\omega_0^2$, $(1 + \epsilon - r)(1 - r) = \epsilon$, so
>
> $$
> r = 1 + \frac\epsilon2 \pm \sqrt{\epsilon + \frac{\epsilon^2}{4}} = 1.25 \ \text{or}\ 0.80, \qquad f = 27.95\ \mathrm{Hz} \ \text{or}\ 22.36\ \mathrm{Hz} .
> $$
>
> The single resonance at $25\ \mathrm{Hz}$ has split into two, about $\pm 11\%$ away. Starting and stopping the machine passes through $22.4\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and a running speed that drifts toward either new resonance is dangerous; a heavier absorber pushes them further apart.
>
> *Source: Series I §7.4 (absorber principle); numbers chosen to illustrate Theorem §B2.3.3*
