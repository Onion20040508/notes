[[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators|§B2.1]] found the special motions, the normal modes. This section builds every other motion from them. In normal coordinates the coupled system falls apart into independent simple harmonic oscillators, each solved by [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]], and the initial conditions fix how much of each mode is present by a projection. When two modes have nearly equal frequencies, their superposition beats ([[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]]): energy swings back and forth between the two oscillators while the energy of each mode stays fixed.

## Normal coordinates

> [!definition] Definition §B2.2.1: Normal Coordinates
> The **normal coordinates** of a configuration $\vec x$ are its components in a basis of mode shapes, $\vec x = \sum_n q_n\vec v_n$; with the modes $\mathsf M$-orthogonal ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]]),
>
> $$
> q_n = \frac{\langle\vec v_n, \vec x\rangle_{\mathsf M}}{\langle\vec v_n, \vec v_n\rangle_{\mathsf M}} = \frac{\vec v_n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\,\vec x}{\vec v_n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\,\vec v_n} .
> $$
>
> For the coupled pair of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^mod-b2-1-1|Model §B2.1.1]], with $\vec v_1 = (1, 1)$ and $\vec v_2 = (1, -1)$,
>
> $$
> q_1 = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \qquad q_2 = \frac{x_1 - x_2}{2} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad x_1 = q_1 + q_2, \quad x_2 = q_1 - q_2 .
> $$
>
> PHY 287 and PHY 300 use $x_1 \pm x_2$ without the factor $\tfrac12$; only the normalisation differs.
>
> *Source: Series I §6.4 · PHY 287, week 11 coupled-oscillator slides · PHY 300, Worksheet 3*

^def-b2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.1: Normal Coordinates Decouple
> Each normal coordinate obeys its own oscillator equation,
>
> $$
> \ddot q_n + \omega_n^2\,q_n = 0, \qquad n = 1, \dots, N .
> $$
>
> For the coupled pair, $\ddot q_1 + \omega_0^2\,q_1 = 0$ and $\ddot q_2 + (\omega_0^2 + 2\omega_c^2)\,q_2 = 0$. In matrix language, if $\mathsf S$ is the matrix whose columns are the mode shapes, then $\mathsf S^{-1}\mathsf A\,\mathsf S = \operatorname{diag}(\omega_1^2, \dots, \omega_N^2)$: the change to normal coordinates diagonalises the dynamical matrix.
>
> *Source: Series I §6.4 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 3 · PHY 287, week 11 coupled-oscillator slides · PHY 300, Worksheet 3*

^thm-b2-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *The pair.* Add and subtract the equations of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-2|Theorem §B2.1.2]], divided by $m$. In the sum the coupling terms $\omega_c^2(x_2 - x_1)$ and $-\omega_c^2(x_2 - x_1)$ cancel; in the difference they add:
>
> $$
> \ddot x_1 + \ddot x_2 = -\omega_0^2\,(x_1 + x_2), \qquad \ddot x_1 - \ddot x_2 = -(\omega_0^2 + 2\omega_c^2)\,(x_1 - x_2) .
> $$
>
> Dividing by 2 gives the equations for $q_1$ and $q_2$.
>
> *In general.* The columns of $\mathsf S$ form a basis ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]]), so $\mathsf S$ is invertible, and $\mathsf A\vec v_n = \omega_n^2\vec v_n$ column by column says $\mathsf A\mathsf S = \mathsf S\mathsf D$ with $\mathsf D = \operatorname{diag}(\omega_n^2)$. Substituting $\vec x = \mathsf S\vec q$ into $\ddot{\vec x} = -\mathsf A\vec x$ gives $\mathsf S\ddot{\vec q} = -\mathsf A\mathsf S\vec q = -\mathsf S\mathsf D\vec q$; multiplying by $\mathsf S^{-1}$ gives $\ddot{\vec q} = -\mathsf D\vec q$, one equation per row. A matrix with a basis of eigenvectors is diagonalisable exactly in this way ([[§17 Diagonalizable Operators#^ladr-5-55|LADR Thm. 5.55]]).

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.2: Energy Is a Sum of Mode Energies
> The total energy $E = \tfrac12\dot{\vec x}^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\dot{\vec x} + \tfrac12\vec x^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K\vec x$ separates into one term per mode,
>
> $$
> E = \sum_n \tfrac12\,\mu_n\bigl(\dot q_n^2 + \omega_n^2q_n^2\bigr), \qquad \mu_n = \vec v_n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\,\vec v_n ,
> $$
>
> and each term is separately constant in time. For the coupled pair ($\mu_1 = \mu_2 = 2m$),
>
> $$
> E = m\bigl(\dot q_1^2 + \omega_1^2q_1^2\bigr) + m\bigl(\dot q_2^2 + \omega_2^2q_2^2\bigr) .
> $$
>
> *Source: derived here from Series I §6.4; not stated in the sources*

^thm-b2-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Substitute $\vec x = \sum_n q_n\vec v_n$. The kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\sum_{n,n'}\dot q_n\dot q_{n'}\,\vec v_n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\vec v_{n'}$, and the cross terms vanish by $\mathsf M$-orthogonality ([[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]]). In the potential energy, $\vec v_n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf K\vec v_{n'} = \omega_{n'}^2\,\vec v_n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\vec v_{n'}$, which is $\omega_n^2\mu_n$ for $n = n'$ and $0$ otherwise. Each $q_n$ is a simple harmonic oscillator ([[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]]), whose energy $\tfrac12\mu_n(\dot q_n^2 + \omega_n^2q_n^2)$ is constant ([[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-1|Theorem §A1.2.1]]).
>
> Check for the pair directly: $\tfrac12m(\dot x_1^2 + \dot x_2^2) = \tfrac12m\bigl[(\dot q_1 + \dot q_2)^2 + (\dot q_1 - \dot q_2)^2\bigr] = m(\dot q_1^2 + \dot q_2^2)$, and $\tfrac12k(x_1^2 + x_2^2) + \tfrac12k_c(x_1 - x_2)^2 = kq_1^2 + kq_2^2 + 2k_cq_2^2 = m\omega_1^2q_1^2 + m\omega_2^2q_2^2$.

^der-b2-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]], [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]], [[§A1.2 Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-2-1|Theorem §A1.2.1]], [[§A4.4 Potential Energy#^thm-a4-4-2|CM Theorem §A4.4.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Independent means no energy exchange
> "The modes are independent" has a precise meaning: no energy ever flows from one mode to another. Energy can and does flow between the *blocks*, through the coupling spring, but that is a statement about a different basis. What one sees as exchange between oscillators is a fixed mixture of modes drifting in and out of phase.

^rem-b2-2-1

## The general solution

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.3: General Solution and Initial Conditions
> Every motion of $\ddot{\vec x} = -\mathsf A\vec x$ (with the hypotheses of [[§B2.1 Two Coupled Oscillators#^thm-b2-1-5|Theorem §B2.1.5]]) is a superposition of the normal modes,
>
> $$
> \vec x(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\vec v_n\bigl(a_n\cos\omega_nt + b_n\sin\omega_nt\bigr),
> $$
>
> and the $2N$ constants are fixed uniquely by the $2N$ initial data, by projecting onto the modes:
>
> $$
> a_n = q_n(0) = \frac{\vec v_n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\,\vec x(0)}{\mu_n}, \qquad b_n = \frac{\dot q_n(0)}{\omega_n} = \frac{\vec v_n^{\mathsf T}\mathsf M\,\dot{\vec x}(0)}{\mu_n\,\omega_n} .
> $$
>
> For the coupled pair, in amplitude–phase form,
>
> $$
> x_1(t) = A_1\cos(\omega_1t - \phi_1) + A_2\cos(\omega_2t - \phi_2), \qquad x_2(t) = A_1\cos(\omega_1t - \phi_1) - A_2\cos(\omega_2t - \phi_2),
> $$
>
> with four constants for the four data $x_1(0), x_2(0), \dot x_1(0), \dot x_2(0)$. A single mode is excited exactly when the initial displacement and the initial velocity are both multiples of its mode shape. The motion is periodic only if the frequency ratios of the excited modes are rational.
>
> *Source: Series I §6.5 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 3 and Lecture 4, §3 · PHY 287, week 11 coupled-oscillator slides · PHY 300, Worksheet 3*

^thm-b2-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-1|Theorem §B2.2.1]] each $q_n$ solves the oscillator equation, so by [[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^thm-a1-1-2|Theorem §A1.1.2]] $q_n = a_n\cos\omega_nt + b_n\sin\omega_nt$, the only solutions; $\vec x = \sum q_n\vec v_n$ gives the superposition. At $t = 0$, $q_n(0) = a_n$ and $\dot q_n(0) = \omega_nb_n$, and $q_n(0)$, $\dot q_n(0)$ are the normal coordinates of $\vec x(0)$ and $\dot{\vec x}(0)$ (Def. §B2.2.1), which are unique because the $\vec v_n$ form a basis. This is the expansion of a vector in an orthogonal basis ([[§21 Orthonormal Bases#^ladr-6-30|LADR Thm. 6.30]]), with $\langle\,\cdot\,,\cdot\,\rangle_{\mathsf M}$ as inner product. For the pair, $q_1 = A_1\cos(\omega_1t - \phi_1)$ and $q_2 = A_2\cos(\omega_2t - \phi_2)$, and $x_{1,2} = q_1 \pm q_2$.
>
> Only mode $n$ is present when $a_{n'} = b_{n'} = 0$ for all $n' \ne n$, that is, when $\vec x(0)$ and $\dot{\vec x}(0)$ have no component along the other modes. If modes $n$ and $n'$ are both present and $\vec x$ has period $T$, then each of their coordinates has period $T$, so $\omega_nT$ and $\omega_{n'}T$ are multiples of $2\pi$ and $\omega_n/\omega_{n'}$ is rational.

> [!remark] Remark: Normal modes and initial conditions
> Schwartz's way to excite one mode in the lab: pull both masses the same way by the same amount and let go (mode 1), or pull them apart symmetrically (mode 2). Any other start excites both. In this sense, as Schwartz puts it, the normal modes are in one-to-one correspondence with special initial conditions, and the general initial condition is a sum of them, the decomposition being the projection above. The same projection, with infinitely many modes and an integral as inner product, gives the Fourier coefficients of a plucked string ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-2|Theorem §B4.1.2]], [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 4, §1*

## Energy exchange

> [!theorem] Theorem §B2.2.4: Energy Exchange Between Weakly Coupled Oscillators
> Displace block 1 of the coupled pair by $a$ and release both blocks from rest. Then, with $\bar\omega = \tfrac12(\omega_1 + \omega_2)$ and $\Delta = \omega_2 - \omega_1$,
>
> $$
> x_1(t) = a\cos\Bigl(\frac{\Delta}{2}t\Bigr)\cos\bar\omega t, \qquad x_2(t) = a\sin\Bigl(\frac{\Delta}{2}t\Bigr)\sin\bar\omega t .
> $$
>
> For weak coupling, $\omega_c \ll \omega_0$, $\Delta \approx \omega_c^2/\omega_0 \ll \bar\omega \approx \omega_0$: both blocks oscillate at about $\omega_0$ inside slow, complementary envelopes. Up to corrections of relative order $\omega_c^2/\omega_0^2$, the energies of the blocks (each counted with its own wall spring) are
>
> $$
> E_1 \approx E\cos^2\Bigl(\frac{\Delta}{2}t\Bigr), \qquad E_2 \approx E\sin^2\Bigl(\frac{\Delta}{2}t\Bigr):
> $$
>
> the energy passes completely to block 2 in the time $\pi/\Delta$ and back again, with exchange period
>
> $$
> T_{\text{ex}} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_2 - \omega_1} \approx \frac{2\pi\,\omega_0}{\omega_c^2} .
> $$
>
> The product form of $x_1$ is the beat identity of [[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]] with the two normal modes as the two tones; new here are the complementary envelope of the second block and the transfer of energy between the blocks, with its exchange period.
>
> *Source: Series I §6.6 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 3, §2 · PHY 287, week 11 coupled-oscillator slides · PHY 300, Worksheet 3*

^thm-b2-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In normal coordinates the initial data are $q_1(0) = q_2(0) = a/2$ and $\dot q_1(0) = \dot q_2(0) = 0$, so by [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-3|Theorem §B2.2.3]] $q_n = \tfrac a2\cos\omega_nt$ and
>
> $$
> x_1 = \tfrac a2\bigl(\cos\omega_1t + \cos\omega_2t\bigr), \qquad x_2 = \tfrac a2\bigl(\cos\omega_1t - \cos\omega_2t\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The sum is the beat identity of [[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]], $\cos u + \cos w = 2\cos\frac{u + w}{2}\cos\frac{u - w}{2}$; the difference follows the same way from the addition formulas, $\cos u - \cos w = -2\sin\frac{u + w}{2}\sin\frac{u - w}{2}$. With $u = \omega_1t$, $w = \omega_2t$ these give the two products.
>
> For weak coupling, $\omega_2 = \omega_0\sqrt{1 + 2\omega_c^2/\omega_0^2} = \omega_0 + \omega_c^2/\omega_0 + O(\omega_c^4/\omega_0^3)$, so $\Delta \approx \omega_c^2/\omega_0$ and $\bar\omega = \omega_0 + O(\omega_c^2/\omega_0)$. Treat $C(t) = \cos(\Delta t/2)$ as constant over one fast period (it changes by a fraction of order $\Delta/\bar\omega$). Then $\dot x_1 \approx -a\bar\omega C\sin\bar\omega t$ and
>
> $$
> E_1 = \tfrac12m\dot x_1^2 + \tfrac12kx_1^2 \approx \tfrac12ma^2C^2\bigl(\bar\omega^2\sin^2\bar\omega t + \omega_0^2\cos^2\bar\omega t\bigr) \approx \tfrac12m\omega_0^2a^2\,C^2 .
> $$
>
> The total energy is $E = \tfrac12(k + k_c)a^2 = \tfrac12m\omega_0^2a^2\bigl(1 + \omega_c^2/\omega_0^2\bigr)$, so $E_1 \approx E\cos^2(\Delta t/2)$; likewise $E_2 \approx E\sin^2(\Delta t/2)$. The coupling spring itself holds only a fraction of order $\omega_c^2/\omega_0^2$ of the energy. $\cos^2(\Delta t/2)$ has period $2\pi/\Delta$.

> [!remark] Remark: Beats, seen from the modes
> The beat period of each block, the time between maxima of its envelope, is $2\pi/\Delta = T_{\text{ex}}$; the two envelopes are offset by half a beat period, $\pi/\Delta$, so one block is quiet when the other swings widest. Complete transfer happens because the two modes were excited with equal amplitudes: the two cosines then cancel exactly in $x_1$ at $t = \pi/\Delta$. Two oscillators that are not identical, or a start that excites the modes unequally, give only partial transfer, and a pure mode gives none. Beats also produce a time scale far longer than either period; Schwartz stresses that this is how a slow throb arises from two fast oscillations. Coupled pendulums show the effect best: one pendulum, set swinging, gradually stops while the other takes over, and then the roles reverse.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 3, §2 · PHY 287, week 11 coupled-oscillator slides*

^rem-b2-2-3

> [!caution] Caution: Envelope period and exchange period
> The envelope factor $\cos(\Delta t/2)$ has period $4\pi/\Delta$, but block 1's energy, $\propto\cos^2(\Delta t/2)$, repeats after $2\pi/\Delta$: the exchange period is $2\pi/(\omega_2 - \omega_1)$, the beat period, not twice it. This is the same factor of 2 as in [[§A3.4 Beats#^cau-a3-4-1|Caution: Not half the difference]].

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B2.2.1: Strong and Weak Coupling
> Block 1 of a coupled pair is displaced by $A_0$ and released; block 2 starts at rest at equilibrium.
>
> **(a) Three equal springs**, $k_c = k$ (PHY 300's "two masses, three springs"). Then $\omega_1 = \omega_0$ and $\omega_2 = \sqrt3\,\omega_0$, and by [[§B2.2 Normal-Mode Solutions and Energy Exchange#^thm-b2-2-4|Theorem §B2.2.4]]
>
> $$
> x_1 = \tfrac{A_0}{2}\bigl(\cos\omega_0t + \cos\sqrt3\,\omega_0t\bigr) = A_0\cos(0.366\,\omega_0t)\cos(1.366\,\omega_0t), \qquad x_2 = \tfrac{A_0}{2}\bigl(\cos\omega_0t - \cos\sqrt3\,\omega_0t\bigr) .
> $$
>
> The product form is still exact, but the "envelope" frequency $0.366\,\omega_0$ is not small compared with the carrier $1.366\,\omega_0$: the motion looks irregular rather than beating, and since $\sqrt3$ is irrational it never exactly repeats.
>
> **(b) Weak coupling.** Schwartz's numbers: $m = 1$, $k = 4$, $k_c = 0.5$ (consistent units, times in seconds). Then $\omega_1 = 2\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\omega_2 = \sqrt5 = 2.236\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so $\Delta = 0.236\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and
>
> $$
> T_{\text{ex}} = \frac{2\pi}{0.236\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}} = 26.6\ \mathrm s, \qquad \text{fast period } \frac{2\pi}{\bar\omega} = \frac{2\pi}{2.118\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}} = 2.97\ \mathrm s .
> $$
>
> Block 1 comes to rest after $13.3\ \mathrm s$, about four and a half fast oscillations, with block 2 then swinging at amplitude $A_0$. The weak-coupling estimate $\Delta \approx \omega_c^2/\omega_0 = 0.5/2 = 0.25\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ is 6% high, since here $\omega_c^2/\omega_0^2 = 1/8$ is not very small.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 3 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 3, §2*
