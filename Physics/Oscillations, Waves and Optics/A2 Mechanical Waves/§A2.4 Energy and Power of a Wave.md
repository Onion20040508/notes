A wave moves no matter from place to place, but it does move energy: earthquakes level buildings far from the epicentre, and a buoy can draw electric power from ocean swell. This section computes the energy a sinusoidal wave on a string carries and the rate at which it carries it, then turns to waves spreading out in space, where the energy flow per unit area, the intensity, falls off with distance.

## Energy carried by a string wave

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.1: Energy Density on a String
> On an ideal string ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^mod-a2-3-1|Model §A2.3.1]]) carrying a transverse wave $y(x, t)$, the kinetic and the potential (stretching) energy per unit length are
>
> $$
> \frac{dK}{dx} = \frac12\,\mu\Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\Bigr)^2, \qquad \frac{dU}{dx} = \frac12\,F_T\Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Bigr)^2 .
> $$
>
> For a travelling wave $y = f(x \mp vt)$ the two are equal at every point and every instant, so the energy per unit length is $\mu\,(\partial y/\partial t)^2$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10*

^thm-a2-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Kinetic.* The element of length $dx$ has mass $\mu\,dx$ and moves with transverse speed $\partial y/\partial t$, so $dK = \tfrac12(\mu\,dx)(\partial y/\partial t)^2$ ([[§A4.2 Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem#^def-a4-2-1|CM Def. §A4.2.1]]).
>
> *Potential.* Displaced, the element has length $\sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2} = dx\sqrt{1 + (\partial y/\partial x)^2} \approx dx\bigl[1 + \tfrac12(\partial y/\partial x)^2\bigr]$ for a small slope. It has been stretched by $\tfrac12(\partial y/\partial x)^2\,dx$ against the constant tension $F_T$, which takes work $\tfrac12F_T(\partial y/\partial x)^2\,dx$; that work is stored as potential energy.
>
> *Equality.* For $y = f(x \mp vt)$, [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-4|Theorem §A2.2.4]] gives $(\partial y/\partial x)^2 = v^{-2}(\partial y/\partial t)^2$, and $F_T/v^2 = \mu$ ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]). So $\tfrac12F_T(\partial y/\partial x)^2 = \tfrac12\mu(\partial y/\partial t)^2$.

^der-a2-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.2: Energy in One Wavelength
> A sinusoidal wave $y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$ on a string carries, per wavelength,
>
> $$
> K_\lambda = U_\lambda = \frac14\,\mu A^2\omega^2\lambda, \qquad E_\lambda = K_\lambda + U_\lambda = \frac12\,\mu A^2\omega^2\lambda .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.4*

^thm-a2-4-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> At $t = 0$, $\partial y/\partial t = -A\omega\cos(kx)$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]), so by [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]]
>
> $$
> K_\lambda = \int_0^\lambda \frac12\,\mu A^2\omega^2\cos^2(kx)\,dx = \frac12\,\mu A^2\omega^2 \Bigl[\frac x2 + \frac{\sin(2kx)}{4k}\Bigr]_0^\lambda = \frac14\,\mu A^2\omega^2\lambda ,
> $$
>
> since $\sin(2k\lambda) = \sin 4\pi = 0$. Any other instant shifts the interval of integration and gives the same result. $U_\lambda = K_\lambda$ because the densities are equal point by point.

^der-a2-4-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-1|Theorem §A2.4.1]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: University Physics' route to the potential energy
> University Physics treats each element as a simple harmonic oscillator with spring constant $k_s = \Delta m\,\omega^2$ and assigns it $U = \tfrac12k_s y^2$. Integrated over a wavelength this gives the correct $U_\lambda = \tfrac14\mu A^2\omega^2\lambda$, but it puts the potential energy in the wrong places: at the crests, where $y$ is largest. The potential energy of a string is stretching energy; it depends on the slope, not on the displacement, and is largest where the string crosses equilibrium. In a travelling wave each element gains and loses kinetic and potential energy *together* (figure above), unlike a mass on a spring, which trades one for the other; the energy is not stored in place but passed along.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10*

^rem-a2-4-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.3: Average Power of a Sinusoidal Wave
> The time-averaged power carried past any point by a sinusoidal wave on a string is
>
> $$
> P_{\text{ave}} = \frac12\,\mu A^2\omega^2 v = \frac12\sqrt{\mu F_T}\;\omega^2A^2 .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.4, eq. (16.10) · PHY 287, week 13 slides (waves at the boundary)*

^thm-a2-4-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Energy per period.* In one period one wavelength of the wave passes a given point, carrying $E_\lambda$ ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]]). So $P_{\text{ave}} = E_\lambda/T = \tfrac12\mu A^2\omega^2\,(\lambda/T) = \tfrac12\mu A^2\omega^2 v$.
>
> *Force times velocity.* The string to the left of a point $x$ pulls on the string to the right with transverse force $-F_T\,\partial y/\partial x$ (as in [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^der-a2-3-2|the derivation of Theorem §A2.3.2]]), and that point moves with velocity $\partial y/\partial t$. The instantaneous power transmitted is ([[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]])
>
> $$
> P(x, t) = -F_T\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = \frac{F_T}{v}\Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\Bigr)^2 = \mu v A^2\omega^2\cos^2(kx - \omega t) \ \ge 0
> $$
>
> for a wave moving in the $+x$ direction, using $\partial y/\partial x = -v^{-1}\,\partial y/\partial t$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-4|Theorem §A2.2.4]]) and $F_T/v = \mu v$. Energy always flows forward; the average of $\cos^2$ over a period is $\tfrac12$. Finally $\mu v = \mu\sqrt{F_T/\mu} = \sqrt{\mu F_T}$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10*

^der-a2-4-3

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-2|Theorem §A2.4.2]], [[§A4.3 Power#^def-a4-3-1|CM Def. §A4.3.1]], [[§A4.3 Power#^thm-a4-3-1|CM Theorem §A4.3.1]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-4|Theorem §A2.2.4]], [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]], [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|Theorem §A2.1.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: How the power scales
> - $P_{\text{ave}} \propto A^2$ and $\propto \omega^2$ (so $\propto f^2$): doubling either the amplitude or the frequency quadruples the power.
> - At fixed amplitude and frequency, $P_{\text{ave}} = \tfrac12\sqrt{\mu F_T}\,\omega^2A^2$: doubling the tension multiplies the power by $\sqrt2$, and the power grows as $\sqrt\mu$, not as $\mu$, because a heavier string also carries the wave more slowly.
> - The $\omega^2$ is special to mechanical waves, where the energy is that of moving matter. The intensity of an electromagnetic wave depends on the field amplitude only, not on the frequency ([[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-3|EM Theorem §A12.3.3]]).

^cau-a2-4-1

## Intensity

> [!definition] Definition §A2.4.1: Intensity
> The **intensity** of a wave is the average power it carries per unit area through a surface perpendicular to its direction of propagation,
>
> $$
> I = \frac{P}{A} \qquad (\mathrm{W/m^2}) .
> $$
>
> The definition applies to any energy in transit; for electromagnetic waves see [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^def-a12-3-2|EM Def. §A12.3.2]]. For sound, intensity is usually quoted on the logarithmic decibel scale ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.4, eq. (16.11)*

^def-a2-4-1

> [!model] Model §A2.4.4: Point Source
> A **point source** emits waves of total average power $P$ from a single point, equally in all directions, into a medium that absorbs none of the energy. Its wave fronts are spheres centred on the source. It is a good model at distances large compared with the size of the source, when there is no dissipation and no reflection from nearby objects.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.4*

^mod-a2-4-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.5: Inverse-Square Law
> At a distance $r$ from a point source of power $P$,
>
> $$
> I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2}, \qquad \frac{I_2}{I_1} = \frac{r_1^2}{r_2^2} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.4, eq. (16.12)*

^thm-a2-4-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In the steady state, with no absorption, energy conservation says the same average power $P$ crosses every sphere centred on the source; otherwise energy would pile up or disappear in the shell between two spheres. By symmetry the intensity is the same at all points of a sphere, so $I\cdot 4\pi r^2 = P$.

^der-a2-4-5

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^def-a2-4-1|Def. §A2.4.1]], [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^mod-a2-4-4|Model §A2.4.4]], [[§A4.6 Conservation of Energy#^pr-a4-6-3|CM Principle §A4.6.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.4.6: Amplitude and Distance
> For a mechanical wave the intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude. Therefore, with no absorption,
> - a **spherical** wave from a point source has $I \propto 1/r^2$ and amplitude $A \propto 1/r$;
> - a **circular** wave on a surface, such as a ripple on a pond, has $I \propto 1/r$ (power per unit length of the wave front, $P/2\pi r$) and amplitude $A \propto 1/\sqrt r$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.4*

^thm-a2-4-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]] shows $P \propto A^2$ for a string; University Physics states that the energy and power of mechanical waves are proportional to the squared amplitude in general. In two dimensions the power $P$ crosses every circle of circumference $2\pi r$, so the power per unit length of wave front is $P/2\pi r \propto 1/r$. In three dimensions use [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-5|Theorem §A2.4.5]]. Then $A \propto \sqrt I$ gives the two amplitude laws.

^der-a2-4-6

*Uses:* [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]], [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-5|Theorem §A2.4.5]], [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^def-a2-4-1|Def. §A2.4.1]]

> [!caution] Caution: Spreading in two dimensions
> The amplitude of a circular ripple falls as $1/\sqrt r$, not as $1/r$; only the intensity of a *spherical* wave falls as $1/r^2$. In both cases the energy is conserved; it is only spread over a larger wave front. Real waves also lose energy to absorption, so they fall off faster still. The same inverse-square law holds for light from a point source: [[§A12.3 Energy Carried by EM Waves#^thm-a12-3-4|EM Theorem §A12.3.4]].

^cau-a2-4-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.4.1: Power Supplied by a String Vibrator
> A string $2.00\ \mathrm m$ long with mass $70.0\ \mathrm g$ is under tension $90.0\ \mathrm N$. A vibrator drives one end at $60.0\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and makes a sinusoidal wave of amplitude $4.00\ \mathrm{cm}$. The power the vibrator supplies equals the power the wave carries away. With
>
> $$
> \mu = \frac{0.0700\ \mathrm{kg}}{2.00\ \mathrm m} = 0.0350\ \mathrm{kg/m}, \qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{90.0\ \mathrm N}{0.0350\ \mathrm{kg/m}}} = 50.7\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad \omega = 2\pi(60.0\ \mathrm{Hz}) = 377\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},
> $$
>
> [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]] gives
>
> $$
> P_{\text{ave}} = \frac12\,(0.0350\ \mathrm{kg/m})(0.0400\ \mathrm m)^2(377\ \mathrm{s^{-1}})^2(50.7\ \mathrm{m/s}) = 202\ \mathrm W .
> $$
>
> Doubling the amplitude or the frequency would quadruple this; quadrupling the tension would double $v$ and hence the power.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 16.6*

^ex-a2-4-1

> [!example] Example §A2.4.2: A Ripple Spreading on a Pond
> A circular ripple on a pond has amplitude $0.10\ \mathrm{cm}$ at $6.00\ \mathrm m$ from where a stone fell. What was its amplitude at $2.00\ \mathrm m$? The ripple spreads in two dimensions, so by [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-6|Theorem §A2.4.6]] $A \propto 1/\sqrt r$:
>
> $$
> A = (0.10\ \mathrm{cm})\sqrt{\frac{6.00\ \mathrm m}{2.00\ \mathrm m}} = 0.17\ \mathrm{cm},
> $$
>
> not $0.30\ \mathrm{cm}$, which the $1/r$ law of a spherical wave would give. Tripling the distance divides a spherical wave's amplitude by $3$ (its intensity by $9$), a circular wave's amplitude only by $\sqrt3$ (its intensity by $3$). A numerical spherical case, a $50\ \mathrm W$ loudspeaker, is worked in decibels in [[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^ex-a3-2-2|Example §A3.2.2]].
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.4, Problem 89*

^ex-a2-4-2
