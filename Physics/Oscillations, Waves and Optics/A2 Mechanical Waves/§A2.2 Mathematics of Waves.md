[[§A2.1 Travelling Waves|§A2.1]] described a wave by a handful of numbers. This section describes it completely, by a function $y(x, t)$ that gives the displacement of every element of the medium at every time. Two velocities then come apart cleanly, the speed of the pattern and the velocity of the medium, and differentiating twice leads to the linear wave equation, which every wave of this chapter obeys.

## Pulses and wave functions

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.1: Wave Function; Pulse
> For a wave travelling along the $x$ axis, the **wave function** $y(x, t)$ is the displacement at time $t$ of the element of the medium whose equilibrium position is $x$ (perpendicular to $x$ for a transverse wave, along $x$ for a longitudinal one). A **pulse** is a single, localized disturbance that moves through the medium.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.2*

^def-a2-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.1: A Shape Moving at Constant Speed
> If a disturbance keeps its shape $y(x, 0) = f(x)$ and moves at constant speed $v$, its wave function is
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = f(x - vt) \quad \text{(moving in the } +x \text{ direction)}, \qquad y(x, t) = f(x + vt) \quad \text{(moving in the } -x \text{ direction)} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.2*

^thm-a2-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> In a time $t$ the whole pattern shifts a distance $vt$ in the $+x$ direction. So whatever displacement is now found at $x$ was found at $x - vt$ at time zero: $y(x, t) = y(x - vt, 0) = f(x - vt)$. For motion in the $-x$ direction replace $v$ by $-v$. (In general, the graph of $f(x - d)$ is the graph of $f$ moved a distance $d$ to the right.)

^der-a2-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-1|Def. §A2.2.1]], [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^def-a2-1-4|Def. §A2.1.4]]

## Sinusoidal waves

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.2: Wave Number and Angular Frequency
> For a sinusoidal wave of wavelength $\lambda$ and period $T$, the **wave number** and the **angular frequency** are
>
> $$
> k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \quad (\mathrm{rad/m}, \text{ written } \mathrm{m^{-1}}), \qquad \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f \quad (\mathrm{rad/s}, \text{ written } \mathrm{s^{-1}}) .
> $$
>
> $k$ is to space what $\omega$ is to time: the phase advances by $k$ radians per metre along the wave and by $\omega$ radians per second at a fixed point.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.2, eq. (16.2)*

^def-a2-2-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.2: Sinusoidal Wave Function
> A sinusoidal wave of amplitude $A$, wave number $k$ and angular frequency $\omega$ travelling along the $x$ axis has the wave function
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = A\sin(kx \mp \omega t + \phi),
> $$
>
> with the **minus** sign for a wave moving in the **$+x$** direction and the **plus** sign for one moving in the **$-x$** direction. Its speed is
>
> $$
> v = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{\lambda}{T} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.2, eqs. (16.3)–(16.4)*

^thm-a2-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A sinusoidal snapshot that repeats every $\lambda$ is $y(x, 0) = A\sin\bigl(\tfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}x + \phi\bigr) = A\sin(kx + \phi)$. By [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]], moving it in the $+x$ direction at speed $v$ gives
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = A\sin\bigl(k(x - vt) + \phi\bigr) = A\sin(kx - kvt + \phi), \qquad kv = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\lambda}{T} = \omega ,
> $$
>
> using $v = \lambda/T$ ([[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|Theorem §A2.1.1]]). For the $-x$ direction, $v \to -v$ turns $-\omega t$ into $+\omega t$.
>
> To read the direction off a given wave function, follow a crest: it sits where the phase has a fixed value, say $kx - \omega t + \phi = \pi/2$. As $t$ grows, $x$ must grow too, at $dx/dt = \omega/k$: the crest moves to $+x$. With $kx + \omega t + \phi = \pi/2$ it moves to $-x$.

^der-a2-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-2|Def. §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|Theorem §A2.1.1]]

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.3: Phase; Initial Phase
> The argument $kx \mp \omega t + \phi$ of the sinusoidal wave function is the **phase** of the wave at $(x, t)$, and $\phi$ is its **initial phase** (phase shift), the phase at $x = 0$, $t = 0$. It plays the same role as the phase constant of simple harmonic motion ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion|§A1.1]]): it adjusts the wave to the initial conditions.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.2, eq. (16.5)*

^def-a2-2-3

> [!remark] Remark: Reading a sinusoidal wave function
> Given $y(x, t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)$: the amplitude is $A$; the wavelength is $\lambda = 2\pi/k$; the period is $T = 2\pi/\omega$ and the frequency $f = 1/T$; the speed is $v = \omega/k$; the sign in front of $\omega t$ gives the direction. A cosine describes the same wave with a shifted initial phase, because $\sin\theta = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$:
>
> $$
> A\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi) = A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi') , \qquad \phi' = \phi - \frac{\pi}{2} .
> $$

^rem-a2-2-1

## Motion of the medium

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.3: Velocity and Acceleration of the Medium
> In the sinusoidal wave $y(x, t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)$, the element at $x$ has transverse velocity and acceleration
>
> $$
> v_y(x, t) = \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = -A\omega\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi), \qquad a_y(x, t) = \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = -A\omega^2\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2\,y(x, t) .
> $$
>
> Every element oscillates in simple harmonic motion with the wave's angular frequency $\omega$, with maximum speed $\lvert v_y\rvert_{\max} = A\omega$ and maximum acceleration $\lvert a_y\rvert_{\max} = A\omega^2$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.2*

^thm-a2-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The element at $x$ stays at $x$, so its velocity is the derivative of $y$ with respect to $t$ at fixed $x$: the partial derivative $\partial y/\partial t$. By the chain rule, $\partial_t \sin(kx - \omega t + \phi) = -\omega\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$, and once more $\partial_t\bigl[-\omega\cos(\cdots)\bigr] = -\omega^2\sin(\cdots)$. The relation $a_y = -\omega^2 y$ is the defining equation of SHM with angular frequency $\omega$ ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion|§A1.1]]).

^der-a2-2-3

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.4: Transverse Velocity from the Slope
> For any wave of fixed shape, $y(x, t) = f(x \mp vt)$,
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = \mp\, v\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} .
> $$
>
> For a wave moving in the $+x$ direction, an element moves up where the string slopes down and down where it slopes up; its speed is the wave speed times the magnitude of the slope.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10 · PHY 300, Midterm 2*

^thm-a2-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put $u = x \mp vt$. By the chain rule, $\partial y/\partial x = f'(u)$ and $\partial y/\partial t = f'(u)\cdot(\mp v)$. Compare.

^der-a2-2-4

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]]

> [!caution] Caution: Wave speed is not medium speed
> The wave speed $v$ is constant and points along the direction of propagation. The velocity $v_y$ of the medium in a transverse wave is perpendicular to it and changes all the time, between $\pm A\omega$. The two are unrelated in size: $A\omega$ depends on the amplitude, $v$ does not, and $A\omega$ can be larger or smaller than $v$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^ex-a2-2-1|Example §A2.2.1]]). By [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-4|Theorem §A2.2.4]], $A\omega = v \cdot Ak$, and $Ak$ is the largest slope of the string, small in the regime of this chapter.

^cau-a2-2-1

## The linear wave equation

> [!definition] Definition §A2.2.4: Linear Wave Equation; Linear Wave
> The **linear wave equation** with wave speed $v$ is the partial differential equation
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2 y(x, t)}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{v^2}\,\frac{\partial^2 y(x, t)}{\partial t^2} .
> $$
>
> A wave whose wave function satisfies it is a **linear wave**. The left side is the curvature of the snapshot; the right side is the acceleration of the medium divided by $v^2$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.2, eq. (16.6)*

^def-a2-2-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.5: Travelling Waves Solve the Wave Equation
> Every wave of fixed shape moving at speed $v$, $y(x, t) = f(x - vt)$ or $f(x + vt)$ with $f$ twice differentiable, satisfies the linear wave equation with that speed $v$. In particular the sinusoidal wave $A\sin(kx \mp \omega t + \phi)$ satisfies it with $v = \omega/k$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.2 · PHY 287, Problem Set 10*

^thm-a2-2-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> With $u = x \mp vt$, the chain rule gives $\partial y/\partial x = f'(u)$, $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2 = f''(u)$, and $\partial y/\partial t = \mp v f'(u)$, $\partial^2 y/\partial t^2 = (\mp v)^2 f''(u) = v^2 f''(u)$. Hence $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2 = f''(u) = v^{-2}\,\partial^2 y/\partial t^2$.
>
> University Physics checks the sinusoidal case directly: the acceleration is $-A\omega^2\sin(\cdots)$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]) and the curvature is $-Ak^2\sin(\cdots)$, so their ratio is $\omega^2/k^2 = v^2$ wherever $\sin(\cdots) \ne 0$, and both sides vanish together elsewhere.

^der-a2-2-5

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-4|Def. §A2.2.4]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-3|451 Thm. §28.3]], [[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.2.6: Sums of Solutions Are Solutions
> If $y_1(x, t)$ and $y_2(x, t)$ satisfy the linear wave equation with the same speed $v$, then so does $a\,y_1 + b\,y_2$ for any constants $a$ and $b$. In particular two waves moving in opposite directions, or of different frequencies, can share the same medium, and their sum is again a wave of that medium.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.2*

^thm-a2-2-6

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Derivatives are linear: $\partial_x^2(a y_1 + b y_2) = a\,\partial_x^2 y_1 + b\,\partial_x^2 y_2 = v^{-2}\bigl(a\,\partial_t^2 y_1 + b\,\partial_t^2 y_2\bigr) = v^{-2}\,\partial_t^2(a y_1 + b y_2)$.

^der-a2-2-6

*Uses:* [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-4|Def. §A2.2.4]], [[§28 Basic Properties of the Derivative#^thm-28-2|451 Thm. §28.2]]

> [!remark] Remark: Why this equation matters
> - It is the equation of motion of the medium, not just a property of sinusoids: [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String|§A2.3]] derives it for a string from Newton's second law, [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound|§A3.1]] for sound, and Maxwell's equations give it for light ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|EM Theorem §A12.2.4]]). Any disturbance that obeys it travels at $v$ without changing shape.
> - The name "linear" refers to the linear restoring force of the medium, which makes the equation linear in $y$. [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-6|Theorem §A2.2.6]] is the mathematical root of the superposition principle ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^pr-a2-5-1|Principle §A2.5.1]]).
> - Conversely, every solution on an unbounded medium is a sum $f(x - vt) + g(x + vt)$ of a right-moving and a left-moving wave, and every solution on a string with fixed ends is a sum of standing waves (Fourier series). Both facts are proved at the upper level: Chapter B3 and Chapter B4 (planned).

^rem-a2-2-2

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.2.1: Reading a Sinusoidal Wave
> A transverse wave on a taut string is $y(x, t) = (0.20\ \mathrm m)\sin\bigl(6.28\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\,x - 1.57\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\,t\bigr)$. Reading off $A = 0.20\ \mathrm m$, $k = 6.28\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $\omega = 1.57\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^rem-a2-2-1|Remark]]):
>
> $$
> \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k} = 1.00\ \mathrm m, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 4.00\ \mathrm s, \qquad v = \frac{\omega}{k} = 0.250\ \mathrm{m/s} \ \text{(in the } +x \text{ direction, minus sign)} .
> $$
>
> In $0.80\ \mathrm s$ every crest moves $vt = 0.20\ \mathrm m$. Each element of the string moves in SHM between $\pm 0.20\ \mathrm m$ with period $4.00\ \mathrm s$; by [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]] its largest speed is $A\omega = 0.314\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and its largest acceleration $A\omega^2 = 0.493\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. Here the string moves faster than the wave: its largest slope $Ak = 1.26$ is not small, so this is a large-amplitude wave, beyond the regime in which the wave equation for a string holds ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^mod-a2-3-1|Model §A2.3.1]]).
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 16.3*

> [!example] Example §A2.2.2: A Pulse Passing a Point
> A pulse $y(x, t) = \dfrac{b}{1 + \bigl((x - vt)/a\bigr)^2}$ with $b = 0.010\ \mathrm m$, $a = 0.50\ \mathrm m$ travels in the $+x$ direction at $v = 80\ \mathrm{m/s}$. How fast does an element of the string move as the pulse goes by?
> - When the peak is over the element, the slope there is zero, so by [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-4|Theorem §A2.2.4]] $v_y = 0$: the element is momentarily at rest at its highest point.
> - The largest speed occurs where the pulse is steepest. With $u = (x - vt)/a$, the slope is $\partial y/\partial x = -\dfrac{2b}{a}\,\dfrac{u}{(1 + u^2)^2}$, whose magnitude is largest at $u^2 = 1/3$, where it equals $\dfrac{9}{8\sqrt3}\,\dfrac{b}{a} = 0.650\,\dfrac ba = 0.0130$. Then
>
> $$
> \lvert v_y\rvert_{\max} = v\,\Bigl\lvert\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Bigr\rvert_{\max} = (80\ \mathrm{m/s})(0.0130) = 1.04\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> The element moves up on the leading edge of the pulse and down on the trailing edge, at most $1.04\ \mathrm{m/s}$, while the pulse itself moves sideways at $80\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Differentiating $y$ in time at fixed $x$ gives the same result with more algebra.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Midterm 2*

^ex-a2-2-2
