So far each wave had the medium to itself. Two stones dropped in a pond make ripples that run through each other and emerge unchanged; an echo comes back from a cliff. This section states the principle that governs waves sharing a medium, superposition, works out the sum of two sinusoidal waves that differ only in phase, and then treats a wave meeting the end of its medium, where part or all of it is reflected.

## Superposition

> [!principle] Principle §A2.5.1: Superposition Principle
> When two or more waves travel through the same point of a medium at the same time, the displacement of the medium there is the algebraic sum of the displacements the individual waves would produce on their own:
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = y_1(x, t) + y_2(x, t) + \cdots .
> $$
>
> Each wave then continues as if the others had not been there.
>
> *Domain: linear media, small amplitudes (the restoring force of the medium is proportional to the deformation); for electromagnetic waves the electric and magnetic fields add.*
> *Source: UP1 §16.5*

^pr-a2-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Where superposition comes from
> For waves governed by the linear wave equation the principle is a theorem: sums of solutions are solutions ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-6|Theorem §A2.2.6]]); it is derived in this form at the upper level (Chapter B3, planned). It fails when the medium is pushed beyond its linear range. A string is linear only while its slopes are small ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^mod-a2-3-1|Model §A2.3.1]]), which for mechanical waves usually means amplitudes small compared with the wavelength; breaking ocean waves are a familiar nonlinear case. Waves that obey superposition are **linear waves**; those that do not are **nonlinear**.

^rem-a2-5-1

> [!definition] Definition §A2.5.1: Interference; Constructive and Destructive Interference
> **Interference** is the combination, by superposition, of two or more waves at the same place and time. It is **constructive** where the waves arrive in phase, crest on crest and trough on trough, so that their displacements add; it is **destructive** where they arrive exactly out of phase, crest on trough, so that their displacements cancel (completely, for equal amplitudes).
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.5*

^def-a2-5-1

## Two waves that differ in phase

> [!definition] Definition §A2.5.2: Phase Difference
> Two sinusoidal waves with the same wave number and angular frequency travelling in the same direction, $A_1\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi_1)$ and $A_2\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi_2)$, have the **phase difference** $\phi = \phi_1 - \phi_2$, the same at every point and every time. They are **in phase** if $\phi$ is a multiple of $2\pi$ and **out of phase** ($180^\circ$ out of phase) if $\phi$ is an odd multiple of $\pi$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.5*

^def-a2-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.5.2: Superposition of Two Waves Differing in Phase
> The sum of $y_1 = A\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)$ and $y_2 = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$ is
>
> $$
> y_R(x, t) = \bigl[2A\cos(\phi/2)\bigr]\,\sin\!\Bigl(kx - \omega t + \frac{\phi}{2}\Bigr) ,
> $$
>
> a wave with the same $k$ and $\omega$, phase shift $\phi/2$ and amplitude $A_R = \lvert 2A\cos(\phi/2)\rvert$. The interference is fully constructive ($A_R = 2A$) for $\phi = 0, \pm2\pi, \pm4\pi, \dots$ and fully destructive ($A_R = 0$) for $\phi = \pm\pi, \pm3\pi, \dots$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.5, eq. (16.13)*

^thm-a2-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the superposition principle the resultant is $y_1 + y_2$. Use $\sin u + \sin w = 2\sin\frac{u + w}{2}\cos\frac{u - w}{2}$ with $u = kx - \omega t + \phi$ and $w = kx - \omega t$: then $\frac{u + w}{2} = kx - \omega t + \frac\phi2$ and $\frac{u - w}{2} = \frac\phi2$, which gives the formula. The factor $2A\cos(\phi/2)$ does not depend on $x$ or $t$, so it is the amplitude (up to sign); it equals $\pm 2A$ exactly when $\phi/2$ is a multiple of $\pi$ and $0$ exactly when $\phi/2$ is an odd multiple of $\pi/2$.

^der-a2-5-2

![[ph-wo-a2-5-1.svg]]
*Two waves of equal amplitude, wavelength and frequency (blue, and dashed green) and their sum (red), at one instant, for three phase differences. In phase the sum has amplitude $2A$; at $\phi = 2\pi/3$ it has amplitude $2A\cos(\pi/3) = A$ and lies halfway between them; at $\phi = \pi$ the waves cancel everywhere.*

> [!remark] Remark: Other ways two waves combine
> The superposition principle is always the tool; what changes is the pair of waves.
> - Waves of different shapes or frequencies add point by point; the sum can look complicated (the sound of an orchestra), though each component is simple.
> - Two identical waves moving in **opposite** directions give a standing wave: [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance|§A2.6]].
> - Two waves of slightly **different frequencies** give beats: [[§A3.4 Beats|§A3.4]].
> - Two sources at different distances give a phase difference that varies from place to place, set by the path difference: [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference#^thm-a6-1-1|Theorem §A6.1.1]].

^rem-a2-5-2

## Reflection and transmission

> [!definition] Definition §A2.5.3: Incident, Reflected and Transmitted Waves; Fixed and Free Boundaries
> A wave arriving at a boundary of its medium is the **incident wave**; the wave sent back into the same medium is the **reflected wave**; the wave that continues into a second medium beyond the boundary is the **transmitted wave**. A boundary is **fixed** if the medium there cannot move (a string tied to a wall), and **free** if it can move without resistance (a string tied to a massless ring that slides on a frictionless pole).
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.5*

^def-a2-5-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.5.3: Reflection at a Fixed End and at a Free End
> On an ideal string, a wave reflected at a **fixed end** is inverted: it returns with the same amplitude and a phase change of $\pi$ ($180^\circ$), a crest coming back as a trough. A wave reflected at a **free end** returns upright, with the same amplitude and no phase change.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.5*

^thm-a2-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put the end at $x = 0$, the string on $x < 0$, and let the incident wave be $f(x - vt)$. Each kind of end imposes one condition, and in each case adding a mirror-image wave coming from beyond the end meets it.
>
> *Fixed end:* $y(0, t) = 0$ for all $t$. Try $y = f(x - vt) - f(-x - vt)$. The second term is the incident shape reflected in the end, turned upside down, and moving in the $-x$ direction ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-1|Theorem §A2.2.1]]). At $x = 0$ the two terms cancel, so the condition holds. Both terms solve the wave equation, so their sum does ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-6|Theorem §A2.2.6]]). Once the pulse has gone past the end, only the second term remains on the string: an inverted pulse of the same size moving back.
>
> *Free end:* the ring has no mass, so the net force on it must vanish; the only transverse force on it is the string's pull, $-F_T\,\partial y/\partial x$, so $\partial y/\partial x(0, t) = 0$. Try $y = f(x - vt) + f(-x - vt)$: at $x = 0$ the slopes of the two terms, $f'(-vt)$ and $-f'(-vt)$, cancel. The reflected pulse is upright.
>
> In the language of forces (University Physics): at a fixed end the string pulls up on the wall and, by Newton's third law, the wall pulls down on the string, launching an inverted pulse. At a free end the ring overshoots to twice the pulse height and the string pulls it back down, launching an upright pulse.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 6*

^der-a2-5-3

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.5.4: Reflection and Transmission at a Junction of Two Strings
> Two strings under the same tension, with wave speeds $v_1$ and $v_2$ (linear densities $\mu_1$, $\mu_2$), are knotted together. A sinusoidal wave of amplitude $A_i$ and angular frequency $\omega$ travelling on string 1 toward the knot produces a reflected wave on string 1 and a transmitted wave on string 2, both of the same angular frequency $\omega$, with amplitudes
>
> $$
> A_r = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{v_1 + v_2}\,A_i = \frac{\sqrt{\mu_1} - \sqrt{\mu_2}}{\sqrt{\mu_1} + \sqrt{\mu_2}}\,A_i, \qquad A_t = \frac{2v_2}{v_1 + v_2}\,A_i = \frac{2\sqrt{\mu_1}}{\sqrt{\mu_1} + \sqrt{\mu_2}}\,A_i .
> $$
>
> A negative $A_r$ means the reflected wave is inverted.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 13 slides (waves at the boundary)*

^thm-a2-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put the knot at $x = 0$ and write $y_i = A_i\cos(k_1x - \omega_i t)$, $y_r = A_r\cos(k_1x + \omega_r t)$ on $x < 0$ and $y_t = A_t\cos(k_2x - \omega_t t)$ on $x > 0$, with $k = \omega/v$ on each string.
> - *The string is continuous:* $y_i + y_r = y_t$ at $x = 0$ for all $t$, that is $A_i\cos\omega_i t + A_r\cos\omega_r t = A_t\cos\omega_t t$. This can hold at all times only if $\omega_i = \omega_r = \omega_t = \omega$; then $A_i + A_r = A_t$.
> - *The slope is continuous:* the knot is massless, so the tension forces on it, $F_T\,\partial y/\partial x$ from the right string and $-F_T\,\partial y/\partial x$ from the left one, must cancel. Differentiating, $k_1(A_i - A_r)\sin\omega t = k_2A_t\sin\omega t$, so $k_1(A_i - A_r) = k_2A_t$.
>
> Solving the two equations: $A_t = \dfrac{2k_1}{k_1 + k_2}A_i$ and $A_r = \dfrac{k_1 - k_2}{k_1 + k_2}A_i$. With $k_j = \omega/v_j$, multiplying numerator and denominator by $v_1v_2/\omega$ gives the forms in $v$; with $v_j = \sqrt{F_T/\mu_j}$, those in $\mu$.

^der-a2-5-4

*Uses:* [[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^pr-a2-5-1|Principle §A2.5.1]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]], [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Reading the junction formulas
> - **Into a slower (denser) string**, $v_2 < v_1$: the reflected wave is inverted. **Into a faster (lighter) string**, $v_2 > v_1$: it is upright. The transmitted wave is always upright; it is smaller than the incident wave when it enters a denser string and larger when it enters a lighter one.
> - **Limits.** $\mu_2 \to \infty$ ($v_2 \to 0$, a wall): $A_r = -A_i$, $A_t = 0$, the fixed end of [[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-3|Theorem §A2.5.3]]. $\mu_2 \to 0$ ($v_2 \to \infty$, a massless ring): $A_r = +A_i$, the free end, whose motion $A_t = 2A_i$ is twice the incident amplitude. Equal strings: $A_r = 0$, no reflection.
> - **Energy.** With $P \propto \mu v\,\omega^2A^2 = (F_T/v)\,\omega^2A^2$ ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^thm-a2-4-3|Theorem §A2.4.3]]) one checks $P_r + P_t = P_i$: the junction neither stores nor loses energy. The combination $\mu v = \sqrt{\mu F_T}$ is the string's impedance, which also handles strings of different tension: Chapter B5 (planned).
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 13 slides (waves at the boundary)*

^rem-a2-5-3

> [!caution] Caution: What changes at a boundary
> The frequency never changes at a boundary; it is fixed by the source. The reflected wave travels on the same string as the incident one, so it has the same speed and wavelength. The transmitted wave has the speed of the new string and therefore a different wavelength, $\lambda_2 = v_2/f$.

^cau-a2-5-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.5.1: Phase Shift from the Resultant Amplitude
> Two sinusoidal waves travel in the same direction through a medium, each with amplitude $3.00\ \mathrm{cm}$, wavelength $5.20\ \mathrm m$ and period $6.52\ \mathrm s$, but one is shifted in phase by $\phi$. The resultant has amplitude $5.00\ \mathrm{cm}$. By [[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-2|Theorem §A2.5.2]],
>
> $$
> 2A\cos\frac\phi2 = 5.00\ \mathrm{cm} \;\Longrightarrow\; \cos\frac\phi2 = \frac{5.00}{6.00} = 0.833 \;\Longrightarrow\; \phi = 2(33.6^\circ) = 67.1^\circ = 1.17\ \mathrm{rad}
> $$
>
> (or $-67.1^\circ$). This is $67.1^\circ/360^\circ = 0.186$ of a cycle: one wave is displaced along $x$ by $0.186\,\lambda = 0.970\ \mathrm m$ relative to the other. The period plays no role.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.5, Problem 93*

^ex-a2-5-1

> [!example] Example §A2.5.2: Two Strings Spliced Together
> A string with $\mu_1 = 0.006\ \mathrm{kg/m}$ is spliced to one with $\mu_2 = 0.012\ \mathrm{kg/m}$, under the same tension, and a sinusoidal wave runs on the light string toward the splice. Which properties do the reflected and transmitted waves share with the incident wave?
> - **Frequency:** all three equal, forced by the continuity of the string at the splice ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-4|Theorem §A2.5.4]]).
> - **Speed and wavelength:** the reflected wave has the incident ones. The transmitted wave is slower, $v_2/v_1 = \sqrt{\mu_1/\mu_2} = 0.707$, and has the shorter wavelength $\lambda_2 = 0.707\,\lambda_1$.
> - **Amplitude:** with $\sqrt{\mu_1}:\sqrt{\mu_2} = 1:\sqrt2$,
>
> $$
> A_r = \frac{1 - \sqrt2}{1 + \sqrt2}\,A_i = -0.172\,A_i \ \text{(inverted)}, \qquad A_t = \frac{2}{1 + \sqrt2}\,A_i = 0.828\,A_i .
> $$
>
> - **Power:** $P_r/P_i = (0.172)^2 = 0.029$ and $P_t/P_i = \sqrt{\mu_2/\mu_1}\,(0.828)^2 = 0.971$: they add to $1$, and almost all the energy goes through.
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Final practice (with data from UP1 §16.5, Conceptual Question 25)*

^ex-a2-5-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The sum-to-product identity $\sin u + \sin w = 2\sin\frac{u + w}{2}\cos\frac{u - w}{2}$ does the work in Theorem §A2.5.2. The same calculation, with the phase difference produced by a path difference, gives the double-slit pattern of light: [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference|§A6.1]].
> - The phase change of light on reflection from a medium of higher index is the optical counterpart of the fixed end: [[§A6.3 Thin-Film Interference|§A6.3]].
