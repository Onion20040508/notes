A wave on a string of finite length reflects at both ends ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-3|Theorem §A2.5.3]]) and runs back and forth through itself. By superposition, two identical waves moving in opposite directions add up to a pattern that does not travel at all: a standing wave. On a string held at both ends only certain wavelengths fit, so the string can vibrate in this way only at a discrete set of frequencies, its normal modes. They set the notes of every string instrument.

## Standing waves

> [!definition] Definition §A2.6.1: Standing Wave; Node; Antinode
> A **standing wave** is a wave pattern that oscillates in place without propagating. Its **nodes** are the points where the medium never moves; its **antinodes** are the points where it oscillates with the largest amplitude.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.6*

^def-a2-6-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.6.1: Standing Wave from Two Opposite Travelling Waves
> The superposition of two identical sinusoidal waves moving in opposite directions, $y_1 = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$ and $y_2 = A\sin(kx + \omega t)$, is
>
> $$
> y(x, t) = \bigl[2A\sin(kx)\bigr]\cos(\omega t) .
> $$
>
> The element at $x$ oscillates in SHM with amplitude $\lvert 2A\sin kx\rvert$. The nodes are at
>
> $$
> x = 0, \ \frac\lambda2, \ \lambda, \ \frac{3\lambda}{2}, \dots = n\,\frac\lambda2 \quad (n = 0, 1, 2, \dots),
> $$
>
> and the antinodes, which oscillate between $\pm 2A$, halfway between them at $x = \lambda/4, 3\lambda/4, 5\lambda/4, \dots$. The whole string is momentarily straight at $t = T/4, 3T/4, \dots$ and has its largest displacement at $t = 0, T/2, T, \dots$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.6, eq. (16.14)*

^thm-a2-6-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the superposition principle $y = y_1 + y_2$. Expand with $\sin(\alpha \mp \beta) = \sin\alpha\cos\beta \mp \cos\alpha\sin\beta$, $\alpha = kx$, $\beta = \omega t$: the $\cos(kx)\sin(\omega t)$ terms cancel and the $\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)$ terms add, giving $2A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)$. The displacement vanishes for all $t$ exactly where $\sin kx = 0$, that is $kx = n\pi$, $x = n\pi/k = n\lambda/2$. The amplitude $\lvert 2A\sin kx\rvert$ is largest, $2A$, where $\sin kx = \pm1$: $kx = \pi/2, 3\pi/2, \dots$, $x = \lambda/4, 3\lambda/4, \dots$. The time factor $\cos\omega t$ vanishes at $\omega t = \pi/2, 3\pi/2, \dots$, $t = T/4, 3T/4, \dots$, where the two waves are exactly out of phase everywhere, and equals $\pm1$ at $t = nT/2$, where they are in phase.

^der-a2-6-1

> [!remark] Remark: Standing versus travelling
> - In a travelling wave every element has the same amplitude and the phase changes steadily along $x$. In a standing wave the amplitude depends on $x$, and all elements between two neighbouring nodes move in step, while those in the next loop move exactly opposite.
> - A standing wave carries no net energy. The power through a point ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave#^der-a2-4-3|derivation of Theorem §A2.4.3]]), $-F_T\,(\partial y/\partial x)(\partial y/\partial t) = F_TA^2k\omega\,\sin(2kx)\sin(2\omega t)$, averages to zero: energy sloshes back and forth within each loop, kinetic near the antinodes, where the string moves fastest, and stretching energy near the nodes, where it is steepest.
> - Standing waves are not limited to strings. Vibrations of a refrigerator or a fan set up standing waves on the surface of milk in a bowl; air columns in flutes and organ pipes carry standing sound waves ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]]).

## Normal modes of a string fixed at both ends

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.6.2: Normal Modes of a String Fixed at Both Ends
> An ideal string of length $L$ fixed at both ends, with wave speed $v = \sqrt{F_T/\mu}$, can vibrate as a standing wave only with the wavelengths and frequencies
>
> $$
> \lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \qquad f_n = \frac{v}{\lambda_n} = n\,\frac{v}{2L} = n f_1, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
> $$
>
> The $n$th mode has the shape $y_n(x, t) = A_n\sin(n\pi x/L)\cos(\omega_n t + \delta_n)$, with $\omega_n = 2\pi f_n$: $n$ half-wavelengths fit between the ends.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.6, eqs. (16.15)–(16.16) · PHY 287, week 13 slides*

^thm-a2-6-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put the ends at $x = 0$ and $x = L$. A wave travelling toward $x = 0$, $A\sin(kx + \omega t)$, reflects there inverted ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-3|Theorem §A2.5.3]]): the reflected wave is $-A\sin(-kx + \omega t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$. Their sum is the standing wave $2A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)$ of [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-1|Theorem §A2.6.1]], which has a node at $x = 0$ automatically. The end $x = L$ must be a node too, for all $t$:
>
> $$
> \sin(kL) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad kL = n\pi, \quad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
> $$
>
> ($n = 0$ is the string at rest). So $\lambda_n = 2\pi/k_n = 2L/n$, and $f_n = v/\lambda_n$ by [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|Theorem §A2.1.1]], with $v$ from [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]. A time shift $\delta_n$ is allowed because the starting time is arbitrary.

^der-a2-6-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-1|Theorem §A2.6.1]], [[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-3|Theorem §A2.5.3]], [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|Theorem §A2.1.1]], [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]

> [!definition] Definition §A2.6.2: Normal Mode; Fundamental; Harmonics and Overtones
> A **normal mode** of a system is a pattern of motion in which every part oscillates sinusoidally at one common frequency, a **normal frequency** (resonant frequency) of the system. For a string, the normal modes are its possible standing waves. The lowest normal frequency $f_1$ is the **fundamental frequency** or **first harmonic**; $f_n = nf_1$ is the **$n$th harmonic**. The normal frequencies above the fundamental are the **overtones**: the second harmonic is the first overtone, the third harmonic the second overtone, and so on.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.6*

^def-a2-6-2

![[ph-wo-a2-6-2.svg]]
*The first four normal modes of a string fixed at both ends, each drawn at its two extreme positions (solid and dashed blue), with the interior nodes in red. Mode $n$ fits $n$ half-wavelengths into $L$ and vibrates at $n$ times the fundamental frequency.*

> [!caution] Caution: Counting modes
> - $\lambda_n = 2L/n$, not $2nL$: higher modes have *shorter* wavelengths.
> - The $n$th mode has $n$ loops and $n$ antinodes, and $n + 1$ nodes counting the two ends.
> - "Harmonic" and "overtone" are numbered differently: the $n$th harmonic is the $(n - 1)$th overtone.
> - In a lab, the end attached to a string vibrator moves a little, but its motion is small compared with that of the antinodes at resonance, so it counts as a node.

^cau-a2-6-1

> [!remark] Remark: Other boundary conditions
> - **Both ends free** (antinodes at both ends, such as a metal rod held at its quarter points, [[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^thm-a2-5-3|free end]]: $\partial y/\partial x = 0$): the same $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ and $f_n = nv/2L$. University Physics' formulas hold for any **symmetric** boundary conditions.
> - **One end fixed, one free**: a node at one end and an antinode at the other, so an odd number of quarter-wavelengths fits, $L = n\lambda_n/4$ with $n = 1, 3, 5, \dots$, and $f_n = nv/4L$. Only odd harmonics occur. This is the pipe closed at one end: [[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]].
> - Supports in the middle force extra nodes and remove the modes that do not have them: a rod clamped at its quarter points cannot vibrate in the mode with $\lambda = 2L$ (for longitudinal modes, [[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^ex-a3-3-3|Example §A3.3.3]]).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 6*

^rem-a2-6-2

> [!remark] Remark: Resonance and general motion
> - **Resonance.** Driven at one of its normal frequencies, a string builds up a large standing wave from a small driving amplitude, because each reflected wave returns in step with the drive; between normal frequencies the response stays small. This is the extended-system version of the resonance of a driven oscillator ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^def-a1-6-2|Def. §A1.6.2]]): each normal mode responds to the drive like a damped oscillator whose natural frequency is the mode's normal frequency, so the response peaks at each $f_n$, and the weaker the damping the higher and narrower the peaks ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-2|Theorem §A1.6.2]]). Buildings, bridges and roofs have normal frequencies too: an earthquake that shakes them at one of them can destroy them while leaving neighbouring structures of different size intact. Engineers find the troublesome frequencies of machines by Fourier analysis of the sound they make.
> - **General motion.** A plucked string does not vibrate in a single mode: its motion is a superposition $y(x, t) = \sum_n B_n\sin(n\pi x/L)\cos(\omega_n t + \delta_n)$ of all its modes, with amplitudes fixed by the initial shape (Fourier series; PHY 287, weeks 13–14; Chapter B4, planned). A chain of $N$ beads has exactly $N$ normal modes; the continuous string has infinitely many, and for $n \ll N$ the bead chain's frequencies approach $f_n = nv/2L$ ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^rem-a2-3-1|§A2.3]]; Chapter B2, planned).
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 13 slides*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.6.1: Standing Waves on a Lab String
> A string with $\mu = 0.006\ \mathrm{kg/m}$ runs from a string vibrator over a pulley $L = 2.00\ \mathrm m$ away to a hanging mass of $2.00\ \mathrm{kg}$; both ends are nodes. The tension is the weight of the hanging mass, so
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{\frac{(2.00\ \mathrm{kg})(9.80\ \mathrm{m/s^2})}{0.006\ \mathrm{kg/m}}} = 57.2\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> By [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]] the first three modes have $\lambda_n = 2L/n = 4.00\ \mathrm m,\ 2.00\ \mathrm m,\ 1.33\ \mathrm m$, and the vibrator must be tuned to
>
> $$
> f_1 = \frac{v}{2L} = 14.3\ \mathrm{Hz}, \qquad f_2 = 28.6\ \mathrm{Hz}, \qquad f_3 = 42.9\ \mathrm{Hz} .
> $$
>
> The same modes can be reached at a fixed frequency by changing the wave speed instead: mode $n$ at frequency $f$ needs $v = 2Lf/n$, so a hanging mass proportional to $1/n^2$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 16.7*

^ex-a2-6-1

> [!example] Example §A2.6.2: Harmonics of a Guitar String
> A guitar string has length $L = 60\ \mathrm{cm}$, mass $M = 0.20\ \mathrm g$ and tension $50\ \mathrm N$. Plucked, it sounds its fundamental together with many harmonics. With $\mu = M/L = 3.33 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{kg/m}$,
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{\frac{50\ \mathrm N}{3.33 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{kg/m}}} = 387\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad f_1 = \frac{v}{2L} = 323\ \mathrm{Hz} .
> $$
>
> Now the player touches the string lightly one third of the way from one end. That point is forced to be a node, so only the modes with a node at $x = L/3$ survive: $\sin(n\pi/3) = 0$, that is $n = 3, 6, 9, \dots$, with frequencies $f_3 = 968\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $f_6 = 1940\ \mathrm{Hz}$, $f_9 = 2900\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and so on. The note jumps to three times the fundamental (a guitarist's "harmonic"). Pressing the string down onto the fingerboard at that point is different: it shortens the vibrating part to $2L/3$, whose fundamental is $\tfrac32f_1 = 484\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Midterm 1 practice*
