[[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves|§A2.2]] showed that a shape travelling at speed $v$ obeys the linear wave equation, but not *which* speed a medium allows. For a stretched string, Newton's second law applied to a short piece of it answers both questions at once: the string obeys the wave equation, and the speed is fixed by the tension and the mass per length.

## The string and its model

> [!definition] Definition §A2.3.1: Linear Mass Density
> The **linear mass density** of a string is its mass per unit length, $\mu = m/l$ (in $\mathrm{kg/m}$). For a uniform string, a piece of length $\Delta x$ has mass $\Delta m = \mu\,\Delta x$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.3, eq. (16.7)*

^def-a2-3-1

> [!model] Model §A2.3.1: Ideal String
> The **ideal string** is
> - uniform, with constant linear mass density $\mu$;
> - perfectly flexible: the tension $F_T$ ([[§A3.6 Common Forces#^def-a3-6-2|CM Def. §A3.6.2]]) acts along the tangent to the string;
> - displaced only slightly and only transversely: the slope $\partial y/\partial x$ is small everywhere, so the tension keeps the same magnitude $F_T$ along the string and the elements do not move along $x$;
> - free of gravity and damping (the tension is large compared with the weight, and friction is neglected).
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.3*

^mod-a2-3-1

## Wave speed on a string

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.3.2: Wave Equation for a Stretched String
> On an ideal string under tension $F_T$ with linear mass density $\mu$, the transverse displacement obeys
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} = \frac{\mu}{F_T}\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} ,
> $$
>
> the linear wave equation ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-4|Def. §A2.2.4]]) with wave speed
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{\frac{F_T}{\mu}} .
> $$
>
> Pulses and waves of every shape and every frequency travel at this speed.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.3, eq. (16.8)*

^thm-a2-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the element between $x_1$ and $x_2 = x_1 + \Delta x$, of mass $\mu\,\Delta x$. The rest of the string pulls on it at both ends with force $F_T$ along the tangent, at angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ to the horizontal. For small angles $\cos\theta \approx 1$ and $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta = \partial y/\partial x$ (errors of order $\theta^2$). So the horizontal components, $\mp F_T\cos\theta$, cancel and the element does not move along $x$. The vertical components are $+F_T\,(\partial y/\partial x)_{x_2}$ at the right end and $-F_T\,(\partial y/\partial x)_{x_1}$ at the left end, where the string pulls back toward $-x$. Newton's second law for the vertical motion gives
>
> $$
> \mu\,\Delta x\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = F_T\left[\Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Bigr)_{x_2} - \Bigl(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\Bigr)_{x_1}\right] .
> $$
>
> Divide by $F_T\,\Delta x$ and let $\Delta x \to 0$. The right side becomes the difference quotient of $\partial y/\partial x$, whose limit is $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\mu}{F_T}\,\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} .
> $$
>
> This is the linear wave equation with $1/v^2 = \mu/F_T$. By [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-5|Theorem §A2.2.5]] every shape $f(x \mp vt)$ solves it with this one $v$.
>
> The net force is a *difference* of two tension components, each proportional to a slope; that is why the acceleration is proportional to the *second* derivative, the curvature. A straight piece of string, however tilted, feels no net force.

^der-a2-3-2

> [!remark] Remark: From beads to a string
> PHY 287 reaches the same equation from the other side. Take $N$ beads of mass $m$, spaced $l$ apart on a massless string under tension $T$. For small displacements the neighbours pull bead $p$ with a net transverse force $(T/l)(y_{p+1} - 2y_p + y_{p-1})$, so
>
> $$
> m\,\ddot y_p = \frac{T}{l}\,\bigl(y_{p+1} - 2y_p + y_{p-1}\bigr) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot y_p = \frac{T}{m/l}\;\frac{y(x + l) - 2y(x) + y(x - l)}{l^2}, \qquad x = pl .
> $$
>
> As $l \to 0$ with $m/l = \mu$ fixed, the last fraction tends to $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2$, and the bead chain becomes the string with $v^2 = T/\mu$. The normal modes of the chain turn into those of the string ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]]). Coupled oscillators and this limit are treated at the upper level: Chapters B2–B3 (planned).
>
> *Source: PHY 287, week 13 slides (N very large)*

^rem-a2-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Elastic over inertial
> $v = \sqrt{F_T/\mu}$ has the general form $v = \sqrt{\text{elastic property}/\text{inertial property}}$: a stiffer restoring force speeds a wave up, more inertia slows it down. The speed depends on the medium only, not on the amplitude or the frequency, so on an ideal string a pulse of any shape keeps its shape. For a longitudinal wave in a fluid the elastic property is the bulk modulus $B$ ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-3|CM Def. §A7.2.3]]) and the inertial one the density $\rho$: $v = \sqrt{B/\rho}$, about $343\ \mathrm{m/s}$ for air at $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]]).

^rem-a2-3-2

> [!caution] Caution: Tension, mass and scaling
> - When a hanging mass $M$ provides the tension over a pulley, $F_T = Mg$, a force in newtons, not $M$.
> - $\mu$ is mass per length. Two strings of the same material and tension but different lengths carry waves at the same speed.
> - $v \propto \sqrt{F_T}$: doubling the tension multiplies the speed by $\sqrt2$; doubling the speed takes four times the tension.

^cau-a2-3-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.3.1: Guitar Strings
> The high E string of a guitar has $\mu = 3.09 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{kg/m}$ and tension $F_T = 56.40\ \mathrm N$; the low E string has $\mu = 5.78 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg/m}$, about $19$ times more. By [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]], waves on the high E string travel at
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{\frac{56.40\ \mathrm N}{3.09 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{kg/m}}} = 427\ \mathrm{m/s} .
> $$
>
> For the same speed on the low E string the tension must grow in proportion to $\mu$: $F_T = \mu v^2 = (5.78 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg/m})(427.2\ \mathrm{m/s})^2 = 1055\ \mathrm N$. Real guitars do not do this. All strings have the same vibrating length $L$, and the fundamental frequency is $f_1 = v/2L$ ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]]). The high E note $329.63\ \mathrm{Hz}$ gives $L = v/2f_1 = 0.648\ \mathrm m$; the low E note $82.41\ \mathrm{Hz}$ on that length needs only $v = 2Lf_1 = 107\ \mathrm{m/s}$, hence $F_T = \mu v^2 = 66\ \mathrm N$, close to the high E tension. The heavy string makes the low note without an enormous tension.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 16.5*

^ex-a2-3-1

> [!example] Example §A2.3.2: Measuring a Cable with a Stopwatch
> A long cable of unknown length $L$ and mass $M$ is fixed to a wall at one end; the other end runs over a pulley to a hanging weight of mass $100M$. A pulse sent along the cable returns after a round trip of $4.00\ \mathrm s$. The tension is $F_T = 100Mg$ and $\mu = M/L$, so
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{\frac{100Mg}{M/L}} = \sqrt{100\,gL}, \qquad 2L = v\,(4.00\ \mathrm s) \;\Longrightarrow\; 4L^2 = 100\,gL\,(16.0\ \mathrm{s^2}) \;\Longrightarrow\; L = 400\,g\ \mathrm{s^2} = 3.92\ \mathrm{km} .
> $$
>
> The pulse travels at $v = 1.96\ \mathrm{km/s}$. The cable's mass cancels: only the ratio of the hanging mass to the cable mass enters (sag under the cable's own weight is neglected).
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Problem Set 10*

^ex-a2-3-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The passage from the difference quotient of the slope to $\partial^2 y/\partial x^2$, and from the bead chain to the string, uses the definition of the (partial) derivative ([[§4 Partial Derivatives#^def-4-1|452 Def. §4.1]]); $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta$ for small slopes is Taylor's theorem to first order ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]).
> - The same balance of elastic and inertial terms gives the speed of light from $\varepsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$: [[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^thm-a12-2-4|EM Theorem §A12.2.4]].
