Chapter A1 followed a single oscillator. When oscillators are linked, as the elements of a string or the molecules of air are, a disturbance at one place sets its neighbours moving and spreads: a wave. This section names the kinds of waves and the quantities that describe a periodic one, and proves the one relation that ties them together, $v = f\lambda$.

## Waves and media

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.1: Wave; Mechanical Wave
> A **wave** is a disturbance that propagates away from the place where it was created. A **mechanical wave** is a wave in a material **medium** that resists deformation with an elastic restoring force; it is governed by Newton's laws. A mechanical wave carries energy and momentum from place to place, but no mass: each piece of the medium only oscillates about its own equilibrium position.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.1*

^def-a2-1-1

> [!remark] Remark: Three kinds of waves
> - **Mechanical waves** need a medium: water waves, sound, seismic waves, waves on strings. They are the subject of chapters A2 and A3.
> - **Electromagnetic waves** are oscillations of electric and magnetic fields and need no medium; in vacuum they all travel at $c = 2.99792458 \times 10^8\ \mathrm{m/s}$ ([[§A12.2 Plane Electromagnetic Waves#^mod-a12-2-1|EM Model §A12.2.1]]). Light is the subject of chapters A4 to A7.
> - **Matter waves** are associated with electrons, protons and other particles (de Broglie, 1924): Quantum Mechanics (planned).
>
> Ocean breakers are mechanical waves, but large and nonlinear; the simple models of this chapter do not describe them.

^rem-a2-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.2: Transverse and Longitudinal Waves
> In a **transverse wave** the medium is displaced perpendicular to the direction of propagation. In a **longitudinal** (or **compressional**) **wave** it is displaced parallel to the direction of propagation. A wave may also combine the two.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.1*

^def-a2-1-2

> [!remark] Remark: Which waves are which
> A wave on a string or a ripple on a pond is transverse. Sound in air or water is longitudinal: a fluid has no appreciable shear strength, so it cannot support a sideways displacement, only compressions and rarefactions ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^def-a3-1-1|Def. §A3.1.1]]). A solid can carry both. Earthquakes send out longitudinal P-waves (compressional) and transverse S-waves (shear) through the Earth, at different speeds, plus surface waves; the delay between their arrivals at a seismograph gives the distance to the source. A stretched spring (a Slinky) carries either kind: shake the end sideways for a transverse wave, push it back and forth along its length for a longitudinal one.

^rem-a2-1-2

## Wave quantities

A **periodic** wave repeats itself in space and in time. Its simplest kind, a **simple harmonic** (sinusoidal) wave, is produced by a source that oscillates in simple harmonic motion ([[§A1.1 Simple Harmonic Motion#^def-a1-1-2|Def. §A1.1.2]]): then every element of the medium oscillates in SHM too.

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.3: Amplitude, Wavelength, Period and Frequency
> For a periodic wave:
> - the **amplitude** $A$ is the largest displacement of the medium from its equilibrium position; a symmetric wave has **crests** at $+A$ and **troughs** at $-A$;
> - the **wavelength** $\lambda$ is the distance between two adjacent identical points of the wave, points with the same displacement *and* the same slope, for example two neighbouring crests;
> - the **period** $T$ is the time for one complete oscillation of an element of the medium, which is also the time for one wavelength to pass a fixed point;
> - the **frequency** $f = 1/T$ is the number of wavelengths passing a fixed point per unit time, measured in hertz, $1\ \mathrm{Hz} = 1\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.1*

^def-a2-1-3

> [!definition] Definition §A2.1.4: Wave Velocity and Wave Speed
> The **wave velocity** (or **propagation velocity**) $\vec v$ is the velocity with which the disturbance moves through the medium: its magnitude is the distance a fixed feature of the wave, such as a crest, travels per unit time. Its magnitude $v$ is the **wave speed**.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.1*

^def-a2-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A2.1.1: Wave Speed, Wavelength and Frequency
> For every periodic wave,
>
> $$
> v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.1, eq. (16.1)*

^thm-a2-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Watch a crest. During one period $T$ the element at the crest's position goes through one full oscillation and is back at the top, so the wave pattern there looks exactly as it did; meanwhile the next crest behind has arrived. The crest has moved one wavelength in one period, so its speed is $v = \lambda/T$, and $1/T = f$.

^der-a2-1-1

*Uses:* [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^def-a2-1-3|Def. §A2.1.3]], [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^def-a2-1-4|Def. §A2.1.4]]

![[ph-wo-a2-1-1.svg]]
*A sinusoidal wave moving in the $+x$ direction, at $t = 0$ (dashed grey) and a quarter period later (blue): every crest has moved $\lambda/4$ to the right. The marked element (red) does not travel with the wave; it moves up and down between $+A$ and $-A$.*

> [!remark] Remark: What sets what
> In $v = f\lambda$ the three quantities are not on an equal footing. The **frequency** is set by the source: a wave driven by an oscillating end has the frequency of the drive. The **speed** is set by the medium (tension and mass density for a string, [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]; bulk modulus and density for a fluid, [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]]). The **wavelength** then follows, $\lambda = v/f$. When a wave passes into another medium its frequency stays the same and its wavelength changes with the speed. The amplitude is independent of all three: it is fixed by how hard the source drives. For electromagnetic waves in vacuum the relation reads $c = f\lambda$ ([[§A12.5 The Electromagnetic Spectrum#^thm-a12-5-1|EM Theorem §A12.5.1]]).

^rem-a2-1-3

> [!caution] Caution: Two different velocities
> - The wave speed $v$ is the speed of the *pattern*. The medium itself moves perpendicular to $\vec v$ in a transverse wave (along it in a longitudinal one), back and forth about equilibrium, with a velocity that changes all the time ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]).
> - Two points at the same height are not necessarily a wavelength apart: on a sinusoid, consecutive zero crossings are only $\lambda/2$ apart, because the slopes there are opposite.

^cau-a2-1-1

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A2.1.1: Wave on a Rope
> One end of a $30.00\ \mathrm m$ rope is tied to a wall; a student holds the other end at constant tension and moves it up and down at $f = 2.00\ \mathrm{Hz}$ with an amplitude of $20.00\ \mathrm{cm}$. The first crest reaches the wall $6.00\ \mathrm s$ after it is made. The wave speed is distance over time, the period is fixed by the drive, and the wavelength follows from [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|Theorem §A2.1.1]]:
>
> $$
> v = \frac{30.00\ \mathrm m}{6.00\ \mathrm s} = 5.00\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad T = \frac1f = 0.500\ \mathrm s, \qquad \lambda = vT = 2.50\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> The amplitude played no role: it does not affect $v$, $T$ or $\lambda$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 16.1*

^ex-a2-1-1

> [!example] Example §A2.1.2: Same Frequency, Different Wavelengths
> The high E string of a guitar vibrates at $f = 329.63\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and waves on it travel at $v_{\text{string}} = 427\ \mathrm{m/s}$ (computed in [[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^ex-a2-3-1|Example §A2.3.1]]). The vibrating string pushes the air at its own frequency, so the sound has the same $f$, but it travels at $v_{\text{air}} = 343\ \mathrm{m/s}$. With $\lambda = v/f$:
>
> $$
> \lambda_{\text{string}} = \frac{427\ \mathrm{m/s}}{329.63\ \mathrm{Hz}} = 1.30\ \mathrm m, \qquad \lambda_{\text{air}} = \frac{343\ \mathrm{m/s}}{329.63\ \mathrm{Hz}} = 1.04\ \mathrm m .
> $$
>
> The two waves share the frequency, which comes from the source, and differ in wavelength, because the media have different wave speeds.
>
> *Source: UP1 §16.1, with data from Example 16.5*

^ex-a2-1-2
