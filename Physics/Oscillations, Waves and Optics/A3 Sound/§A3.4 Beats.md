[[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound|§A3.3]] superposed waves of the *same* frequency, which interfere steadily in space. Two sounds of slightly *different* frequencies drift in and out of step, so at a fixed place the interference alternates in time between constructive and destructive: the sound throbs. These beats are how instruments are tuned, and beating between overtones is a large part of why some pairs of notes sound harsh.

## Beat frequency

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.4.1: Beats
> Two sound waves of equal amplitude $A$ and frequencies $f_1$ and $f_2$ produce at a fixed point the superposition
>
> $$
> y(t) = A\cos(2\pi f_1 t) + A\cos(2\pi f_2 t) = \Bigl[2A\cos\Bigl(2\pi\,\frac{f_2 - f_1}{2}\,t\Bigr)\Bigr]\cos(2\pi f_{\text{avg}}\,t), \qquad f_{\text{avg}} = \frac{f_1 + f_2}{2} .
> $$
>
> When $\lvert f_2 - f_1\rvert \ll f_{\text{avg}}$ one hears a tone of frequency $f_{\text{avg}}$ whose amplitude swells and fades. The loudness peaks $f_{\text{beat}}$ times per second, with the **beat frequency**
>
> $$
> f_{\text{beat}} = \lvert f_2 - f_1\rvert .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.6, eq. (17.17)*

^thm-a3-4-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> By the superposition principle ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves#^pr-a2-5-1|Principle §A2.5.1]]) the displacements add. Take the point at $x = 0$, so each wave $A\cos(k_ix - 2\pi f_it)$ reduces to $A\cos(2\pi f_it)$. With $\cos u + \cos w = 2\cos\frac{u + w}{2}\cos\frac{u - w}{2}$ and $u = 2\pi f_2t$, $w = 2\pi f_1t$, the sum is the product in the statement (cosine is even, so the sign of $f_2 - f_1$ does not matter). If $\lvert f_2 - f_1\rvert$ is small, the first factor changes slowly compared with the second and acts as a slowly varying amplitude. The loudness follows the magnitude of that amplitude, $2A\lvert\cos(\pi\,\lvert f_2 - f_1\rvert\,t)\rvert$. Since $\lvert\cos\theta\rvert$ has period $\pi$ (not $2\pi$), this envelope repeats after $\Delta t = 1/\lvert f_2 - f_1\rvert$: the beat frequency is $\lvert f_2 - f_1\rvert$. The maxima are the moments when the two waves are in phase, the silences those when they are in antiphase.

> [!caution] Caution: Not half the difference
> The amplitude factor $\cos\bigl(2\pi\frac{f_2 - f_1}{2}t\bigr)$ oscillates at *half* the frequency difference, and it is tempting to call that the beat frequency. But the ear hears loudness, which peaks at both the maxima and the minima of this factor: the beat frequency is the full difference $\lvert f_2 - f_1\rvert$. Schwartz's example: $250\ \mathrm{Hz} + 270\ \mathrm{Hz}$ contains a $10\ \mathrm{Hz}$ factor, heard as a $20\ \mathrm{Hz}$ rattle.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7*

^cau-a3-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: When beats are heard, and tuning
> The description as "one tone with a slowly varying amplitude" needs $\lvert f_2 - f_1\rvert \ll f_{\text{avg}}$. Beats of a few per second up to a few tens per second are heard as a throb or a rattle; when the difference is large, one hears two separate tones (or a chord) instead. A piano tuner sounds a tuning fork together with a string and adjusts the tension: the beats slow down as the string approaches the fork's frequency and stop when the two agree.

^rem-a3-4-1

> [!remark] Remark: Beats and consonance
> Real instruments produce harmonics along with the fundamental ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^rem-a3-3-6|Remark: What makes an instrument sound like itself]]), so two notes beat whenever some harmonic of one is close to, but not equal to, some harmonic of the other. Two notes whose frequencies are in a ratio of small integers have harmonics that coincide exactly, and sound **consonant**: the octave $2:1$, the perfect fifth $3:2$ (the third harmonic of C4 at $261\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is $783\ \mathrm{Hz}$, and so is the second harmonic of G4 tuned to an exact fifth, $\tfrac32 \times 261\ \mathrm{Hz} = 391.5\ \mathrm{Hz}$), the perfect fourth $4:3$, the major third $5:4$. A keyboard cannot hold these exact ratios in every key at once; it compromises with the equal-tempered scale, in which successive semitones differ by the factor $2^{1/12}$. Its fifths ($2^{7/12} = 1.498$) and fourths ($2^{5/12} = 1.335$) are very close to $3/2$ and $4/3$, close enough that most ears do not notice the mismatch.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7*

^rem-a3-4-2

> [!remark] Remark: Beats in a driven oscillator
> Beats are not special to sound: they appear wherever two nearby frequencies superpose. An undamped oscillator driven near its natural frequency $\omega_0$ and started from rest keeps its free oscillation at $\omega_0$ alongside the response at the drive frequency $\omega$, so it beats forever, with beat period $2\pi/\lvert\omega_0 - \omega\rvert$ ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^rem-a1-6-5|Remark: Driving without damping, and beats]]). For $\ddot y + 11y = \cos 3t$ the envelope returns every $2\pi/(\sqrt{11} - 3) = 19.8\ \mathrm s$ ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^ex-a1-6-2|Example §A1.6.2]]). With damping, the free oscillation dies out and the beats with it, leaving the steady state ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance#^thm-a1-6-1|Theorem §A1.6.1]]).
>
> *Source: PHY 287, Final practice · PHY 287, week 11 slides*

^rem-a3-4-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.4.1: Two Nearby Pure Tones
> Pure tones of $250\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $270\ \mathrm{Hz}$ are played together. By [[§A3.4 Beats#^thm-a3-4-1|Theorem §A3.4.1]],
>
> $$
> \cos(2\pi\cdot 270\ \mathrm{Hz}\,t) + \cos(2\pi\cdot 250\ \mathrm{Hz}\,t) = 2\cos(2\pi\cdot 10\ \mathrm{Hz}\,t)\,\cos(2\pi\cdot 260\ \mathrm{Hz}\,t) .
> $$
>
> One hears a $260\ \mathrm{Hz}$ tone beating at $f_{\text{beat}} = 20\ \mathrm{Hz}$, an unpleasant rattle. Tuning the second tone to $252\ \mathrm{Hz}$ slows the beats to $2$ per second; at $250\ \mathrm{Hz}$ they vanish.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7*

^ex-a3-4-1

> [!example] Example §A3.4.2: Beating Harmonics
> (a) A pure $300\ \mathrm{Hz}$ tone and a pure $580\ \mathrm{Hz}$ tone: the formula gives $\lvert f_2 - f_1\rvert = 280\ \mathrm{Hz}$, but that is far too fast to be heard as a throb; one hears two notes. (b) Play the $580\ \mathrm{Hz}$ tone against a plucked string of fundamental $300\ \mathrm{Hz}$ instead. The string also sounds its harmonics $600, 900, \ldots\ \mathrm{Hz}$, with amplitudes falling off roughly as $1/n$ (so the $600\ \mathrm{Hz}$ harmonic has half the fundamental's amplitude). The $580\ \mathrm{Hz}$ tone beats against the $600\ \mathrm{Hz}$ harmonic at $20\ \mathrm{Hz}$: the pair sounds harsh. (c) Tuning forks of $256$ and $512\ \mathrm{Hz}$ give, by the formula, $256\ \mathrm{Hz}$; again this is not an audible beat but an octave, and every harmonic of the $512\ \mathrm{Hz}$ note coincides with an even harmonic of the $256\ \mathrm{Hz}$ one, so the pair is consonant.
>
> The formula $f_{\text{beat}} = \lvert f_2 - f_1\rvert$ is always correct arithmetic; whether one *hears* beats depends on the difference being small, and between real instruments the relevant differences are often those between harmonics.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7 · UP1 Example 17.7*
