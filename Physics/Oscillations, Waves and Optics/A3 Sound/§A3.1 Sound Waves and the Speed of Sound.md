Chapter A2 treated mechanical waves in general and the string in detail. Sound is the mechanical wave of a fluid or a solid: a longitudinal disturbance that can be described either by how far the matter is displaced or by how much the pressure changes. This section relates the two descriptions and derives the speed of sound from the stiffness and the density of the medium, as $v = \sqrt{F_T/\mu}$ came from the tension and the mass density of a string ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]).

## Sound as a pressure wave

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.1: Sound; Infrasound and Ultrasound
> **Sound** is a disturbance of matter transmitted outward from its source; **hearing** is its perception. Regions where the medium is squeezed are **compressions** (pressure above ambient), regions where it is thinned are **rarefactions** (pressure below ambient). In a fluid, which has almost no shear strength, sound waves are longitudinal ([[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^def-a2-1-2|Def. §A2.1.2]]); in a solid they can be longitudinal or transverse.
>
> The human ear hears roughly $20\ \mathrm{Hz}$ to $20\ \mathrm{kHz}$ ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^law-a3-2-3|Law §A3.2.3]]). Sound below that range is **infrasound**, above it **ultrasound**.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.1, chapter introduction*

^def-a3-1-1

> [!definition] Definition §A3.1.2: Displacement Wave and Pressure Wave
> Let a sinusoidal sound wave travel along $x$. The **displacement** $s(x,t)$ of the layer of the medium whose equilibrium position is $x$, and the **pressure fluctuation** $\Delta p(x,t)$, the gauge pressure relative to the undisturbed pressure ([[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^def-a9-1-4|CM Def. §A9.1.4]]), are
>
> $$
> s(x,t) = s_{\max}\cos(kx \mp \omega t + \phi), \qquad \Delta p(x,t) = \Delta p_{\max}\sin(kx \mp \omega t + \phi) .
> $$
>
> $s_{\max}$ is the **displacement amplitude** and $\Delta p_{\max}$ the **pressure amplitude**. As for any sinusoidal wave ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-2|Theorem §A2.2.2]]), $k = 2\pi/\lambda$, $\omega = 2\pi f$, $v = \omega/k = f\lambda$; the upper sign is a wave moving toward $+x$, the lower sign one moving toward $-x$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.1, eqs. (17.1)–(17.2)*

^def-a3-1-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.1: Pressure Wave from Displacement Wave
> In a medium of bulk modulus $B$ ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-3|CM Def. §A7.2.3]]), small displacements produce the pressure fluctuation
>
> $$
> \Delta p(x,t) = -B\,\frac{\partial s(x,t)}{\partial x} .
> $$
>
> For the sinusoidal wave of [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^def-a3-1-2|Def. §A3.1.2]] this gives $\Delta p_{\max} = Bk\,s_{\max}$: the pressure wave is a quarter cycle out of phase with the displacement wave. Pressure is highest or lowest where the layers are at their equilibrium positions, and is ambient where the displacement is largest.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.3*

^thm-a3-1-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Follow a slab of the medium of cross-section $A$ whose faces sit at $x$ and $x + \Delta x$ at equilibrium, so its volume is $V = A\,\Delta x$. The wave moves the faces to $x + s(x,t)$ and $x + \Delta x + s(x + \Delta x, t)$, so the volume changes by $\Delta V = A\,[s(x+\Delta x,t) - s(x,t)]$ and
>
> $$
> \frac{\Delta V}{V} = \frac{s(x+\Delta x,t) - s(x,t)}{\Delta x} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{\partial s}{\partial x} \qquad (\Delta x \to 0) .
> $$
>
> By the definition of the bulk modulus, a fractional volume change $\Delta V/V$ comes with the pressure change $\Delta p = -B\,\Delta V/V$, hence $\Delta p = -B\,\partial s/\partial x$. For $s = s_{\max}\cos(kx \mp \omega t + \phi)$, $\partial s/\partial x = -k s_{\max}\sin(kx \mp \omega t + \phi)$, so $\Delta p = Bk s_{\max}\sin(kx \mp \omega t + \phi)$, with either sign. Sine and cosine of the same argument are a quarter cycle apart: where $\lvert s\rvert$ is largest, $\Delta p = 0$, and conversely.

> [!remark] Remark: Which way the air moves at a compression
> The velocity of the medium (not of the wave) is $u = \partial s/\partial t$, as for the string ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^thm-a2-2-3|Theorem §A2.2.3]]). For a wave toward $+x$, $u = \omega s_{\max}\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)$, which is in phase with $\Delta p$: using $\Delta p_{\max} = Bk s_{\max}$ and $B = \rho v^2$ ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]] below), $u = \Delta p/(\rho v)$. So in a compression the air moves forward, along the direction of propagation, and in a rarefaction it moves backward; for a wave toward $-x$, $u = -\Delta p/(\rho v)$, again along the propagation in a compression. In the wave toward $+x$, at a fixed point the pressure, like the velocity of the air, peaks a quarter period *before* the displacement does. (Schwartz says that the density "lags behind" the displacement by $90^\circ$; he counts phase in the argument $kx - \omega t$, which decreases with time, so in time the density leads.)
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 6*

^rem-a3-1-1

> [!caution] Caution: Symbols
> - $\Delta p$ is the *change* from the ambient pressure, not the absolute pressure. For ordinary sounds it is tiny compared with $1\ \mathrm{atm}$ ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^rem-a3-2-3|Remark: How small the threshold is]]).
> - University Physics writes the bulk modulus as $\beta$ in its derivations (§17.3, p. 870–871), the same letter it uses for the sound intensity level in decibels. These notes keep $B$ for the bulk modulus, as in Classical Mechanics.
> - $s$ is a displacement *along* the direction of propagation. Drawing $s(x)$ as a curve, as in the figure, is a graph, not a picture of the wave.

## The speed of sound

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.2: Speed of Sound in a Fluid
> Small-amplitude displacements in a fluid of equilibrium density $\rho$ and bulk modulus $B$ obey the linear wave equation ([[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-4|Def. §A2.2.4]])
>
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2 s}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{v^2}\,\frac{\partial^2 s}{\partial t^2}, \qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{B}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{dp}{d\rho}} .
> $$
>
> The speed is a property of the medium alone: elastic property over inertial property.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.2, eq. (17.4) and derivation · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 6*

^thm-a3-1-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Apply Newton's second law to the slab of the previous derivation, of mass $\rho A\,\Delta x$. The fluid on its left pushes it toward $+x$ with force $[p_0 + \Delta p(x)]A$, the fluid on its right pushes it back with $[p_0 + \Delta p(x + \Delta x)]A$. The ambient pressure $p_0$ cancels, and with [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]]
>
> $$
> \rho A\,\Delta x\,\frac{\partial^2 s}{\partial t^2} = -A\,\bigl[\Delta p(x + \Delta x) - \Delta p(x)\bigr] \approx -A\,\Delta x\,\frac{\partial \Delta p}{\partial x} = AB\,\Delta x\,\frac{\partial^2 s}{\partial x^2} .
> $$
>
> Dividing by $\rho A\,\Delta x$ gives the wave equation with $v^2 = B/\rho$. The mass $\rho A\,\Delta x$ is that of the undisturbed slab, which is accurate for small amplitudes.
>
> For a fixed mass of fluid $V \propto 1/\rho$, so $dV/V = -d\rho/\rho$ and $B = -dp/(dV/V) = \rho\,dp/d\rho$; hence also $v^2 = dp/d\rho$. University Physics reaches this form directly: in the frame of a moving wave front, fluid enters a thin layer with speed $v$ and leaves with $v + dv$; continuity gives $\rho\,dv = -v\,d\rho$, and Newton's second law for the fluid crossing the layer gives $\rho v\,dv = -dp$. Eliminating $dv$, $v^2 = dp/d\rho$.

^der-a3-1-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A9.4 Flow and Continuity#^thm-a9-4-2|CM Theorem §A9.4.2]], [[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-3|CM Def. §A7.2.3]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-4|Def. §A2.2.4]], [[§5 Partial Derivatives#^def-5-1|452 Def. §5.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.3: Longitudinal Waves in a Thin Rod
> Longitudinal displacements of a thin solid rod of Young's modulus $Y$ ([[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-2|CM Def. §A7.2.2]]) and density $\rho$ travel with speed
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{\frac{Y}{\rho}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.2, eq. (17.5) · PHY 300, Worksheet 6*

^thm-a3-1-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> As in [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]], with tensile stress in place of pressure. The strain of the rod at $x$ is $\partial s/\partial x$, so the cross-section at $x$ carries the tension $F = YA\,\partial s/\partial x$, pulling on the part to its left toward $+x$ and on the part to its right toward $-x$. The net force on the slab between $x$ and $x + \Delta x$ is $F(x + \Delta x) - F(x) \approx YA\,\Delta x\,\partial^2 s/\partial x^2$, and Newton's second law gives $\rho\,\partial^2 s/\partial t^2 = Y\,\partial^2 s/\partial x^2$.

^der-a3-1-3

*Uses:* [[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^def-a7-2-2|CM Def. §A7.2.2]], [[§A3.3 Newton's Second Law#^pr-a3-3-1|CM Principle §A3.3.1]], [[§A2.2 Mathematics of Waves#^def-a2-2-4|Def. §A2.2.4]]

> [!remark] Remark: Stiff and light means fast
> Every result here has the form $v = \sqrt{\text{elastic property}/\text{inertial property}}$, like $v = \sqrt{F_T/\mu}$ on a string ([[§A2.3 Wave Speed on a Stretched String#^thm-a2-3-2|Theorem §A2.3.2]]) and like $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ for a mass on a spring: a stiffer medium pushes back harder, a denser one responds more sluggishly. Air is easily compressed, so sound in air is slow; liquids and solids are hard to compress, so sound in them is fast. The rod formula assumes a thin rod, with the same stress across each cross-section. In an extended solid, longitudinal waves compress the material while transverse waves shear it; the shear modulus is the smaller (for granite, [[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^rem-a7-2-1|CM Remark: Some values]]), so transverse waves are slower. That is why an earthquake's longitudinal P-waves ($4$–$7\ \mathrm{km/s}$) outrun its transverse S-waves ($2$–$5\ \mathrm{km/s}$), and the delay between them gives the distance to the source. S-waves do not cross Earth's liquid outer core, since a liquid has no shear stiffness.

^rem-a3-1-2

## Sound in a gas

> [!model] Model §A3.1.4: Sound in an Ideal Gas as an Adiabatic Process
> In a sound wave in a gas, each small parcel of gas is treated as
> - an ideal gas ([[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^mod-a2-1-2|TH Model §A2.1.2]]);
> - compressed and expanded **adiabatically**: compressions and rarefactions are half a wavelength apart, too far for appreciable heat to flow between them during one period;
> - **quasi-statically**, staying close to equilibrium, because the pressure changes are small ($\Delta p_{\max} \ll p$).
>
> Each parcel then follows a quasi-static adiabat, $pV^\gamma =$ const ([[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|TH Theorem §A3.6.2]]). The small heat flows that do occur, and viscosity, slowly convert the organised motion into thermal motion: they damp the wave.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.1–17.2*

^mod-a3-1-4

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.1.5: Speed of Sound in an Ideal Gas
> In an ideal gas at pressure $p$, density $\rho$ and absolute temperature $T$, with ratio of heat capacities $\gamma$ ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^def-a3-5-2|TH Def. §A3.5.2]]) and molar mass $M$,
>
> $$
> v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma p}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma R T}{M}} .
> $$
>
> For air ($\gamma = 1.40$, $M = 0.02897\ \mathrm{kg/mol}$),
>
> $$
> v = (331\ \mathrm{m/s})\sqrt{\frac{T}{273\ \mathrm K}} = (331\ \mathrm{m/s})\sqrt{1 + \frac{T_C}{273\ ^\circ\mathrm C}} ,
> $$
>
> with $T$ in kelvins and $T_C$ in degrees Celsius: $331\ \mathrm{m/s}$ at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, $343\ \mathrm{m/s}$ at $20\ ^\circ\mathrm C$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.2, eqs. (17.6)–(17.7) and derivation*

^thm-a3-1-5

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Along the adiabat of [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^mod-a3-1-4|Model §A3.1.4]], $pV^\gamma = K$. Differentiating, $V^\gamma\,dp + \gamma p V^{\gamma - 1}\,dV = 0$, so $dp = -\gamma p\,dV/V$: the **adiabatic bulk modulus** is
>
> $$
> B = -\frac{dp}{dV/V} = \gamma p .
> $$
>
> [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]] gives $v = \sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}$. A mass $m$ of gas is $n = m/M$ moles, so the ideal gas law $pV = nRT$ reads $p = \rho RT/M$, and $v = \sqrt{\gamma RT/M}$. For air, $\sqrt{(1.40)(8.314\ \mathrm{J/(mol\cdot K)})(273\ \mathrm K)/(0.02897\ \mathrm{kg/mol})} = 331\ \mathrm{m/s}$; since $v \propto \sqrt T$, $v = (331\ \mathrm{m/s})\sqrt{T/273\ \mathrm K}$, and $T = 273\ \mathrm K + T_C$ gives the Celsius form.

^der-a3-1-5

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^mod-a3-1-4|Model §A3.1.4]], [[§A3.6 Adiabatic Processes#^thm-a3-6-2|TH Theorem §A3.6.2]], [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^law-a2-1-1|TH Law §A2.1.1]], [[§A2.1 The Ideal Gas#^def-a2-1-1|TH Def. §A2.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: The isothermal value, and the molecules that carry the sound
> - If the gas stayed at constant temperature, the bulk modulus would be the isothermal one, $p$ instead of $\gamma p$, and the speed $\sqrt{p/\rho} = (331\ \mathrm{m/s})/\sqrt{1.40} = 280\ \mathrm{m/s}$ at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$, about 15% below the measured value. The measured speed confirms that sound is adiabatic. In the language of entropy, the relevant derivative is $(\partial p/\partial\rho)$ at constant entropy, since a reversible adiabatic process is isentropic ([[§A4.5 Entropy#^thm-a4-5-4|TH Theorem §A4.5.4]]).
> - Comparing with the rms speed $v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3RT/M}$ of the molecules ([[§A2.2 Pressure, Temperature and RMS Speed#^thm-a2-2-4|TH Theorem §A2.2.4]]): $v = \sqrt{\gamma/3}\;v_{\text{rms}}$, which is $0.68\,v_{\text{rms}}$ for air ($\gamma = 1.40$) and $0.75\,v_{\text{rms}}$ for a monatomic gas ($\gamma = \tfrac53$, [[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-3|TH Theorem §A3.5.3]]). Sound is a coherent disturbance passed from molecule to molecule, so it is somewhat slower than the random molecular motion that transmits it, and like that motion it grows as $\sqrt{T/M}$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 6*

^rem-a3-1-3

> [!caution] Caution: Molar mass and kelvins
> In $v = \sqrt{\gamma RT/M}$, $M$ is the **molar mass** in kg/mol (for air $0.02897\ \mathrm{kg/mol}$, not $28.97$), and $T$ is the absolute temperature. University Physics (p. 862) calls $M$ the "molecular mass"; the formula needs the molar mass. With the mass $m$ of one molecule instead, $v = \sqrt{\gamma k_B T/m}$.

## Frequency, wavelength and medium

> [!remark] Remark: One speed for all frequencies
> In air, sound of every audible frequency travels at the same speed: music from a distant band arrives in time, low notes no later than high ones. So in a given medium $f\lambda = v$ is fixed, and low frequencies have long wavelengths (a woofer is large, a tweeter small). When sound passes into another medium, the frequency stays that of the source, and the wavelength changes with the speed: $\lambda = v/f$.

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.1.1: Wavelengths at the Edges of Hearing
> Find the wavelengths of $20\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $20\ \mathrm{kHz}$ sound in air at $30.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$. By [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-5|Theorem §A3.1.5]],
>
> $$
> v = (331\ \mathrm{m/s})\sqrt{\frac{303\ \mathrm K}{273\ \mathrm K}} = 348.7\ \mathrm{m/s}, \qquad \lambda_{\max} = \frac{v}{20\ \mathrm{Hz}} = 17\ \mathrm m, \qquad \lambda_{\min} = \frac{v}{20\,000\ \mathrm{Hz}} = 1.7\ \mathrm{cm} .
> $$
>
> Because $f\lambda$ is fixed, audible sound spans a factor of a thousand in wavelength, from the size of a house to the width of a finger.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 17.1*

^ex-a3-1-1

> [!example] Example §A3.1.2: Three Media, Three Formulas
> Check the three speed formulas against measured values.
> - *Water* ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]]): $B = 2.2 \times 10^9\ \mathrm{Pa}$, $\rho = 1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, so $v = \sqrt{2.2\times10^9/1000}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 1.48 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{m/s}$, matching $1480\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
> - *Helium at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$* ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-5|Theorem §A3.1.5]]): monatomic, so $\gamma = \tfrac53$; $M = 4.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg/mol}$. Then $v = \sqrt{(5/3)(8.314)(273)/(4.00\times10^{-3})}\ \mathrm{m/s} = 972\ \mathrm{m/s}$, within 1% of the measured $965\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Relative to air, $\sqrt{(\gamma_{\text{He}}/\gamma_{\text{air}})(M_{\text{air}}/M_{\text{He}})} = 2.9$: the light molecules make the difference.
> - *Aluminium rod* ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-3|Theorem §A3.1.3]]): $Y = 7.0 \times 10^{10}\ \mathrm{Pa}$, $\rho = 2700\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, so $v = 5.1 \times 10^3\ \mathrm{m/s}$, against the accepted $5082\ \mathrm{m/s}$ quoted for the rods used in PHY 300.
>
> The moduli and densities are the rounded values of [[§A7.2 Stress, Strain and Elastic Moduli#^rem-a7-2-1|CM Remark: Some values]] and [[§A9.1 Density and Pressure#^rem-a9-1-1|CM Remark: Typical densities]], so agreement to a percent is all one can ask.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.2, Table 17.1 · PHY 300, Worksheet 6*

^ex-a3-1-2

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The pressure–displacement relation and the wave equation use partial derivatives of $s(x,t)$ ([[§5 Partial Derivatives#^def-5-1|452 Def. §5.1]]). In three dimensions the same argument gives the wave equation for $\Delta p$ with the Laplacian; sound in three dimensions and acoustic impedance are level B ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-b7-1-1|Def. §B7.1.1]]; [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^def-b5-2-2|Def. §B5.2.2]], [[§B5.2 Impedance, Reflection and Transmission#^cau-b5-2-2|Caution: Pipes]]).
> - Level B: the fluid, the rod and the string as cases of one linear continuum, with one derivation of the wave equation and of the speed — [[§B5.1 Waves in Strings, Fluids and Solids#^thm-b5-1-2|Theorem §B5.1.2]].
> - The speed of sound links waves to thermodynamics: it measures $\gamma$, hence the active degrees of freedom of the gas molecules ([[§A3.5 Heat Capacities of an Ideal Gas#^thm-a3-5-3|TH Theorem §A3.5.3]]).
