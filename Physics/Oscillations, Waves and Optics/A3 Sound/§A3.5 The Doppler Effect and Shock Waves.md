So far source, listener and air were at rest. When the source or the listener moves through the air, the frequency heard differs from the one emitted: the siren of a passing ambulance drops in pitch. This section derives the shift from the wave fronts, and then follows the source up to and beyond the speed of sound, where the wave fronts pile up into a shock wave.

## The Doppler effect

> [!definition] Definition §A3.5.1: Doppler Effect; Doppler Shift
> The **Doppler effect** is the change of the observed frequency of a wave caused by motion of the source or of the observer; the difference between the observed frequency $f_o$ and the source frequency $f_s$ is the **Doppler shift**. Below, $v$ is the speed of sound, $v_s$ and $v_o$ are the speeds of source and observer relative to the air, and the motion is along the line joining them.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.7*

^def-a3-5-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.1: Moving Source, Stationary Observer
> A source of frequency $f_s$ moving with speed $v_s < v$ through still air is heard by a stationary observer at
>
> $$
> f_o = f_s\,\frac{v}{v \mp v_s} ,
> $$
>
> with the upper sign when the source approaches and the lower sign when it recedes. The wavelength in front of the source is $(v - v_s)/f_s$, behind it $(v + v_s)/f_s$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.7, eq. (17.18)*

^thm-a3-5-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Each crest spreads from the point where it was emitted at speed $v$, whatever the source does afterwards. Between two crests, one period $T_s = 1/f_s$ apart, the first crest travels $vT_s$ while the source moves $v_sT_s$ in the same direction (in front) or the opposite one (behind). So successive crests are separated by $\lambda_o = (v \mp v_s)T_s$. They pass the stationary observer at speed $v$, so the observed frequency is $f_o = v/\lambda_o = f_s\,v/(v \mp v_s)$.

^der-a3-5-1

*Uses:* [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|Theorem §A2.1.1]], [[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]]

![[ph-wo-a3-5-1.svg]]
*Crests (blue circles) emitted once per period by a source (red) moving to the right at half the speed of sound; each circle is centred on the blue dot where the source was when it emitted that crest. In front of the source the crests are bunched, $(v - v_s)T$ apart; behind it they are spread out, $(v + v_s)T$ apart. Both observers receive crests at the speed $v$, so the one ahead hears a higher frequency.*

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.2: Moving Observer, Stationary Source
> An observer moving with speed $v_o$ through still air hears a stationary source of frequency $f_s$ at
>
> $$
> f_o = f_s\,\frac{v \pm v_o}{v} ,
> $$
>
> with the upper sign when the observer approaches the source and the lower sign when the observer recedes from it ($v_o < v$).
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.7, eq. (17.19)*

^thm-a3-5-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The source is at rest, so the crests in the air are a wavelength $\lambda_s = v/f_s$ apart. Relative to the observer they approach at speed $v + v_o$ (observer moving toward the source) or $v - v_o$ (moving away). The observer therefore meets $(v \pm v_o)/\lambda_s$ crests per second: $f_o = f_s\,(v \pm v_o)/v$.

^der-a3-5-2

*Uses:* [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves#^thm-a2-1-1|Theorem §A2.1.1]], [[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^def-a3-5-1|Def. §A3.5.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.3: Doppler Formula for Sound
> When both move along the line joining them, with speeds $v_s, v_o < v$ relative to still air,
>
> $$
> f_o = f_s\,\frac{v \pm v_o}{v \mp v_s} ,
> $$
>
> where the upper signs are for motion *toward* the other and the lower signs for motion *away*. Motion toward each other raises the frequency, motion apart lowers it.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.7, eq. (17.20), Table 17.4 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 21*

^thm-a3-5-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> The two effects act at different stages, so they combine. The moving source fixes the spacing of the crests in the air on the observer's side, $\lambda = (v \mp v_s)/f_s$ ([[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]). The moving observer meets these crests at the relative speed $v \pm v_o$ ([[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]). Hence $f_o = (v \pm v_o)/\lambda = f_s(v \pm v_o)/(v \mp v_s)$. Setting $v_o = 0$ or $v_s = 0$ recovers the two special cases.

^der-a3-5-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]], [[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]]

> [!caution] Caution: Signs, and the role of the air
> - Pick the signs physically, not by formula: approach raises the pitch, recession lowers it. Numerator: observer's motion ($+$ toward); denominator: source's motion ($-$ toward).
> - The shift is not a function of the relative velocity alone. A source approaching at $35\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and an observer approaching at $35\ \mathrm{m/s}$ give different frequencies ([[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^ex-a3-5-1|Example §A3.5.1]]), because sound travels relative to the air, which defines a preferred frame. If source and observer move together, $v_o = v_s$ in the same direction, there is no shift at all; neither is there for two people talking in a steady wind.
> - For a source passing at constant velocity, the heard frequency is constant and high while it is far ahead, constant and low when it is far behind; it slides between the two while the source passes, the faster the closer the source passes.

^cau-a3-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Motion at an angle
> If the source moves at an angle $\theta$ to the line toward a stationary observer, only the component of its velocity along that line matters:
>
> $$
> f_o = f_s\,\frac{v}{v - v_s\cos\theta} ,
> $$
>
> which gives the approaching case for $\theta = 0$, the receding case for $\theta = 180^\circ$, and no shift for $\theta = 90^\circ$. This is the smooth slide of the siren as a car passes. The angle is the one at the moment the sound now heard was *emitted*: what the source does while the sound is on its way cannot matter. So the sound whose pitch changes fastest is emitted slightly *before* the car reaches its closest point, about $(v_s/v)\,d$ before it for a road a distance $d$ away; it is still in transit, and arrives at about the moment the car is closest to you.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 21*

^rem-a3-5-1

> [!remark] Remark: Small speeds, light, and uses
> - For $v_s, v_o \ll v$, expanding $1/(1 \mp v_s/v) \approx 1 \pm v_s/v$ ([[§31 Taylor's Theorem#^thm-31-2|451 Thm. §31.2]]) gives $f_o \approx f_s\,(1 + u/v)$, where $u$ is the speed at which source and observer approach each other (negative if they separate). To first order only the relative motion matters.
> - Light needs no medium, and its Doppler shift depends only on the relative velocity of source and observer; the exact formula requires the time dilation of special relativity (Relativity, planned). For speeds small compared with $c$ it reduces to $f_o \approx f_s(1 + u/c)$. Light from distant galaxies is shifted to lower frequency and longer wavelength (the **redshift**): they recede from us, faster the farther they are, which is the basis for the estimate of about 14 billion years for the age of the universe.
> - Doppler shifts measure velocities: of blood, by ultrasound reflected from moving blood cells; of cars, by police radar; of rain and storm clouds, by Doppler weather radar; of stars along the line of sight.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.7 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 21*

^rem-a3-5-2

## Shock waves

> [!definition] Definition §A3.5.2: Mach Number
> The **Mach number** of a source moving with speed $v_s$ through a medium in which sound travels at $v$ is
>
> $$
> M = \frac{v_s}{v} .
> $$
>
> A source with $M < 1$ is subsonic, one with $M > 1$ supersonic.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.8, eq. (17.21)*

^def-a3-5-2

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.5.4: Mach Cone
> A source moving through still air at constant speed $v_s > v$ drags behind it a cone, with the source at its apex, on which the wave fronts it has emitted overlap. The half-angle $\theta$ of the cone satisfies
>
> $$
> \sin\theta = \frac{v}{v_s} = \frac{1}{M} .
> $$
>
> The faster the source, the narrower the cone.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.8, eq. (17.22)*

^thm-a3-5-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> A wave front emitted a time $t$ ago is now a sphere of radius $vt$ centred on the point where it was emitted, which the source has meanwhile left a distance $v_st > vt$ behind. All these spheres lie inside the cone with apex at the source that touches them. Its tangent line from the apex to the sphere of age $t$ is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact, so the right triangle formed by the apex, the centre and the point of contact has hypotenuse $v_st$ and the side opposite $\theta$ equal to $vt$: $\sin\theta = vt/(v_st) = v/v_s$. The ratio does not depend on $t$, so the spheres of all ages touch the same cone, and their crests add up along it.

^der-a3-5-4

*Uses:* [[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^def-a3-5-2|Def. §A3.5.2]]

![[ph-wo-a3-5-2.svg]]
*Wave fronts (blue) emitted by a source (red) moving at $M = 2$. Each front is still centred where it was emitted, and the source has outrun them all. The fronts touch the red lines of the Mach cone, of half-angle $\theta = 30^\circ$; the dashed right triangle for the oldest front shows $\sin\theta = vt/v_st = 1/M$.*

> [!definition] Definition §A3.5.3: Shock Wave; Sonic Boom; Bow Wake
> The large pressure disturbance on the Mach cone, where the crests of all the wave fronts arrive together, is a **shock wave**. Inside the cone the interference is mostly destructive, and the sound is much weaker. A **sonic boom** is the loud noise heard as the shock wave of a supersonic aircraft sweeps along the ground. In general, a source moving faster than its waves propagate leaves a V-shaped or conical **bow wake**, like that of a boat or a duck on water.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.8*

^def-a3-5-3

> [!caution] Caution: The boom is not a one-time event
> A sonic boom is not produced once, at the moment the aircraft "breaks the sound barrier". The cone travels with the aircraft for as long as it is supersonic, and every point on the ground hears the boom when the cone passes over it, after the aircraft has gone by (which is why observers often do not see the plane that made it). An aircraft carries two cones, from its nose and its tail, so two booms are heard, separated by the time it takes the aircraft to pass a point. A cracking whip is a small sonic boom.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.8 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 21*

^cau-a3-5-2

> [!remark] Remark: Approaching and passing the speed of sound
> - As $v_s \to v$, [[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]] gives $f_o \to \infty$ in front: the crests pile up at the nose of the source, which moves as fast as they do, and reach an observer all at once. For $v_s > v$ the formula gives a negative frequency, which has no meaning: no sound reaches an observer ahead before the source has passed, and then the sounds of approach and recession arrive mixed. The formula's breakdown signals the shock wave.
> - The bow wake is not limited to sound. A charged particle crossing water faster than light travels in water (about $0.75c$) emits light on a cone whose angle gives the particle's speed: **Cherenkov radiation**, the blue glow in the pool of a research reactor. The speed of light in a medium, $c/n$: [[§A4.1 The Propagation of Light#^def-a4-1-1|Def. §A4.1.1]].

^rem-a3-5-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.5.1: A Passing Train
> A train with a $150\ \mathrm{Hz}$ horn moves at $35.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ through still air; $v = 340\ \mathrm{m/s}$.
>
> (a) *A person beside the track* ([[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^thm-a3-5-1|Theorem §A3.5.1]]):
>
> $$
> f_{\text{approach}} = (150\ \mathrm{Hz})\,\frac{340}{340 - 35.0} = 167\ \mathrm{Hz}, \qquad f_{\text{recede}} = (150\ \mathrm{Hz})\,\frac{340}{340 + 35.0} = 136\ \mathrm{Hz} .
> $$
>
> The shifts, $+17\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $-14\ \mathrm{Hz}$, are not symmetric.
>
> (b) *The engineer on the train* moves with the horn: $v_o = v_s = 35.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in the same direction. In [[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^thm-a3-5-3|Theorem §A3.5.3]] the two factors cancel and $f_o = f_s = 150\ \mathrm{Hz}$, as experience says: there is no shift between driver and passenger.
>
> (c) *Now reverse the roles*: a car drives at $35.0\ \mathrm{m/s}$ past a stationary $150\ \mathrm{Hz}$ horn. By [[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^thm-a3-5-2|Theorem §A3.5.2]] the driver hears $150 \times 375/340 = 165\ \mathrm{Hz}$ approaching and $150 \times 305/340 = 135\ \mathrm{Hz}$ receding. The relative motion is the same as in (a), the frequencies are not: what counts is motion relative to the air.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 17.8*

^ex-a3-5-1

> [!example] Example §A3.5.2: The Cone of the SR-71
> In 1976 an SR-71 Blackbird flew at $3529.6\ \mathrm{km/h} = 980.4\ \mathrm{m/s}$, Mach 2.85 (which implies $v = 980.4/2.85 = 344\ \mathrm{m/s}$). By [[§A3.5 The Doppler Effect and Shock Waves#^thm-a3-5-4|Theorem §A3.5.4]] the half-angle of its Mach cone was
>
> $$
> \theta = \arcsin\frac{1}{2.85} = 20.5^\circ .
> $$
>
> If the plane flies level at height $h$ through still air with this speed of sound throughout, the cone reaches a ground observer directly below the flight path only when the plane is a horizontal distance $h/\tan\theta = 2.67\,h$ beyond the overhead point: the boom comes long after the plane has passed.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.8*

^ex-a3-5-2
