Sound waves superpose like all waves in a linear medium ([[§A2.5 Interference of Waves|§A2.5]]). Two consequences matter for sound: waves from two sources reinforce or cancel according to their path difference, and waves bouncing back and forth in a pipe build up standing waves at selected frequencies, as on a string ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance|§A2.6]]). The new ingredient is the boundary condition at the end of an air column, which decides which frequencies a wind instrument can play.

## Interference of sound

> [!remark] Remark: Path difference
> Two speakers driven in phase by the same signal send out waves of equal frequency. At a point at distances $r_1$ and $r_2$ from them, the waves arrive with the phase difference $2\pi\,\Delta r/\lambda$, where $\Delta r = \lvert r_2 - r_1\rvert$. The interference is constructive where $\Delta r = n\lambda$ ($n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$) and destructive where $\Delta r = (n + \tfrac12)\lambda$, and the loud and quiet spots can be found with a sound-level meter. The general treatment of two coherent sources, with the pattern of maxima and minima, is in [[§A6.1 Young's Double-Slit Interference|§A6.1]]; it is the same mathematics that Young used to show that light is a wave. In a room, reflections from the walls and the mixture of frequencies in music wash the pattern out, which is why one does not normally notice it.

^rem-a3-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Noise cancellation
> Pressures from two sources add as signed numbers. Active noise-cancelling headphones analyse the incoming noise electronically and play a second sound $180^\circ$ out of phase with it, so the positive and negative gauge pressures largely cancel. Complete cancellation is possible only under the simplest conditions, but reductions of $30\ \mathrm{dB}$ or more are achieved, better than passive ear protection.

^rem-a3-3-2

## Standing waves in air columns

> [!model] Model §A3.3.1: Ideal Air Column
> A uniform tube of length $L$ holds air in which sound travels at speed $v$. Assume:
> - the tube is narrow compared with the wavelength, so the waves are plane and travel along its axis, with displacement $s(x,t)$;
> - at a **closed end** the rigid wall stops the air: the displacement vanishes there (displacement node), so the pressure fluctuation is largest (pressure antinode);
> - at an **open end** the air meets the atmosphere, which holds the pressure at its ambient value: $\Delta p = 0$ (pressure node), so by [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]] $\partial s/\partial x = 0$ and the displacement is largest (displacement antinode);
> - the end correction (below) and losses are neglected.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.4–17.5 · Schwartz 15c, Lecture 6*

^mod-a3-3-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.2: Normal Modes of a Pipe Open at Both Ends
> An ideal air column open at both ends resonates at
>
> $$
> \lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \qquad f_n = n\,\frac{v}{2L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
> $$
>
> All integer multiples of the fundamental $f_1 = v/2L$ occur. The same frequencies hold for a string fixed at both ends, with $v = \sqrt{F_T/\mu}$ ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance|§A2.6]]), and for a pipe closed at both ends.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.4, eqs. (17.15)–(17.16)*

^thm-a3-3-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Two equal sinusoidal waves travelling in opposite directions superpose to a standing wave ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance|§A2.6]]); with the origin of $x$ placed anywhere, its general form is $s(x,t) = S\cos(kx + \alpha)\cos\omega t$. An open end at $x = 0$ needs $\partial s/\partial x = -kS\sin\alpha\,\cos\omega t = 0$ at all times, so $\alpha = 0$ (or $\pi$, the same mode): $s = S\cos kx\,\cos\omega t$. An open end at $x = L$ needs $\partial s/\partial x = -kS\sin kL\,\cos\omega t = 0$, so $kL = n\pi$ with $n = 1, 2, \ldots$ ($n = 0$ is no wave). Then $\lambda = 2\pi/k = 2L/n$ and $f = v/\lambda = nv/2L$. Closed ends at both $x = 0$ and $x = L$ need $s = 0$ there instead, which gives $s = S\sin kx\,\cos\omega t$ with $\sin kL = 0$: the same $k$, the same frequencies.

^der-a3-3-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^mod-a3-3-1|Model §A3.3.1]], [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance|§A2.6]], [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves|§A2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.3.3: Normal Modes of a Pipe Closed at One End
> An ideal air column closed at one end and open at the other resonates at
>
> $$
> \lambda_n = \frac{4L}{n}, \qquad f_n = n\,\frac{v}{4L}, \qquad n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots
> $$
>
> Its fundamental $f_1 = v/4L$ is half that of an open pipe of the same length, and only the odd harmonics $f_1, 3f_1, 5f_1, \ldots$ occur.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.4, eqs. (17.13)–(17.14)*

^thm-a3-3-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Put the closed end at $x = 0$. There $s = 0$ at all times, so the standing wave is $s = S\sin kx\,\cos\omega t$. At the open end $x = L$, $\partial s/\partial x = kS\cos kL\,\cos\omega t = 0$, so $\cos kL = 0$: $kL = \pi/2, 3\pi/2, 5\pi/2, \ldots = n\pi/2$ with $n$ odd. Then $\lambda = 2\pi/k = 4L/n$ and $f = v/\lambda = nv/4L$. In words: the pipe must hold an odd number of quarter wavelengths, node to antinode.

^der-a3-3-3

*Uses:* [[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^mod-a3-3-1|Model §A3.3.1]], [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance|§A2.6]], [[§A2.1 Travelling Waves|§A2.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Harmonics and overtones in pipes
> With the terms of [[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance|§A2.6]]: the lowest resonance is the fundamental, the higher ones are overtones, and the resonant frequencies that are integer multiples of the fundamental are harmonics. An open pipe has all of them: its first overtone is the second harmonic. A closed pipe has only the odd ones: its first overtone is the *third* harmonic, its second overtone the fifth, and its $m$-th overtone is the $(2m+1)$-th harmonic.

^rem-a3-3-3

> [!caution] Caution: Counting harmonics
> University Physics calls the fundamental the *first* harmonic and $2f_1$ the second. Some texts, including Schwartz's lecture notes on music, call $2f_1$ the "first harmonic" (the first one above the fundamental). Check the convention before comparing numbers; in these notes the $n$-th harmonic is $nf_1$.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 7*

^cau-a3-3-1

> [!remark] Remark: Real pipes
> - The antinode at an open end sits slightly outside the tube, because the wave does not spread out fully until it has left the opening. For a thin-walled tube of radius $r$, add an **end correction** of about $0.6\,r$ per open end to $L$.
> - All resonant frequencies are proportional to $v \propto \sqrt T$: an organ in an unheated church plays flat in winter, and wind players warm their instruments to room temperature before tuning.

^rem-a3-3-4

> [!remark] Remark: Longitudinal standing waves in a rod
> A rod carries longitudinal standing waves with speed $\sqrt{Y/\rho}$ ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-3|Theorem §A3.1.3]]). A free end carries no stress, so $\partial s/\partial x = 0$ there: it is a displacement antinode, like an open pipe end. A clamped point, or the point where the rod is held, is (approximately) a node. A rod free at both ends therefore has the open-pipe spectrum $f_n = nv/2L$ with $s \propto \cos(n\pi x/L)$; a rod clamped at one end and free at the other has the closed-pipe spectrum, its second mode holding three quarter wavelengths.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 6*

^rem-a3-3-5

## Musical sound

> [!remark] Remark: What makes an instrument sound like itself
> - A note played on an instrument contains the fundamental, which usually sets the pitch and is the most intense, together with overtones in a mix of intensities. The mix is the timbre ([[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^def-a3-2-2|Def. §A3.2.2]]); it is why middle C on a trumpet and on a clarinet, both roughly pipes closed at one end, sound different.
> - The spectrum depends on the boundary conditions. A flute is open at both ends and has all harmonics; a clarinet behaves like a pipe closed at one end and lacks the even harmonics, which contributes to its hollow sound; much of the shaping of brass instruments serves to restore the even harmonics. Two pipes with the same fundamental, one open and one closed, sound different for the same reason.
> - Players change the resonating length with finger holes (flute, oboe), valves, or a slide (trombone); a bugle has a fixed length and plays only its own resonances. Low instruments need long tubes, which are coiled (tuba). The trombone does not sound its fundamental at all, only overtones.
> - The throat and mouth form an air column closed at one end, driven by the vocal cords; moving the tongue and lips reshapes it and changes which overtones are strong, which is how vowels are formed.
> - In a violin or guitar the string drives the sounding box and the air inside it, which resonate ([[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance|§A1.6]]), greatly amplify the sound and shape its overtones; the marimba uses resonating gourds or pots under its bars in the same way.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.5 · Schwartz 15c, Lectures 6–7*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.3.1: Finding a Quiet Spot
> Two speakers $5.00\ \mathrm m$ apart are driven in phase at one frequency; $v = 343\ \mathrm{m/s}$. A student stands $6.00\ \mathrm m$ out from the line of the speakers, $2.00\ \mathrm m$ to the side of one speaker and so $3.00\ \mathrm m$ to the side of the other, and finds there the first minimum of intensity. The path lengths are
>
> $$
> r_1 = \sqrt{(6.00)^2 + (2.00)^2}\ \mathrm m = 6.325\ \mathrm m, \qquad r_2 = \sqrt{(6.00)^2 + (3.00)^2}\ \mathrm m = 6.708\ \mathrm m ,
> $$
>
> so $\Delta r = 0.383\ \mathrm m$. The first minimum has $\Delta r = \lambda/2$ ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^rem-a3-3-1|Remark: Path difference]]), so $\lambda = 0.767\ \mathrm m$ and $f = v/\lambda = 447\ \mathrm{Hz}$. Path differences are small differences of large numbers: keep enough digits. (University Physics rounds $r_1$ and $r_2$ to $6.32$ and $6.71\ \mathrm m$ first, and gets $440\ \mathrm{Hz}$.)
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 17.5*

^ex-a3-3-1

> [!example] Example §A3.3.2: A 128-Hz Closed Pipe
> (a) What length of pipe closed at one end has the fundamental $128\ \mathrm{Hz}$ (the C below middle C) on a $22.0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ day? (b) What is the frequency of its fourth overtone?
>
> (a) The speed of sound is $v = (331\ \mathrm{m/s})\sqrt{295\ \mathrm K/273\ \mathrm K} = 344\ \mathrm{m/s}$ ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-5|Theorem §A3.1.5]]). By [[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^thm-a3-3-3|Theorem §A3.3.3]] with $n = 1$, $L = v/(4f_1) = (344\ \mathrm{m/s})/(4 \times 128\ \mathrm{Hz}) = 0.672\ \mathrm m$.
>
> (b) The overtones are $n = 3, 5, 7, 9, \ldots$, so the fourth overtone is the ninth harmonic: $f_9 = 9f_1 = 1.15\ \mathrm{kHz}$. An open pipe of the same length would have the fundamental $256\ \mathrm{Hz}$, an octave higher, and its fourth overtone would be $5 \times 256\ \mathrm{Hz} = 1.28\ \mathrm{kHz}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 17.6*

^ex-a3-3-2

> [!example] Example §A3.3.3: The Singing Rod
> An aluminium rod ($v = 5082\ \mathrm{m/s}$ for longitudinal waves) is held at its centre and set ringing; it sounds its lowest mode. Where should it be held to hear the mode $n = 2$, and what is $f_2/f_1$?
>
> Both ends are free, so the modes are $s \propto \cos(n\pi x/L)\cos\omega_n t$ with $f_n = nv/2L$ ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^rem-a3-3-5|Remark: Longitudinal standing waves in a rod]]). Holding the rod forces a node at the grip. At the centre, $\cos(n\pi/2) = 0$ only for odd $n$, so a centre grip allows $n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots$ and the rod sounds its fundamental. The mode $n = 2$, $\cos(2\pi x/L)$, has its nodes at $x = L/4$ and $3L/4$: hold the rod a quarter of its length from one end. Then $f_2/f_1 = 2$, an octave up; for a rod of length $1.00\ \mathrm m$, $f_1 = 2.54\ \mathrm{kHz}$ and $f_2 = 5.08\ \mathrm{kHz}$. The modes are equally spaced, so a plot of the measured $f_n$ against $n$ is a straight line of slope $v/2L$, which measures the speed of sound in the rod.
>
> *Source: PHY 300, Worksheet 6*

^ex-a3-3-3

> [!remark]- Connections
> - The boundary conditions are a fixed (Dirichlet) condition $s = 0$ at a closed end and a free (Neumann) condition $\partial s/\partial x = 0$ at an open or free end; the general theory of normal modes with such conditions, and of how a note decomposes into its harmonics (Fourier series), is level B (planned).
> - Driving an air column near one of its normal frequencies is a case of resonance of a driven oscillator: [[§A1.6 Forced Oscillations and Resonance|§A1.6]].
