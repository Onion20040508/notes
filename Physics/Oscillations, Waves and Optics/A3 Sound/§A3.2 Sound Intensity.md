How much energy does a sound wave carry, and how loud is it? The intensity of a wave and its inverse-square fall-off from a point source hold for every wave ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave|§A2.4]]). This section expresses the intensity of sound through the pressure amplitude of [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound|§A3.1]], introduces the logarithmic decibel scale that matches the enormous range of the ear, and collects the basic facts of human hearing.

## Intensity and pressure amplitude

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.1: Intensity of a Sinusoidal Sound Wave
> A sinusoidal sound wave of pressure amplitude $\Delta p_{\max}$, displacement amplitude $s_{\max}$ and angular frequency $\omega$, in a medium of density $\rho$ and sound speed $v$, has the intensity (time-averaged power per unit area, [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave|§A2.4]])
>
> $$
> I = \frac{(\Delta p_{\max})^2}{2\rho v} = \tfrac12\,\rho v\,\omega^2 s_{\max}^2, \qquad \text{with} \qquad \Delta p_{\max} = \rho v\omega\, s_{\max} .
> $$
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.3, eq. (17.11) and derivation*

^thm-a3-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Take the wave toward $+x$, $s = s_{\max}\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$. Across a plane at $x$, the medium on the left pushes the medium on the right with the pressure $p_0 + \Delta p$ while the plane moves with velocity $u = \partial s/\partial t$; the power delivered per unit area is $(p_0 + \Delta p)\,u$. The term $p_0 u$ averages to zero over a period (the layers only oscillate), so the energy flow comes from
>
> $$
> \Delta p\,u = \bigl[Bk s_{\max}\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)\bigr]\bigl[\omega s_{\max}\sin(kx - \omega t + \phi)\bigr] = Bk\omega\, s_{\max}^2 \sin^2(kx - \omega t + \phi) ,
> $$
>
> using [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]] for $\Delta p$. It is never negative: energy always flows along the direction of propagation. The average of $\sin^2$ over a period is $\tfrac12$ (integrate $\sin^2 = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\theta)$ over a period), so $I = \tfrac12 Bk\omega\, s_{\max}^2$. Now $B = \rho v^2$ ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]]) and $k = \omega/v$, so $Bk = \rho v\omega$: then $\Delta p_{\max} = Bk s_{\max} = \rho v\omega\, s_{\max}$ and
>
> $$
> I = \tfrac12\,\rho v\,\omega^2 s_{\max}^2 = \frac{(\rho v\omega\, s_{\max})^2}{2\rho v} = \frac{(\Delta p_{\max})^2}{2\rho v} .
> $$

^der-a3-2-1

*Uses:* [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-1|Theorem §A3.1.1]], [[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^thm-a3-1-2|Theorem §A3.1.2]], [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave|§A2.4]], [[§34 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus#^thm-34-1|451 Thm. §34.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Same structure as the string, and the role of ρv
> - The power of a sinusoidal wave on a string is $\tfrac12\mu v\omega^2A^2$ ([[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave|§A2.4]]). Sound in a column of cross-section $A$ carries the power $IA = \tfrac12(\rho A)v\omega^2 s_{\max}^2$: the mass per unit length $\rho A$ of the column takes the place of $\mu$. In both cases the energy grows as the square of the amplitude and of the frequency.
> - The combination $Z = \rho v$, the **specific acoustic impedance**, measures how much pressure a medium needs to move at a given speed: $\Delta p = Z u$ ([[§A3.1 Sound Waves and the Speed of Sound#^rem-a3-1-1|Remark]]). It is about $420\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot s/m}$ for air and $1.48 \times 10^6\ \mathrm{Pa\cdot s/m}$ for water. When the impedances on two sides of a boundary differ this much, almost all of the sound is reflected: a person shouting at someone under water is not heard. Impedance and reflection at boundaries are level B (planned).
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 9*

^rem-a3-2-1

> [!caution] Caution: Which density, which amplitude
> - $\rho$ and $v$ are those of the medium carrying the wave. The same pressure amplitude carries far less intensity in water than in air, since $\rho v$ is about 3500 times larger.
> - $\Delta p_{\max}$ is the amplitude of the pressure *fluctuation*, not the ambient pressure, and the formula is for a sinusoidal wave. For a non-sinusoidal sound, $(\Delta p_{\max})^2/2$ is replaced by the time average of $(\Delta p)^2$.
> - $u = \partial s/\partial t$ is the velocity of the medium, not the wave speed $v$: about $1.5\ \mathrm{mm/s}$ in the $87\ \mathrm{dB}$ sound of Example §A3.2.1.

^cau-a3-2-1

## The decibel scale

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.1: Sound Intensity Level
> The **sound intensity level** of a sound of intensity $I$ is
>
> $$
> \beta = (10\ \mathrm{dB})\,\log_{10}\frac{I}{I_0}, \qquad I_0 = 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{W/m^2} ,
> $$
>
> measured in **decibels** (dB). The reference $I_0$ is the threshold of hearing at $1.00\ \mathrm{kHz}$. $\beta$ is a ratio expressed on a logarithmic scale, so it is dimensionless: $I = I_0$ is $0\ \mathrm{dB}$, and the factor 10 makes the unit a tenth of a bel.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.3, eq. (17.12)*

^def-a3-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §A3.2.2: Level Differences
> Two sounds of intensities $I_1$ and $I_2$ differ in level by
>
> $$
> \beta_2 - \beta_1 = (10\ \mathrm{dB})\,\log_{10}\frac{I_2}{I_1} .
> $$
>
> Each factor of 10 in intensity adds $10\ \mathrm{dB}$; a factor of 2 adds $3.01\ \mathrm{dB}$, a factor of 5 adds $6.99\ \mathrm{dB}$. For a point source radiating uniformly, doubling the distance lowers the level by $6.02\ \mathrm{dB}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.3, Table 17.3, Example 17.3*

^thm-a3-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> $\beta_2 - \beta_1 = 10\,[\log_{10}(I_2/I_0) - \log_{10}(I_1/I_0)]\ \mathrm{dB} = 10\log_{10}(I_2/I_1)\ \mathrm{dB}$, since $\log a - \log b = \log(a/b)$; the reference cancels. Then $10\log_{10}10 = 10$, $10\log_{10}2 = 3.01$, $10\log_{10}5 = 6.99$. From a point source $I \propto 1/r^2$ (the inverse-square law, [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave|§A2.4]]), so going from $r$ to $2r$ divides $I$ by 4 and lowers the level by $10\log_{10}4\ \mathrm{dB} = 6.02\ \mathrm{dB}$.

^der-a3-2-2

*Uses:* [[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]], [[§A2.4 Energy and Power of a Wave|§A2.4]]

> [!caution] Caution: Decibels do not add
> Intensities add (for incoherent sources); levels do not. Two machines of $80\ \mathrm{dB}$ each make $83\ \mathrm{dB}$, not $160\ \mathrm{dB}$; ten of them make $90\ \mathrm{dB}$. Conversely, a sound $30\ \mathrm{dB}$ louder is $10^3$ times as intense. A second decibel scale, the **sound pressure level**, is built from the ratio of pressure amplitudes to a reference pressure and is common for sound in water; levels quoted on it are not comparable with the intensity levels used here.

^cau-a3-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: A ladder of levels
> | $\beta$ (dB) | $I$ ($\mathrm{W/m^2}$) | Example |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | 0 | $10^{-12}$ | threshold of hearing at 1000 Hz |
> | 10 | $10^{-11}$ | rustle of leaves |
> | 30 | $10^{-9}$ | quiet home |
> | 60 | $10^{-6}$ | normal conversation |
> | 80 | $10^{-4}$ | loud radio, classroom lecture |
> | 100 | $10^{-2}$ | noisy factory, siren at 30 m; damage from 8 h a day |
> | 120 | 1 | loud rock concert; threshold of pain |
> | 140 | $10^{2}$ | jet airplane at 30 m; damage in seconds |
> | 160 | $10^{4}$ | bursting of eardrums |
>
> Agencies recommend not exceeding $85\ \mathrm{dB}$ for 8 hours a day without hearing protection.
>
> *Source: UP1 Table 17.2*

^rem-a3-2-2

## Hearing

> [!law] Law §A3.2.3: Range of Human Hearing
> For normal human hearing:
> - audible frequencies run from about $20\ \mathrm{Hz}$ to $20\ \mathrm{kHz}$;
> - the threshold intensity at $1\ \mathrm{kHz}$ is about $I_0 = 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$, and pain sets in near $1\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$ ($120\ \mathrm{dB}$): the ear spans twelve orders of magnitude;
> - two frequencies are told apart if they differ by about 0.3% or more ($500.0$ and $501.5\ \mathrm{Hz}$ are noticeably different);
> - at a given frequency, level differences of about $1\ \mathrm{dB}$ can be discerned, and $3\ \mathrm{dB}$ is easily noticed.
>
> *Valid when:* healthy, typical listeners; the threshold depends strongly on frequency and is lowest near $2$–$5\ \mathrm{kHz}$, where the ear canal resonates; all values are approximate (Schwartz quotes $130\ \mathrm{dB}$ for the threshold of pain).
> *Source: UP1 §17.1, §17.3*

^law-a3-2-3

> [!definition] Definition §A3.2.2: Pitch, Loudness, Timbre; Phon
> **Pitch** is the perception of frequency, **loudness** the perception of intensity, and **timbre** (tone quality) the perception of the mix of frequencies and intensities that a source produces together, which distinguishes a violin from a piano playing the same note. Loudness is measured in **phons**: a sound has a loudness of $n$ phons if the average listener judges it as loud as a $1000\ \mathrm{Hz}$ sound of $n\ \mathrm{dB}$. Curves of equal loudness in the frequency–level plane are measured by having many listeners compare sounds; at $1000\ \mathrm{Hz}$ phons equal decibels.
>
> *Source: UP1 §17.3*

^def-a3-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: How small the threshold is
> At the threshold, $I_0 = 10^{-12}\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$ in air ($\rho = 1.20\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $v = 343\ \mathrm{m/s}$) means, by [[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]],
>
> $$
> \Delta p_{\max} = \sqrt{2\rho v I_0} = 2.9 \times 10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Pa} \approx 3 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm{atm}, \qquad s_{\max} = \frac{\Delta p_{\max}}{\rho v\omega} = 1.1 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm m \ \ (f = 1\ \mathrm{kHz}) ,
> $$
>
> a displacement far smaller than a molecule. An eardrum of about $1\ \mathrm{cm^2}$ then receives $10^{-16}\ \mathrm W$. At the threshold of pain, $1\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$, the pressure amplitude is still only $29\ \mathrm{Pa}$, three ten-thousandths of an atmosphere.

^rem-a3-2-3

## Examples

> [!example] Example §A3.2.1: Level from a Pressure Amplitude
> A sound wave in air at $0\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ ($\rho = 1.29\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$, $v = 331\ \mathrm{m/s}$) has pressure amplitude $0.656\ \mathrm{Pa}$. By [[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^thm-a3-2-1|Theorem §A3.2.1]] and [[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^def-a3-2-1|Def. §A3.2.1]],
>
> $$
> I = \frac{(0.656\ \mathrm{Pa})^2}{2(1.29\ \mathrm{kg/m^3})(331\ \mathrm{m/s})} = 5.04 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{W/m^2}, \qquad \beta = 10\log_{10}(5.04 \times 10^{8})\ \mathrm{dB} = 87.0\ \mathrm{dB} .
> $$
>
> A pressure swing of less than $10^{-5}\ \mathrm{atm}$ is already a loud sound, five times as intense as $80\ \mathrm{dB}$.
>
> *Source: UP1 Example 17.2*

^ex-a3-2-1

> [!example] Example §A3.2.2: How Loud Is a 50-W Speaker?
> A speaker draws $50\ \mathrm W$; treat it as a point source radiating uniformly, and stand $3.0\ \mathrm m$ away.
> - *If all the power became sound:* $I = P/(4\pi r^2) = 50\ \mathrm W/[4\pi(3.0\ \mathrm m)^2] = 0.44\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$, so $\beta = 10\log_{10}(4.4\times10^{11})\ \mathrm{dB} = 116\ \mathrm{dB}$, rock-concert loud.
> - *With a realistic efficiency* of about $10^{-5}$ (a typical speaker, poorly matched to the air), $I = 4.4 \times 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$ and $\beta = 116\ \mathrm{dB} - 50\ \mathrm{dB} = 66\ \mathrm{dB}$, by [[§A3.2 Sound Intensity#^thm-a3-2-2|Theorem §A3.2.2]]. The pressure amplitude is $\sqrt{2\rho v I} = 0.06\ \mathrm{Pa}$.
> - *At $6.0\ \mathrm m$:* $6\ \mathrm{dB}$ less, $60\ \mathrm{dB}$, normal conversation.
>
> A factor $10^{-5}$ in power is just $50\ \mathrm{dB}$ on the logarithmic scale.
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 10*

^ex-a3-2-2
