*Sources: Series Part I (the user's notes), §10 · Schwartz, Physics 15c lectures, Lecture 5.*

A string fixed at both ends has the harmonic series of normal modes ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-2|Theorem §A2.6.2]], [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]]), and its motion is a Fourier sine series ([[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]]). This section asks what changes when the wave equation of [[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions|§B7.1]] is confined to a rectangle or a box. The methods are the same: a product ansatz, the boundary condition, a projection. They act on richer geometry, and three things appear that a string cannot show. The overtones are not integer multiples of the fundamental. Modes are silent along nodal *lines*. Different modes can share a frequency (degeneracy), forced by the symmetry of the boundary or by accident of arithmetic. The circular drum is described qualitatively; its radial functions need Bessel functions.

## Normal modes of a rectangular membrane

> [!definition] Definition §B7.2.1: Normal Mode of a Membrane; Nodal Line; Degeneracy
> A **normal mode** of a membrane ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^mod-b7-1-3|Model §B7.1.3]]) is a motion $u = \psi(x, y)\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$ in which every point oscillates in phase or in antiphase at one frequency $\omega$, with a fixed shape $\psi$ that satisfies the boundary condition. Its **nodal lines** are the curves inside the membrane where $\psi = 0$: they never move. A frequency is **degenerate** if two or more modes with linearly independent shapes have it; the number of independent shapes is its **degeneracy**.
>
> *Source: Series I §10*

^def-b7-2-1

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.1: Modes of a Clamped Rectangular Membrane
> A membrane on $0 \le x \le L_x$, $0 \le y \le L_y$, clamped along its edge, has as its separable solutions $u = X(x)Y(y)\Theta(t)$ exactly the modes
>
> $$
> u_{nm} = \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L_x}\,\sin\frac{m\pi y}{L_y}\,\bigl[A\cos\omega_{nm}t + B\sin\omega_{nm}t\bigr], \qquad \omega_{nm} = c\pi\sqrt{\frac{n^2}{L_x^2} + \frac{m^2}{L_y^2}}, \qquad n, m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
> $$
>
> The frequency is $c$ times the length of the wave vector $(k_x, k_y) = (n\pi/L_x, m\pi/L_y)$. The nodal lines of $u_{nm}$ are the straight lines $x = jL_x/n$ ($j = 1, \ldots, n - 1$) and $y = jL_y/m$ ($j = 1, \ldots, m - 1$), which divide the membrane into $nm$ cells moving alternately up and down.
>
> *Source: Series I §10 (the modes and frequencies, "verify by direct substitution")*

^thm-b7-2-1

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Substitute $u = XY\Theta$ into $u_{tt} = c^2(u_{xx} + u_{yy})$ and divide by $c^2XY\Theta$:
>
> $$
> \frac{\Theta''}{c^2\Theta} = \frac{X''}{X} + \frac{Y''}{Y} .
> $$
>
> Each term depends on a different variable, so each is constant: $X''/X = -\kappa_x$, $Y''/Y = -\kappa_y$, and $\Theta''/(c^2\Theta) = -(\kappa_x + \kappa_y)$. The clamped edges give $X(0) = X(L_x) = 0$ and $Y(0) = Y(L_y) = 0$ (otherwise $u$ would vanish identically). These are the one-dimensional problems of [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]], with the solutions $X = \sin(n\pi x/L_x)$, $\kappa_x = (n\pi/L_x)^2$, and likewise in $y$. Then $\Theta$ is simple harmonic with $\omega^2 = c^2(\kappa_x + \kappa_y)$. The shape vanishes inside exactly where one of the sines does.

^der-b7-2-1

*Uses:* [[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^mod-b7-1-3|Model §B7.1.3]], [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]]

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.2: General Motion of a Rectangular Membrane
> Every motion of the clamped rectangular membrane with smooth initial shape $f(x, y)$ and velocity $g(x, y)$, both zero on the edge, is a superposition of its modes,
>
> $$
> u = \sum_{n, m \ge 1}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L_x}\sin\frac{m\pi y}{L_y}\bigl[A_{nm}\cos\omega_{nm}t + B_{nm}\sin\omega_{nm}t\bigr],
> $$
>
> $$
> A_{nm} = \frac{4}{L_xL_y}\int_0^{L_x}\!\!\int_0^{L_y} f\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L_x}\sin\frac{m\pi y}{L_y}\,dy\,dx, \qquad B_{nm} = \frac{4}{L_xL_y\,\omega_{nm}}\int_0^{L_x}\!\!\int_0^{L_y} g\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L_x}\sin\frac{m\pi y}{L_y}\,dy\,dx .
> $$
>
> The modes are orthogonal over the rectangle, the initial data are expanded in them by projection, and each mode then oscillates independently: Fourier's idea in two variables.
>
> *Source: Series I §10 ("initial data is expanded in them by projection")*

^thm-b7-2-2

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> *Orthogonality.* By Fubini's theorem the double integral of a product of mode shapes factorises,
>
> $$
> \iint\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L_x}\sin\frac{m\pi y}{L_y}\sin\frac{n'\pi x}{L_x}\sin\frac{m'\pi y}{L_y}\,dx\,dy = \frac{L_x}{2}\delta_{nn'}\cdot\frac{L_y}{2}\delta_{mm'},
> $$
>
> using the one-dimensional orthogonality of [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-7|Theorem §B4.1.7]] twice. Projecting $u(x, y, 0) = f$ and $u_t(x, y, 0) = g$ onto a mode gives the coefficients, as in [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]].
>
> *Completeness (sketch).* For fixed $y$, expand $f(\cdot, y)$ in its sine series in $x$; each coefficient is a function of $y$ vanishing at $y = 0, L_y$, and expanding it in its sine series in $y$ gives the double series. For smooth data vanishing on the edge both expansions converge uniformly ([[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-7|Theorem §B4.1.7]]); the details of the double limit are not given here (Mathematical Methods, planned). Each term solves the wave equation with the boundary condition ([[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]]), and the equation is linear.

^der-b7-2-2

*Uses:* [[§B4.1 Fourier Series#^thm-b4-1-7|Theorem §B4.1.7]], [[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]], [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-3|Theorem §B4.2.3]], [[§23 Fubini's Theorem#^thm-23-1|452 Thm. §23.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: A mode is four plane waves
> By the product-to-sum identities,
>
> $$
> \sin k_xx\,\sin k_yy\,\cos\omega t = \tfrac14\bigl[\cos(\omega t - k_xx + k_yy) + \cos(\omega t + k_xx - k_yy) - \cos(\omega t - k_xx - k_yy) - \cos(\omega t + k_xx + k_yy)\bigr],
> $$
>
> four plane waves ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-b7-1-2|Def. §B7.1.2]]) with wave vectors $(\pm k_x, \pm k_y)$, all of length $\omega/c$ ([[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^thm-b7-1-4|Theorem §B7.1.4]]). A mode is a plane wave bouncing between the edges, reflected (inverted) at each one, and the boundary condition picks the directions for which the four reflected copies close up into a standing pattern. This is the two-dimensional version of a standing wave as two opposite travelling waves ([[§A2.6 Standing Waves and Resonance#^thm-a2-6-1|Theorem §A2.6.1]]).

## The square membrane: inharmonic overtones and degeneracy

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.3: Spectrum and Degeneracy of the Square Membrane
> For a clamped square membrane of side $L$, $\omega_{nm} = \omega_{11}\sqrt{(n^2 + m^2)/2}$ with $\omega_{11} = \sqrt2\,\pi c/L$.
> 1. **Inharmonic overtones.** The frequency ratios $\omega_{nm}/\omega_{11}$ are $1, 1.581, 2, 2.236, 2.550, 2.915, 3, 3.162, \ldots$: in general not integers. (Only the diagonal modes have $\omega_{nn} = n\,\omega_{11}$.)
> 2. **Symmetry degeneracy.** For $n \ne m$ the modes $(n, m)$ and $(m, n)$ have the same frequency. This is forced by the symmetry of the square: the reflection $x \leftrightarrow y$ maps solutions to solutions and exchanges the two modes.
> 3. **Accidental degeneracy.** When $n^2 + m^2$ can be written as a sum of two squares in more than one way, further modes share the frequency: $50 = 1^2 + 7^2 = 5^2 + 5^2$ makes $(1, 7)$, $(7, 1)$ and $(5, 5)$ degenerate; $65 = 1^2 + 8^2 = 4^2 + 7^2$ makes four modes degenerate.
> 4. **New nodal patterns.** Any combination of degenerate modes is again a normal mode at the same frequency, with nodal lines of its own. On the unit square ($L = 1$): $u_{12} + u_{21}$ has the nodal line $x + y = 1$ and $u_{12} - u_{21}$ the line $x = y$; $u_{13} - u_{31}$ has both diagonals; and $u_{13} + u_{31}$ has the closed nodal curve $\sin^2\pi x + \sin^2\pi y = \tfrac32$ around the centre.
>
> *Source: Series I §10 (inharmonicity, nodal lines, degeneracy forced by symmetry, frequency ratios $1 : \sqrt{5/2} : \sqrt{5/2} : 2$); parts 3–4 added here*

^thm-b7-2-3

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> (1)–(3) follow from $\omega_{nm} \propto \sqrt{n^2 + m^2}$: the smallest values of $n^2 + m^2$ are $2, 5, 8, 10, 13, 17, 18, 20$, and $\sqrt{(n^2 + m^2)/2}$ is an integer only when $n^2 + m^2 = 2j^2$. *Symmetry:* if $u(x, y, t)$ solves the wave equation on the square with $u = 0$ on the edge, so does $v(x, y, t) = u(y, x, t)$, because $\nabla^2$ is symmetric in $x$ and $y$ and the square is mapped onto itself. The map sends $u_{nm}$ to $u_{mn}$, which therefore oscillates at the same frequency.
>
> (4) The equation is linear, so a combination of solutions oscillating at one frequency is a solution oscillating at that frequency. With $\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$,
>
> $$
> u_{12} \pm u_{21} \propto \sin\pi x\sin 2\pi y \pm \sin 2\pi x\sin\pi y = 2\sin\pi x\,\sin\pi y\,\bigl(\cos\pi y \pm \cos\pi x\bigr),
> $$
>
> which vanishes inside the square exactly where $\cos\pi x = \mp\cos\pi y$, that is $x + y = 1$ (upper sign) or $x = y$ (lower sign). With $\sin 3\theta = \sin\theta\,(3 - 4\sin^2\theta)$,
>
> $$
> u_{13} \pm u_{31} \propto \sin\pi x\,\sin\pi y\,\bigl[(3 - 4\sin^2\pi y) \pm (3 - 4\sin^2\pi x)\bigr],
> $$
>
> which gives $\sin^2\pi x + \sin^2\pi y = \tfrac32$ for the sum and $\sin^2\pi x = \sin^2\pi y$, the two diagonals, for the difference.

> [!remark] Remark: What one hears and sees
> - **Drums thud, strings sing.** A plucked string sounds a pitch because its overtones are harmonics and the sound is periodic ([[§B4.3 Harmonics, Timbre and Musical Scales#^rem-b4-3-1|Remark: Reading spectra]]). A struck membrane excites many modes whose frequencies are not in integer ratios, so the sound is not periodic and has no clear pitch.
> - **Nodal patterns.** Sand sprinkled on a vibrating surface collects along the nodal lines, which become visible: Chladni's figures. (Chladni used stiff plates, whose restoring force is bending rather than tension; their patterns are of the same kind but their frequencies follow a different equation.)
> - **Symmetry and degeneracy.** A symmetry of the boundary maps modes to modes of the same frequency, so the more symmetric the shape, the more degeneracy; a rectangle with $(L_x/L_y)^2$ irrational has none (for $L_x/L_y = \sqrt2$, $(6, 3)$ and $(2, 5)$ still coincide). This link between symmetry and degeneracy is central in quantum mechanics: the stationary states of a particle in a square box have exactly these shapes and the same degeneracies.
>
> *Source: Series I §10*

^rem-b7-2-2

> [!remark] Remark: The circular drum
> For a membrane clamped on a circle of radius $a$, separate in polar coordinates, $u = R(r)\,\Phi(\theta)\cos\omega t$. The angular factor must be periodic: $\Phi = \cos m\theta$ or $\sin m\theta$ with $m = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ The radial factor then satisfies Bessel's equation, $\rho^2R'' + \rho R' + (\rho^2 - m^2)R = 0$ in the variable $\rho = \omega r/c$, whose solution regular at the centre is the Bessel function $J_m(\omega r/c)$. The clamped edge requires $J_m(\omega a/c) = 0$, so the frequencies are $c/a$ times the zeros of the Bessel functions. Qualitatively:
> - the modes are labelled by $m$, the number of **nodal diameters**, and by the number of **nodal circles**;
> - the frequencies are not integer multiples of the fundamental, as for the square;
> - every mode with $m \ge 1$ is doubly degenerate ($\cos m\theta$ and $\sin m\theta$), because a rotation of the circle maps one into a combination of the two.
>
> The zeros of $J_m$ and the orthogonality of the Bessel functions (with the weight $r$ in the integral) are not derived here: Mathematical Methods, planned. The first zero of $J_1$ reappears in the resolution limit of a circular aperture ([[§A7.4 Circular Apertures and Resolution]]), where the Airy pattern is computed: [[§B11.2 Fraunhofer Diffraction Patterns#^thm-b11-2-3|Theorem §B11.2.3]].
>
> *Source: Schwartz 15c, Lecture 5, §6 (Bessel's equation; Bessel functions come up in two-dimensional problems)*

## Cavities

> [!theorem] Theorem §B7.2.4: Modes of a Rectangular Cavity
> Let the three-dimensional wave equation hold in the box $0 \le x \le L_x$, $0 \le y \le L_y$, $0 \le z \le L_z$.
> 1. If $u = 0$ on the walls, the modes are
>    $$
>    u = \sin\frac{n\pi x}{L_x}\sin\frac{m\pi y}{L_y}\sin\frac{l\pi z}{L_z}\cos(\omega t + \varphi), \qquad n, m, l \ge 1 .
>    $$
> 2. For sound in a room with rigid walls, a wall is a displacement node and a pressure antinode ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^mod-a3-3-1|Model §A3.3.1]]), so the normal derivative of the pressure fluctuation vanishes there, and the modes are
>    $$
>    \Delta p = \cos\frac{n\pi x}{L_x}\cos\frac{m\pi y}{L_y}\cos\frac{l\pi z}{L_z}\cos(\omega t + \varphi), \qquad n, m, l \ge 0,\ \text{not all zero} .
>    $$
>
> In both cases
>
> $$
> \omega_{nml} = c\pi\sqrt{\frac{n^2}{L_x^2} + \frac{m^2}{L_y^2} + \frac{l^2}{L_z^2}} .
> $$
>
> *Source: added here; Series I §10 notes that the same equation in three dimensions governs sound in air*

^thm-b7-2-4

> [!derivation]+ Derivation
> Separate $u = X(x)Y(y)Z(z)\Theta(t)$ as in [[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]], now with three constants. Each factor solves a one-dimensional problem: with $X = 0$ at both ends it is $\sin(n\pi x/L_x)$, $n \ge 1$ ([[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]]); with $X' = 0$ at both ends it is $\cos(n\pi x/L_x)$ with $n \ge 0$: the pressure in a pipe closed at both ends, the same functions and frequencies $nc/2L$ as the displacement in a pipe open at both ends ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]). The constant $n = 0$ is allowed in each direction separately, since the other factors can still oscillate; only $n = m = l = 0$, a uniform static pressure, is not a wave. The frequency is $c$ times the length of $(n\pi/L_x, m\pi/L_y, l\pi/L_z)$.

^der-b7-2-4

*Uses:* [[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^thm-b7-2-1|Theorem §B7.2.1]], [[§B4.2 Normal Modes of a String and the Plucked String#^thm-b4-2-1|Theorem §B4.2.1]], [[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]], [[§B7.1 The Wave Equation in Two and Three Dimensions#^def-b7-1-1|Def. §B7.1.1]]

> [!remark] Remark: Counting modes
> The allowed wave vectors $(n\pi/L_x, m\pi/L_y, l\pi/L_z)$ form a grid in $k$-space with one point per cell of volume $\pi^3/V$, $V = L_xL_yL_z$. The modes with $\omega < \Omega$ are the grid points in one octant of the sphere $\lvert\vec k\rvert < \Omega/c$, so for large $\Omega$
>
> $$
> N(\Omega) \approx \frac18\cdot\frac43\pi\Bigl(\frac\Omega c\Bigr)^3\cdot\frac{V}{\pi^3} = \frac{V\,\Omega^3}{6\pi^2c^3}, \qquad \frac{dN}{d\Omega} = \frac{V\,\Omega^2}{2\pi^2c^3} .
> $$
>
> The same count in two dimensions gives $N \approx A\Omega^2/4\pi c^2$, and in one dimension $N = L\Omega/\pi c$. A string's modes are evenly spaced; a membrane's crowd together linearly in $\omega$, a cavity's quadratically. The formula is the leading term: grid points on the boundary planes of the octant (zero indices in the rigid-wall case, missing ones in the clamped case) correct it at lower order in $\Omega$. For light, with two polarizations per wave vector, this count is the one behind the Rayleigh–Jeans law ([[§A1.1 Blackbody Radiation#^der-a1-1-1|QM derivation of Theorem §A1.1.1]]).
>
> *Source: added here; the count is that of the QM derivation*

## Examples

> [!example] Example §B7.2.1: The Lowest Tones of a Square Drum
> By [[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^thm-b7-2-3|Theorem §B7.2.3]], the lowest frequencies of a clamped square membrane, their modes and their degeneracies are:
>
> | $n^2 + m^2$ | $2$ | $5$ | $8$ | $10$ | $13$ | $17$ | $18$ | $20$ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $\omega/\omega_{11}$ | $1$ | $1.581$ | $2$ | $2.236$ | $2.550$ | $2.915$ | $3$ | $3.162$ |
> | modes | $(1,1)$ | $(1,2), (2,1)$ | $(2,2)$ | $(1,3), (3,1)$ | $(2,3), (3,2)$ | $(1,4), (4,1)$ | $(3,3)$ | $(2,4), (4,2)$ |
>
> A string's first eight modes would be at $1, 2, 3, \ldots, 8$ times its fundamental; the square drum crowds thirteen modes into the range up to $3.2\,\omega_{11}$, at irregular spacings. A drum struck at its centre excites only modes with odd $n$ and odd $m$ (the others have a nodal line through the centre): $(1,1)$, $(1,3)$ with $(3,1)$, $(3,3)$, …; a strike off-centre excites the rest, in proportions set by the double Fourier coefficients of [[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^thm-b7-2-2|Theorem §B7.2.2]].
>
> *Source: frequency ratios from Series I §10; the table is computed here*

^ex-b7-2-1

> [!example] Example §B7.2.2: Room Modes
> A rectangular room $5\ \mathrm m \times 4\ \mathrm m \times 3\ \mathrm m$ holds air with $c = 343\ \mathrm{m/s}$. By [[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^thm-b7-2-4|Theorem §B7.2.4]] (rigid walls), $f_{nml} = \frac c2\sqrt{(n/5\ \mathrm m)^2 + (m/4\ \mathrm m)^2 + (l/3\ \mathrm m)^2}$. The lowest are
>
> $$
> f_{100} = 34.3\ \mathrm{Hz}, \quad f_{010} = 42.9\ \mathrm{Hz}, \quad f_{110} = 54.9\ \mathrm{Hz}, \quad f_{001} = 57.2\ \mathrm{Hz}, \quad f_{101} = 66.7\ \mathrm{Hz}, \quad f_{200} = 68.6\ \mathrm{Hz}, \ \ldots
> $$
>
> The first ones are the one-dimensional modes of a pipe closed at both ends, of length $5$, $4$ and $3\ \mathrm m$ ([[§A3.3 Standing Sound Waves and Musical Sound#^thm-a3-3-2|Theorem §A3.3.2]]). Below about $100\ \mathrm{Hz}$ the room has only $13$ modes, well separated, so low notes are boosted or weakened depending on the frequency and on where one stands. By $1\ \mathrm{kHz}$ the count is about $6900$ (the leading estimate of [[§B7.2 Modes of Membranes and Cavities#^rem-b7-2-4|Remark: Counting modes]] gives $V\Omega^3/6\pi^2c^3 = 6200$; the modes with a zero index make up most of the difference), and the response is smooth.
>
> *Source: added here*
